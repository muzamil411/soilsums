# Notes for a future page: renovating existing turf

Kept from `when-to-overseed-a-lawn.mdx`, retired 28 September 2026. Nothing here
is published yet, and nothing here is sourced — treat every claim below as a
starting point to verify, not as copy.

## Why the draft was retired

Its three substantial sections — timing by grass type, how much seed, and
watering — are now covered with sources by
`/blog/how-long-does-grass-seed-take-to-grow/`, which carries the Missouri and
Iowa State seeding windows, both publications' seeding rates, and Iowa State's
watering guidance. Its keyword was under 100 searches a month with no calculated
KD, against about 25,600 for the germination cluster, so publishing it would have
put the weaker page in competition with the stronger one.

## What only a renovation page could own

Overseeding existing turf is a different job from sowing bare ground, and these
are the parts the germination article deliberately does not cover. They are the
reason a renovation page could still be worth building.

**Surface preparation is the step that decides the outcome.** Grass seed needs
contact with soil rather than with thatch, and a dense established lawn is very
good at preventing that. Seed lying on top dries out, is eaten, or germinates and
dies before its root reaches anything. That makes raking or scarifying before
overseeding not a refinement but the thing the job turns on — which is a
different emphasis from a new lawn, where the soil is already exposed.

**Feeding at the same time cuts both ways.** A starter fertilizer helps because
new seedlings want phosphorus for root development. A high-nitrogen lawn feed
works against you, because it pushes the _established_ grass into growth and that
shades out the seedlings just sown. Use a starter formulation or nothing, and
feed properly some weeks later.

**Aeration** was in the draft and is the one part with no figures at all behind
it here. Timing is the open question and is why this page is on hold: Muzamil has
aeration-timing sources to come.

**Penn State's renovation rate is already in the data.** `data/turfgrass.ts`
holds `RENOVATION_RYEGRASS` at 2 to 5 lb per 1,000 sq ft for turf-type perennial
ryegrass seeded into existing turf, which is a much lower rate than the 4 to 5
Penn State gives for a new lawn. That distinction is a renovation page's natural
spine, and it is the only sourced renovation figure on the site.

## Before building it

Check cannibalisation against the germination article, which now owns the
seeding windows and the rates. A renovation page has to be about _preparing and
managing existing turf_ rather than about when to sow, or the two will compete
the way this draft would have.

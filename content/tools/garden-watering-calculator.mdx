  - question: >-
      Is it better to water deeply or often?
    answer: >-
      Deeply and less often, for almost everything in the ground. Deep watering draws roots down
      to where the soil stays damp longer, which makes the plant far more resilient in a dry
      spell. Frequent light watering keeps roots shallow and dependent. Containers are the
      exception: they hold so little water that daily attention in summer is normal.
  - question: >-
      What time of day should I water?
    answer: >-
      Early morning is best. The soil takes the water up before the heat of the day, and the
      foliage dries quickly, which matters for fungal disease. Evening is second best and fine
      if morning is impossible, though leaves staying wet all night raises the risk of mildew
      and blight. Midday is the least efficient, though watering a wilting plant at noon is
      still better than not watering it.
  - question: >-
      How do I measure rainfall?
    answer: >-
      A straight-sided container in the open is all you need: a jam jar, a tin, anything that
      does not taper. Put it away from walls and overhanging branches, and measure the depth of
      water in it with a ruler. A purpose-made gauge is more precise but the jar is honest
      enough for this purpose.
  - question: >-
      Does mulch reduce how much I need to water?
    answer: >-
      Substantially, by slowing evaporation from the soil surface. Two to three inches of mulch
      is one of the most effective water-saving measures available to a gardener, and it also
      keeps soil temperature steadier. It does mean checking under the mulch rather than on top
      of it when deciding whether a bed is dry.

Watering advice is usually given in inches of water per week, and hoses deliver gallons.
This calculator converts between them and subtracts the rain you have already had, so
what comes out is the number of gallons or liters still to give.

## How to use it

1. **Enter the garden area.** Just the planted beds, not the paths between them.
2. **Set the weekly target.** One inch, or 25 millimetres, suits most established
   vegetables. Sandy soil, hot weather and windy sites push it up; heavy soil and cool
   weather pull it down.
3. **Enter the rain so far this week.** A straight-sided jar left in the open is a
   perfectly serviceable gauge.

If the rain has already met the target the calculator says so rather than producing a
number — but check the soil a few inches down before you believe it, because a heavy
shower on dry ground can run off rather than soak in.

## How it is calculated

In imperial:

```
gallons = area (sq ft) x net inches x 0.6234
```

An inch of water over one square foot is 144 cubic inches, and a US gallon is 231 cubic
inches by definition. 144 / 231 = 0.6234, which is where the familiar 0.623 comes from.

In metric it is simpler, because the units were designed to make this easy:

```
liters = area (m2) x net millimetres
```

One millimetre over one square metre is exactly one liter.

The "net" figure is the target minus the rainfall, and never goes below zero — rain in
excess of the target does not create a negative requirement.

### A worked example

A 100 square foot bed, one inch a week target, 0.4 inches of rain so far:

- Net requirement: 1 − 0.4 = **0.6 inches**
- Water needed: 100 x 0.6 x 0.6234 = **37.4 gallons**
- Split over two soakings: **18.7 gallons each**

The same bed with no rain at all needs 62.3 gallons for the week.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Find out what your hose or can actually delivers.** Time how long a watering can takes
to fill, then how many cans go on a bed. Most people substantially overestimate how much
water a few minutes with a hose puts down.

**Two long drinks beat seven short ones.** Deep watering pulls roots downward, where the
soil stays damp longer. Light daily sprinkling keeps them near the surface, where the
first hot day finds them.

**Check the soil, not the surface.** Push a finger in two or three inches. Dry dust on
top over damp soil beneath is normal and needs no water; damp crust over dry soil is a
sign of light watering.

**Mulch before you water more.** Two or three inches of mulch cuts evaporation from the
soil surface noticeably, and does it every day without your involvement.

**Water the soil, not the leaves.** A watering can with the rose removed, a soaker hose
or drip line all put water where roots are and keep foliage dry, which matters for
tomatoes and squash in particular.

**Containers are a different problem.** They hold so little that a large pot in high
summer can need water daily whatever a weekly figure says. Use the container calculator
to see how little soil a pot actually holds.

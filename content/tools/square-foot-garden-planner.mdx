Square foot gardening trades rows for a grid, and the arithmetic changes with it. Rather
than working out how many feet of row a crop needs, you decide how many one-foot squares
to give it, and the crop's density tells you how many plants that is.

This planner does that bookkeeping. Set a grid size, pick a crop, tap squares to plant
them, and the totals build underneath.

## How to use it

1. **Set the rows and columns.** A four by four grid is the classic starting bed. Four
   by eight matches a standard timber-framed raised bed.
2. **Choose a crop to place.** The dropdown shows each crop's density — how many go in a
   square, or how many squares one plant needs.
3. **Tap squares to plant them.** Tapping a square that already holds the selected crop
   clears it again.
4. **Print the plan** with the button at the bottom. Grid lines and labels print
   legibly; the site furniture does not.

The plan is saved in your browser as you work, and also written into the page address,
so you can send the link to yourself to open on another device.

## How it is calculated

There is no clever geometry here — it is bookkeeping, applied carefully:

```
plants = squares assigned x plants per square foot
```

The care is in what happens when that number is less than one. Crops such as zucchini,
winter squash and pumpkin have a density below 1, meaning they need several squares per
plant rather than several plants per square:

```
squares per plant = ceil(1 / plants per square foot)
```

For those crops the planner rounds down, and says so. Assign a zucchini one square and
it reports zero plants with a note explaining it needs about ten. Assign it ten and it
reports one. That is more useful than a cheerful "0.1 plants".

### A worked example

A four by four grid, sixteen squares. Two squares of carrots, one of lettuce, one of
tomato:

- Carrots: 2 squares x 16 per square = **32 plants**
- Lettuce: 1 square x 4 = **4 plants**
- Tomato: 1 square x 1 = **1 plant**
- Total: **37 plants** in four squares, with twelve squares still free

Replace the tomato square with zucchini and the planner will tell you that one square is
not enough: at 0.11 plants per square, a zucchini wants around ten.

Densities that come out as "1 per N squares" are the reciprocal of the published
plants-per-square-foot figure, rounded up to whole squares. Where a crop has no figure,
the Cornell CALS page does not name it — the planner falls back to its in-row spacing and
says so, rather than inventing a square count.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Put the sprawlers on the edge.** A zucchini or a squash given its ten squares will
still spill over the frame. Planting it at the edge of the bed, facing out, saves the
plants next to it.

**Trellis the climbers.** Cucumbers, pole beans and peas cost almost no floor space when
they go up rather than out, which is what makes them good value in a small grid.

**Do not plan every square for the whole season at once.** Fast crops come and go two or
three times while a tomato occupies its square from May to October. Plan the early
round, then replan as squares empty.

**Watch the shade.** In the northern hemisphere, anything tall belongs on the north side
of the bed, or it will put the shorter crops in shadow from midsummer onwards.

**Check the depth your crops want.** A grid says nothing about depth, and carrots in a
six inch bed will fork and stunt. Deep-rooted crops want twelve inches of loose soil.

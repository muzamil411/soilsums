  - question: >-
      Why does the calculator not just use my ZIP code?
    answer: >-
      Because a ZIP code is not a climate. Frost dates vary sharply over short distances with
      elevation, slope, shelter and how much concrete is nearby, and a lookup table would give a
      confident answer that is wrong for a garden at the bottom of a cold valley. Asking you for
      the one local fact that matters is more honest and, for the gardener who knows their own
      site, more accurate.
  - question: >-
      Is it safe to plant on the last frost date itself?
    answer: >-
      No. An average last frost date is a midpoint, which means roughly half of years have a
      frost after it. For anything frost-tender — tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash — wait one to
      two weeks past it, and keep an eye on the forecast. Hardy crops such as peas, spinach and
      kale can go out well before it.
  - question: >-
      Why does garlic not get a date?
    answer: >-
      Because no last-frost offset describes it. Garlic goes in during autumn, a few weeks
      before the ground freezes, and is harvested the following summer. Rather than invent a
      spring date for it, the calculator shows the crop's own timing note and tells you to work
      back from your first fall frost instead. Strawberries are similar, being planted as crowns
      rather than sown.
  - question: >-
      What does the harvest window mean?
    answer: >-
      It is the crop's days-to-maturity range applied to whichever planting step comes last —
      direct sowing, or transplanting for crops that are set out as seedlings. It is an
      estimate, and a cool season will stretch it. If you have entered a first fall frost date,
      the calculator flags anything that would not finish in time.
  - question: >-
      Can I plant more than once in a season?
    answer: >-
      Yes, and for fast crops you should. Radishes, lettuce, spinach, bush beans and carrots can
      be sown every two to three weeks through the season for a continuous supply rather than
      one glut. Use the dates here for the first sowing and count forward from there.

Almost every planting date in a seed catalogue is expressed relative to your last
spring frost: "start indoors six weeks before last frost", "direct sow two weeks
after". This calculator does that arithmetic for thirty crops at once, from the one
date only you can supply.

There is no ZIP code lookup here, deliberately. Frost dates vary too much over short
distances for a postcode to be a good answer, and the second question below explains
why asking you is the more honest approach.

## How to use it

1. **Enter your average last spring frost date.** If you do not know it, the first
   question below lists where to find it for your country.
2. **Add your average first fall frost date** if you know it. It is optional, and worth
   entering: it lets the calculator flag anything that will not ripen before frost, and
   tells you how long your season actually is.
3. **Pick your crops.** Tap as many as you like; the calendar builds as you go.

Each row gives a date to start indoors, to transplant out, and to direct sow, plus a
likely harvest window. Blanks are meaningful — a blank direct-sow date means the crop is
not normally sown straight into the ground, not that the data is missing.

## How it is calculated

Every crop carries three offsets in weeks, relative to the last frost date, and a
days-to-maturity range:

```
start indoors = last frost - (weeks before last frost x 7)
transplant    = last frost + (weeks after last frost x 7)
direct sow    = last frost + (weeks relative to last frost x 7)
harvest       = last planting step + days to maturity
```

Negative offsets mean before the frost date, which is why cool-season crops such as
lettuce and kale show transplant dates earlier than the frost date itself — they take a
light frost without harm.

All of this is computed in UTC and returned as plain calendar dates, so a shared result
cannot shift by a day depending on the reader's timezone.

### A worked example

A last frost date of 15 May, growing tomatoes:

- Start indoors: six weeks before, so 42 days back from 15 May = **3 April**
- Transplant out: one week after, so **22 May**
- Direct sow: **not applicable** — tomatoes are not sown outdoors in a temperate season
- Harvest: 60 to 85 days from transplanting = **21 July to 15 August**

Radishes from the same frost date are direct sown three weeks early on **24 April** and
ready in 22 to 30 days, which is **16 to 24 May**.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Treat the last frost date as a coin toss, not a deadline.** Half of years are later
than the average. Anything tender wants a week or two of margin and a look at the
forecast.

**Soil temperature matters more than air temperature for sowing.** Beans, corn and
squash rot in cold wet soil rather than germinating. A cheap soil thermometer settles
the question better than a calendar does.

**Count back from the fall frost for autumn crops.** For a late sowing, the useful
arithmetic is days to maturity plus a couple of weeks of margin, subtracted from your
first fall frost date.

**Do not start seeds indoors too early.** A tomato sown ten weeks before it can go out
becomes a leggy, root-bound plant that never quite recovers. The offsets here are
deliberately not generous.

**Harden off before transplanting.** A week of increasing time outdoors before planting
out saves plants that would otherwise stall or scorch.

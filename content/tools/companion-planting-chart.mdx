Pick a crop above and you get two lists: what to plant near it, and what to keep away. Every entry
carries a reason and a label saying how well that reason is actually supported.

The label is the point. Most companion planting charts are a grid of names with no indication that
some pairings rest on studied mechanisms and most rest on nothing at all.

## What the three labels mean

**Supported** — a mechanism that has been studied and holds up. There are only a few, and they are
worth planning a bed around:

- **Feeding beneficial insects.** Small, shallow, open flowers — the umbels of
  [dill](/crops/dill/), [cilantro](/crops/cilantro/) and [parsley](/crops/parsley/), and the
  flowers of [oregano](/crops/oregano/) and [thyme](/crops/thyme/) — are accessible to hoverflies,
  parasitic wasps and lacewings, whose larvae eat aphids and caterpillars. The catch, which almost
  every chart omits: **it only works from a plant in flower.** A patch clipped for the kitchen all
  season feeds nobody.
- **Nematode suppression by marigolds.** Real, and specific: it needs a dense stand grown across
  the ground for a season and then dug in, effectively as a cover crop. A few
  [marigolds](/crops/marigold/) dotted between tomatoes will not do it.
- **Shared pests within a family.** Two plants in the same botanical family host the same pests and
  diseases. This is the strongest reason to keep two crops apart, and it is why rotation is
  organised by family rather than by crop.
- **Incompatible watering.** Not a pest question at all. A woody Mediterranean herb wants poor,
  sharply drained soil kept dry; a leafy or fruiting vegetable wants steady moisture and feeding.
  Whichever regime you follow, one of them suffers. This is the most reliably useful thing on the
  chart and almost nobody publishes it.

**Plausible** — a mechanism that makes sense and has little or no testing behind it. Scent masking
is the main one: aromatic herbs near brassicas, said to confuse cabbage moth. It costs nothing to
try. Nitrogen fixing sits here too, for a reason worth knowing — [beans](/crops/bean/) and
[peas](/crops/pea/) do fix their own nitrogen, but they keep most of it until the plant is dug in,
so the crop that _follows_ benefits more than the neighbour standing beside it.

**Tradition only** — repeated in garden lore with no mechanism worth citing. About two thirds of
all the pairings on this chart fall here.

## Why we show the weak pairings instead of dropping them

A chart of only the well-supported pairings would be short, honest and useless, because a reader
who has met a claim elsewhere and cannot find it here will assume we missed it rather than that we
checked. So the traditional claims stay, labelled.

This is the same treatment the marigold-and-beans claim got on our own data. Marigolds were once
listed as inhibiting beans, a claim repeated widely and supported nowhere; rather than leave it
unmarked or silently delete it, we established it was folklore and removed it from the data with the
reasoning recorded. [Does companion planting actually
work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) goes through the five mechanisms in detail and
is the longer read behind this chart.

## How this chart is built

Every row is generated from the companion lists on our crop guides, read directly from the same
data file those pages use. Nothing here is typed out by hand, which means the chart cannot drift
away from the guides — a hand-maintained grid of 28 crops would be several hundred cells, and every
hand-maintained figure in this project has eventually disagreed with its source.

The reasons are derived too, rather than written per pair:

- **Botanical family** gives the shared-pest reason, in both directions. Each crop carries its
  family, so the chart can work out that [dill](/crops/dill/) and [carrots](/crops/carrot/) are both
  Apiaceae without anyone asserting it.
- **Insectary flowers** are a known set of herbs whose flower shape feeds small beneficials.
- **Watering conflict** follows from whether one plant is a woody Mediterranean herb and the other
  is not.
- Anything left over is tradition, and says so.

A name that has no guide on this site — nasturtium, leek, fennel and a few others — still appears
with its claim, without a link. Leaving it out would misrepresent what the guides say.

## Using it to plan a bed

The chart answers "what goes next to this", which is a smaller question than "how do I lay out a
bed". Two things matter more than any pairing on it:

**Spacing.** Crowding does more damage than any bad neighbour. The [plant spacing
calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) works from the same crop data as this chart, and the
[square foot garden planner](/tools/square-foot-garden-planner/) lays a bed out crop by crop.

**Grouping by need, not by tradition.** The single most useful application of this chart is the
watering conflict, because it is the one that actually kills plants. Put the woody herbs —
[lavender](/crops/lavender/), [rosemary](/crops/rosemary/), [sage](/crops/sage/),
[thyme](/crops/thyme/), [oregano](/crops/oregano/) — together at a dry edge, and keep the thirsty
vegetables together where you water. [Growing herbs](/crops/herbs/) covers that contrast, and
[planning a vegetable garden layout](/blog/planning-a-vegetable-garden-layout/) covers the rest of
the bed.

Get the spacing, sun and water right and the neighbours barely matter. That is not a popular thing
to say on a companion planting page, but it is what the evidence supports.

faqs:
  - question: >-
      What do the three numbers on a fertilizer bag mean?
    answer: >-
      They are percentages by weight of nitrogen, phosphate and potash, always in that order. A
      10-10-10 bag is 10% nitrogen, 10% phosphate and 10% potash, and the remaining 70% is
      carrier and other nutrients. So a 50 pound bag of 10-10-10 contains five pounds of actual
      nitrogen.
  - question: >-
      Is the second number phosphorus or phosphate?
    answer: >-
      Phosphate, written P2O5, and the third is potash, K2O. They are not the elements
      themselves. This matters less than it sounds, because extension service recommendations
      are normally written in the same phosphate and potash terms, so the two are consistent and
      no conversion is needed.
  - question: >-
      How much fertilizer does a vegetable garden need?
    answer: >-
      A common general figure is one pound of actual nitrogen per 1,000 square feet for a mixed
      vegetable plot, split across the season rather than given at once. But this varies by crop
      and by what your soil already holds, and only a soil test can tell you the second part.
      Legumes such as beans and peas fix their own nitrogen and need none.
  - question: >-
      Can I use too much fertilizer?
    answer: >-
      Easily. Excess nitrogen gives lush foliage and little fruit, makes plants attractive to
      aphids, and runs off into groundwater and streams. Excess phosphate accumulates in the
      soil for years and is a significant water pollutant. Under-applying is a slower problem to
      fix but a far cheaper mistake.
  - question: >-
      Why does the calculator show the other two nutrients?
    answer: >-
      Because that is where over-application quietly happens. If you aim at a nitrogen rate
      using a balanced fertilizer, you are also applying phosphate and potash whether the soil
      needs them or not. Doing that every year for a decade is how gardens end up with phosphate
      levels well above anything the plants can use.
  - question: >-
      Should I water fertilizer in?
    answer: >-
      Yes, for granular products on soil, unless rain is coming. Watering moves the nutrients to
      the root zone and reduces losses, and it washes granules off leaves where they can scorch.
      Do not water a foliar feed straight off the leaves, which defeats the point of applying it
      that way.

Fertilizer recommendations are written in pounds of a nutrient. Fertilizer is sold in
bags of product with three numbers on the front. This calculator does the conversion
between them, and — the part most calculators leave out — shows you what else the dose
you have just worked out is putting on the ground.

## How to use it

1. **Enter the area** you are feeding, in square feet or square metres.
2. **Enter the target rate** for the nutrient you are aiming at. If a source tells you
   "one pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet", that is a target rate of 1.
3. **Type the three numbers from the bag** into the N, P and K fields exactly as
   printed: 10-10-10 goes in as 10, 10, 10.
4. **Choose which nutrient you are aiming at.** Most vegetable and lawn
   recommendations are written as nitrogen, which is the default.

The table underneath the result shows the phosphate and potash that come along for the
ride. Worth a look before you spread.

## How it is calculated

```
lb of product = (target rate / (nutrient % / 100)) x (area / 1000)
```

Two steps in one line. First, divide the target rate by the nutrient's fraction to find
how much product carries that much nutrient per 1,000 square feet. Then scale by your
actual area.

The other two nutrients follow from the same weight of product:

```
lb of nutrient supplied = lb of product x (its label % / 100)
```

### A worked example

You want one pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet over a 400 square foot bed, using
a 10-10-10 fertilizer.

- Nitrogen fraction: 10% = **0.10**
- Product per 1,000 sq ft: 1 / 0.10 = **10 pounds**
- Scaled to your bed: 10 x (400 / 1000) = **4 pounds of product**
- Nitrogen supplied: 4 x 0.10 = **0.4 lb**
- Phosphate supplied: 4 x 0.10 = **0.4 lb**
- Potash supplied: 4 x 0.10 = **0.4 lb**

Now the same target with a 24-8-16 instead. Nitrogen is 24%, so you need 1 / 0.24 =
4.17 pounds per 1,000 sq ft, or **1.67 pounds** over the bed. That also supplies 0.13
lb of phosphate and 0.27 lb of potash — a third of the phosphate the balanced product
would have given for the same nitrogen.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Weigh it.** A cup of one granular product is not a cup of another — densities differ
enough to matter. A cheap kitchen scale removes all the guesswork here.

**Split the season rather than feeding once.** Most vegetables do better with a portion
at planting and the rest side-dressed as they grow. A single heavy dose is more likely
to be wasted, and more likely to burn.

**Get a soil test before adding phosphate or potash.** Nitrogen moves and leaches, so
it needs topping up. Phosphate and potash accumulate, and many long-established gardens
have plenty of both. A test costs little and can save you years of pointless applying.

**Skip nitrogen for beans and peas.** They fix their own from the air, and a nitrogen
feed gives you foliage instead of pods.

**Keep it away from stems and out of watercourses.** Granules resting against a stem
scorch it. Anything spread on a hard surface or a slope before rain ends up in a drain.

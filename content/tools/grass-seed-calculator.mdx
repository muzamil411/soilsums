  - question: >-
      How much grass seed do I need for 1,000 square feet?
    answer: >-
      It depends entirely on the grass. Tall fescue and perennial ryegrass have large seeds and
      want around 7 pounds per 1,000 square feet for a new lawn. Kentucky bluegrass has much
      smaller seed and needs around 2.5 pounds for the same area. Centipedegrass is finer still
      at under half a pound. Always check the rate for your species rather than using a general
      figure.
  - question: >-
      Why is the overseeding rate lower than the new lawn rate?
    answer: >-
      Because you are filling gaps in existing turf rather than covering bare ground. Roughly
      two thirds of the establishment rate is typical. Sowing a full new-lawn rate into existing
      grass mostly wastes seed, because the seedlings compete with established plants and with
      each other.
  - question: >-
      Can I use more seed to get a thicker lawn faster?
    answer: >-
      No, and it usually backfires. Overseeding at double the rate crowds the seedlings, which
      then compete for water and light, grow spindly and become vulnerable to damping off. A
      correctly sown lawn thickens on its own as the plants tiller and spread.
  - question: >-
      When is the best time to sow grass seed?
    answer: >-
      For cool-season grasses, late summer to early autumn is the best window, with spring
      second — the soil is warm, the nights are cooling and there is less weed competition.
      Warm-season grasses want late spring to early summer, once soil temperatures are reliably
      warm. Sowing outside those windows is the most common reason a lawn fails to establish.
  - question: >-
      Do I need to cover the seed with soil?
    answer: >-
      Barely. Grass seed needs light contact with soil, not burial. Raking it in lightly so it
      sits in the top quarter inch, then rolling or treading it down, is enough. A thin scatter
      of compost or a seed blanket helps keep it damp. Seed buried an inch deep will not come
      up.
  - question: >-
      How long until grass seed germinates?
    answer: >-
      Perennial ryegrass is fastest, often in five to seven days. Tall and fine fescues take one
      to two weeks. Kentucky bluegrass is slow, two to three weeks, which is why it is often
      blended with ryegrass. Warm-season grasses from seed vary widely. Whatever the species,
      the top half inch of soil must stay damp throughout, which in warm weather means light
      watering once or twice a day.

Grass seed rates vary more between species than most people expect — by a factor of
fifteen across the common ones — because seed size differs so much. This calculator
uses per-species rates rather than a single average, for either a new lawn from bare
soil or an overseed of existing turf.

## How to use it

1. **Enter the lawn area.** For an awkward shape, break it into rectangles and add them
   up, or measure the whole plot and subtract the house, drive and beds.
2. **Choose your grass.** If you are overseeding and do not know what is there, match
   the climate: cool-season grasses in the northern US, Canada and the UK; warm-season
   in the southern US and Australia.
3. **Say what you are doing.** Bare soil takes the full establishment rate; existing
   turf takes the lower overseeding rate.

## How it is calculated

```
pounds of seed = rate (lb per 1,000 sq ft) x (area / 1000)
```

The whole question is which rate. Rates come from
`data/grass-seed-rates.ts`, one figure per species for a new lawn and one for
overseeding, and the note under the result explains anything particular about that
grass.

### A worked example

A 5,000 square foot lawn, new, in tall fescue:

- Rate: **7 lb per 1,000 sq ft**
- Seed needed: 7 x (5,000 / 1,000) = **35 pounds**

Overseeding the same lawn instead uses the lower rate of 5 lb per 1,000 sq ft, so
**25 pounds**. Switch the species to Kentucky bluegrass and the new-lawn figure drops to
**12.5 pounds**, because bluegrass seed is so much smaller that a pound goes far further.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Sow half in one direction and half at right angles.** A single pass always leaves
stripes. Two passes crossing each other is the simplest way to get even coverage from a
spreader or by hand.

**Keep the surface damp, not the soil soaked.** Germinating seed dies if the top half
inch dries out even once. That usually means a light watering once or twice a day for
two or three weeks, not a deep soak every few days.

**Do not fertilise heavily at sowing.** A starter fertilizer with phosphorus helps root
development, but a nitrogen-heavy feed encourages the weeds that are competing with
your seedlings.

**Check the seed label for coating and filler.** Some products are a mix of seed, inert
matter and mulch, so a given weight contains less actual seed than a bag of pure seed.
The percentages are on the label.

**Buy fresh seed.** Germination falls off after a couple of years in storage, and a
bargain bag of old seed is not a bargain.

  - question: >-
      How many quarts of soil does a 12 inch pot need?
    answer: >-
      About 17 US dry quarts, filled to ten inches deep. A 10 inch pot takes around 10 dry
      quarts, a 14 inch pot around 26. Those are cylinder figures — real pots taper towards the
      base, so you will usually get away with slightly less.
  - question: >-
      Can I reuse last year's potting mix?
    answer: >-
      Partly. Old mix has lost structure and most of its nutrients, and may carry root disease
      from whatever grew in it. Tipping out the top half, mixing it with an equal volume of
      fresh mix and compost, and topping up works well for annuals. Start fresh for seedlings
      and for anything that struggled last year.
  - question: >-
      Should I put gravel or crocks in the bottom for drainage?
    answer: >-
      No. It is one of the most persistent bits of bad garden advice. A coarse layer under a
      fine one raises the level at which water sits rather than lowering it, because water will
      not cross the boundary until the soil above is saturated. A drainage hole and a mix that
      drains freely is all a pot needs.
  - question: >-
      Is potting mix the same as garden soil or topsoil?
    answer: >-
      No, and the difference matters more in a pot than anywhere else. Potting mix is mostly
      peat or coir with bark and perlite, built to hold air as well as water in a confined
      space. Garden soil compacts into an airless brick inside a pot. Bagged topsoil and garden
      soil are for beds and borders, not containers.
  - question: >-
      How deep does a container need to be?
    answer: >-
      Six inches for salad leaves and most herbs. Eight to ten for bush beans, compact peppers
      and chard. Twelve or more for tomatoes, aubergines and anything that will grow tall enough
      to need an anchor. Depth matters more than width for most crops, since it is what buys the
      plant a reserve of water on a hot day.

Containers are sold by the inch and potting mix is sold by the quart, so the sum
between them is easy to get wrong. This calculator gives you the volume of a pot,
planter, window box or half barrel in the unit the bag is actually labelled in.

One conversion is worth stating plainly because so much of the internet gets it wrong:
bagged potting mix in the United States is sold in **US dry quarts**, and a cubic foot
is **25.71 dry quarts**. The figure 29.92 that appears on many calculator pages is
liquid quarts, a different unit, and using it overstates what a container holds by
about 16%.

## How to use it

1. **Start from a common container if one fits** — pot sizes, window boxes, nursery
   trade sizes and half barrels are all in the dropdown. The measurements fill in and
   stay editable.
2. **Or measure your own.** For a round pot, the diameter across the top of the soil.
   For a square or rectangular planter, the inside width and length.
3. **Enter the soil depth**, not the full height of the pot. Fill to about an inch
   below the rim so watering does not wash mix over the edge.
4. **Set how many containers** you are filling, and the size of the bag you are
   buying — dry quarts in imperial, liters in metric.

## How it is calculated

A round pot or a half barrel is a cylinder:

```
cubic inches = pi x (diameter / 2)^2 x depth
```

A square or rectangular planter is a box:

```
cubic inches = length x width x depth
```

Then everything divides by 1,728, the number of cubic inches in a cubic foot, and
converts from there:

```
cubic feet    = cubic inches / 1728
US dry quarts = cubic feet x 25.714
US gallons    = cubic feet x 7.4805
liters        = cubic feet x 28.317
```

The dry quart is exactly 67.200625 cubic inches by definition, which is where 25.714
comes from: 1,728 / 67.200625.

### A worked example

A twelve inch round pot, filled ten inches deep:

- Radius: 12 / 2 = **6 inches**
- Volume: pi x 6 x 6 x 10 = **1,131 cubic inches**
- In cubic feet: 1,131 / 1,728 = **0.654 cubic feet**
- In dry quarts: 0.654 x 25.714 = **16.8 US dry quarts**
- In liters: 0.654 x 28.317 = **18.5 liters**

A 25 dry quart bag fills one of those pots with some left over. Four of them need three
bags.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Pots taper, so buy for the calculated figure and expect to use a little less.** The
cylinder maths treats a pot as straight-sided. A typical tapered pot holds ten to
twenty per cent less than its top diameter suggests.

**Do not fill to the rim.** An inch of freeboard is the difference between watering a
pot and washing half of it onto the patio.

**Big containers are easier than small ones.** The larger the volume, the longer it
takes to dry out, and drying out is what kills most container plants. If you are
choosing between two sizes, take the bigger one.

**Buy mix by volume, not by weight.** Bag weights vary with how wet the mix is. The
quart or liter figure is the one that tells you whether it will fill your pots.

**Nursery "gallon" sizes are trade sizes, not liquid gallons.** A #1 "one gallon" pot
holds roughly 0.7 US liquid gallons. The presets above use measured dimensions rather
than the label.

  - question: >-
      Why is the estimate a range rather than a number?
    answer: >-
      Because a single number would be a false promise. Yield depends on variety, season length,
      weather, soil, water, pests and how diligently you pick. A two-fold spread between a poor
      year and a good one is normal for most crops. The honest answer is a range, and even that
      should be read as a guide.
  - question: >-
      What affects yield most?
    answer: >-
      Water and picking, in that order, for most crops. Inconsistent watering during fruit set
      costs more yield than almost anything else, and plants such as beans, cucumbers and
      zucchini slow down sharply once mature fruit is left on them. After that: enough sun,
      spacing that is not crowded, and a variety suited to your season length.
  - question: >-
      How do you convert row length into plants?
    answer: >-
      By dividing the row length by the crop's in-row spacing. A ten foot row is 120 inches, so
      at three inch carrot spacing it holds 40 plants. Partial plants are rounded down, because
      two thirds of a carrot position grows nothing. Row spacing is not involved — that is the
      gap between rows, not within them.
  - question: >-
      Are these yields per plant or per season?
    answer: >-
      Per plant, for the whole season. A tomato plant's eight to fifteen pounds is the total it
      produces across all its pickings, not a single harvest. For cut-and-come-again crops the
      figure covers repeated cutting over the season.
  - question: >-
      Why does strawberry carry a warning?
    answer: >-
      Because it is a perennial and its first year is not typical. June-bearing strawberries are
      usually not allowed to fruit in the year they are planted, so the real harvest starts the
      season after. The figures here describe an established planting, and the calculator flags
      that rather than quietly overpromising.

Yield figures are the easiest thing in gardening to exaggerate, and the hardest to
predict. This estimator gives a low-to-high range from published per-plant figures, and
the range is wide on purpose.

## How to use it

1. **Choose how you are counting.** By the plant, if you know how many you are putting
   in. By row length, if you are thinking in feet or metres of row.
2. **Add each crop** and its quantity. The dropdown shows each crop's per-plant range,
   so you can see where the total is coming from.
3. **Add as many crops as you like.** The table underneath breaks the total down crop by
   crop.

## How it is calculated

```
low  = plants x low yield per plant
high = plants x high yield per plant
```

When you are working in row length, the plant count comes from the crop's own in-row
spacing:

```
plants = floor(row length in inches / plant spacing in inches)
```

The rounding down is deliberate: a row that is two and a half plant spacings long holds
two plants, not two and a half. Row spacing plays no part — that is the gap between
rows, not within them.

### A worked example

Four tomatoes, two zucchini and twelve lettuces:

- Tomato: 4 x 8 to 15 lb = **32 to 60 lb**
- Zucchini: 2 x 6 to 12 lb = **12 to 24 lb**
- Lettuce: 12 x 0.5 to 1 lb = **6 to 12 lb**
- Total: **50 to 96 pounds**, or 23 to 44 kilograms

Note where that total comes from. Four tomato plants produce more than twelve lettuces
by a wide margin, which is worth knowing when a small bed has to be allocated.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Pick often.** Beans, cucumbers, zucchini and peas slow right down once mature fruit
sits on the plant. Regular picking is the cheapest yield increase available.

**Water consistently during fruit set.** A dry fortnight while tomatoes and peppers are
setting costs more than any fertiliser will recover.

**Do not plan a season around the high figure.** The low end is the safer number for
deciding whether four plants will feed a household. The high end happens in a good year
with a good variety and attentive care.

**Count the preserving, not just the eating.** A glut is only a problem if you cannot
store it. Twelve tomato plants is a lot to eat fresh and a reasonable amount to turn
into sauce.

**Space, sun and water beat plant count.** Six well-spaced plants in full sun reliably
beat twelve crowded ones in part shade. If your estimate looks too low, the answer is
rarely to squeeze more in.

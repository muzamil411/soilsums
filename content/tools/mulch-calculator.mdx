  - question: >-
      How deep should mulch be?
    answer: >-
      Two to three inches is right for most beds. That is enough to block light from weed seeds
      and slow evaporation, without keeping the soil so cold and wet that roots suffer. Fine
      mulches such as compost or shredded leaves work at the shallower end; coarse bark chips
      can go to three or four inches because water and air move through them more freely.
  - question: >-
      How many bags of mulch are in a cubic yard?
    answer: >-
      Thirteen and a half bags at two cubic feet each, since a cubic yard is 27 cubic feet. Nine
      bags if they are three cubic feet. That crossover is roughly where bulk delivery starts to
      be cheaper than bagged, depending on the delivery charge.
  - question: >-
      How much area does one bag of mulch cover?
    answer: >-
      A two cubic foot bag covers 12 square feet at two inches deep, 8 square feet at three
      inches, or 6 square feet at four inches. The calculator shows this figure for whatever bag
      size and depth you enter, which is the useful number to have in your head at the shop.
  - question: >-
      Should mulch touch the stems of plants?
    answer: >-
      No. Pull it back an inch or two from stems and trunks. Mulch piled against bark stays
      damp, and damp bark invites rot and gives voles somewhere to work unseen. The volcano of
      mulch heaped around a young tree is one of the most common and most damaging garden
      habits.
  - question: >-
      Does mulch steal nitrogen from the soil?
    answer: >-
      Only at the boundary where it meets the soil, and only while it is breaking down. Woody
      mulch laid on the surface has very little effect on the root zone beneath. Digging fresh
      wood chips into the soil is a different matter and will tie up nitrogen for a season, so
      keep them on top.
  - question: >-
      When should I put mulch down?
    answer: >-
      For weed control and moisture, mulch in late spring once the soil has warmed up, since a
      thick layer on cold soil keeps it cold. For winter protection, mulch after the ground has
      begun to freeze rather than before, so you are keeping the cold in and the freeze-thaw
      cycle out rather than insulating warm soil and encouraging growth.

Mulch is sold by volume and spread by area, which is why the arithmetic catches people
out. A bag that looks generous can disappear across a surprisingly small bed. This
calculator turns the area you are covering and the depth you want into a number of
bags, and tells you how far a single bag goes at that depth — the figure worth knowing
before you get to the checkout.

## How to use it

1. **Tell it what you know about the bed.** Enter a length and width, a diameter for a
   round bed or tree ring, or type the area straight in if you already have it.
2. **Set the depth.** Two to three inches suits most beds. The calculator will happily
   work at any depth, but it is worth reading the note above about why deeper is not
   automatically better.
3. **Choose your bag size.** Two cubic feet is close to universal for bagged mulch in
   the United States; three cubic foot bags exist, and metric bags run 50 to 70 liters.
4. Use the **metric toggle** if you work in metres and centimetres.

For an L-shaped or awkward bed, split it into rectangles, run each one and add the
results — that is more accurate than guessing an average width.

## How it is calculated

```
cubic feet = area (sq ft) x depth (in) / 12
cubic yards = cubic feet / 27
bags = cubic feet / bag size, rounded up
```

Depth arrives in inches and area in square feet, so the depth has to become feet before
they multiply — hence the division by twelve. Forgetting it is the single most common
error in this sum, and it produces an answer twelve times too big.

The coverage figure runs the same arithmetic backwards:

```
square feet per bag = bag size (cu ft) x 12 / depth (in)
```

### A worked example

A bed twenty feet by ten, mulched three inches deep:

- Area: 20 x 10 = **200 square feet**
- Volume: 200 x 3 / 12 = **50 cubic feet**
- In cubic yards: 50 / 27 = **1.85 cubic yards**
- At two cubic feet a bag: 50 / 2 = **25 bags**
- Coverage: 2 x 12 / 3 = **8 square feet per bag**

## Tips and common mistakes

**Measure the bed, not the lawn it sits in.** Mulching to the edge of a border and then
along a path adds up fast. Beds are usually smaller than they look from the house and
larger than they look up close.

**Keep it off stems and trunks.** Leave an inch or two of clear ground around anything
woody. Mulch against bark holds moisture where you least want it.

**Do not just add three more inches every spring.** Check what is already there first.
Mulch breaks down slowly, and layers accumulate into a mat that sheds water rather than
letting it through. Rake the old layer to break the crust, then top up to the depth you
want in total.

**Coarse mulch lasts longer; fine mulch feeds the soil.** Bark chips and pine nuggets
hold their structure for two or three seasons. Shredded leaves, compost and grass
clippings disappear within one, but leave the soil visibly better for it. Neither is
wrong — they are doing different jobs.

**Buy a little extra for edges.** Beds are rarely as tidy as their measurements, and
sloped ground needs more at the bottom than the top.

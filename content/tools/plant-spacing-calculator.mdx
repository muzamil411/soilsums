  - question: >-
      Does staggered planting really fit 15% more plants?
    answer: >-
      About 15.5% more in theory, and usually less in practice. The theoretical gain comes from
      rows sitting 0.866 of a spacing apart instead of a full spacing. But rows and plants come
      in whole numbers, so a bed only benefits if it is deep enough to fit the extra row that
      tighter pitch makes room for. In a 4 foot wide bed at 12 inch spacing there is no room for
      a fifth row, and staggering fits fewer plants, not more. The calculator shows both layouts
      so you can see which wins in your bed.
  - question: >-
      What is the difference between plant spacing and row spacing?
    answer: >-
      Plant spacing is the gap between plants within a row. Row spacing is the gap between the
      rows themselves, and on a seed packet it is usually set by the access a person or a hoe
      needs rather than by what the plant wants. In a raised bed you rarely need to walk between
      rows, which is why intensive spacing in beds can be much tighter than a packet suggests.
  - question: >-
      Can I ignore the spacing on the seed packet?
    answer: >-
      Partly, and carefully. Packet spacings often assume field rows with walking room, so
      closing the row spacing up in a bed is usually fine. The in-row figure is closer to what
      the plant actually needs for light and air, and crowding it invites mildew and small
      fruit. Reduce row spacing first, in-row spacing last.
  - question: >-
      How do I space plants near the edge of a bed?
    answer: >-
      This calculator leaves half a spacing at each edge, so plants are not jammed against the
      frame. That is the convention that matches how beds are actually planted, and it is why an
      8 foot row at 12 inch spacing fits 8 plants rather than 9.
  - question: >-
      Why does the calculator say fewer plants than area divided by spacing?
    answer: >-
      Because it counts whole plants that actually fit, not an area average. Dividing 32 square
      feet by one square foot per plant gives a tidy 32, but a bed 7 feet 6 inches long fits the
      same number of plants as one 8 feet long — the leftover 6 inches grows nothing. Counting
      positions rather than dividing areas is the honest answer.
  - question: >-
      How many plants per square foot does my spacing give me?
    answer: >-
      The theoretical density for rectangular rows is 144 ÷ (plant spacing in inches × row
      spacing in inches). The shortcut 144 ÷ spacing² applies only when both spacings are
      equal: 12 inch plant and row spacing gives 1 plant per square foot, 6 and 6 gives 4,
      and 6 inch plant spacing with 12 inch rows gives 2.
      That is theoretical. The calculator counts whole planting positions that actually fit
      your bed, then shows square feet per plant — bed area divided by that count — which is
      the inverse of density, not plants per square foot. For your bed's actual average
      density, divide the plant count by the bed area. The staggered layout derives its row
      pitch from the plant spacing, so the rectangular formula does not describe that layout.
  - question: >-
      Can I use this for seed spacing?
    answer: >-
      Yes, to estimate final planting positions after thinning. Enter the packet's recommended
      final plant spacing and row spacing — not how thickly you sow. The result is the number
      of positions for established plants, not the number of seeds to buy or sow: it does not
      account for germination rates, sowing multiple seeds per position, or losses along
      the way.
  - question: >-
      Does this work for groundcover spacing?
    answer: >-
      Yes, to count planting positions. Use the recommended center-to-center spacing for your
      chosen groundcover and site — check the plant label or nursery guidance rather than
      assuming it equals the mature spread. If the recommendation gives equal spacing in both
      directions, enter the same figure in both spacing fields. The staggered layout derives
      its row pitch from the plant spacing, so compare both layouts for your bed. The
      calculator estimates how many positions fit; it does not predict how quickly the
      ground will be covered.
  - question: >-
      Does closer spacing mean a bigger harvest?
    answer: >-
      Up to a point, then it reverses. Tighter spacing raises yield per square foot while each
      plant still gets enough light and water, which is the whole idea behind intensive bed
      growing. Past that point plants compete, fruit gets smaller, air stops moving through the
      foliage and disease follows. Crowding is a common cause of a disappointing harvest from a
      full-looking bed.

Seed packets give a spacing. Beds come in whatever size you built them. This calculator
turns the two into a plant count, and shows what you gain — or do not gain — by
staggering the rows instead of planting a square grid.

## How to use it

1. **Pick a crop** to fill in its published spacing, or set the two spacings yourself.
   Either way both fields stay editable.
2. **Enter the bed's inside dimensions.**
3. **Choose a layout.** A square grid lines plants up in both directions. Staggered
   rows offset every other row by half a spacing, so each plant sits in the gap between
   two in the row behind.
4. Read the comparison table underneath. It gives both layouts for your bed, so the
   choice is made on numbers rather than on a rule of thumb.

## How it is calculated

For a square grid, the calculator counts how many plant positions fit along each
direction, leaving half a spacing at each edge:

```
plants per row = floor(bed length in inches / plant spacing)
rows           = floor(bed width in inches / row spacing)
total          = rows x plants per row
```

The staggered layout is where it gets interesting. If every plant must stay at least
distance `d` from its neighbours, offsetting alternate rows lets the rows sit closer
together — specifically `d x sqrt(3)/2`, which is about `0.866d`, because the three
plants around any gap form an equilateral triangle:

```
row pitch      = plant spacing x 0.866
rows           = floor(bed width in inches / row pitch)
full rows      = plants per row
offset rows    = plants per row, or one fewer when there is no slack at the end
```

That tighter pitch is where the often-quoted 15% comes from: 1 / 0.866 = 1.155. Note
that in the staggered layout the **row spacing input is not used** for the plant count —
the pitch is derived from the plant spacing. The row spacing figure is kept for the
square-grid comparison.

### A worked example

A twenty by twenty foot plot at twelve inch spacing.

Square grid:

- 240 inches / 12 = **20 plants per row**
- 240 inches / 12 = **20 rows**
- Total: **400 plants**

Staggered:

- Row pitch: 12 x 0.866 = **10.39 inches**
- 240 / 10.39 = **23 rows**
- Full rows hold 20; offset rows hold 19, since 240 inches leaves no slack for the
  half-spacing shift
- 12 full rows x 20 + 11 offset rows x 19 = **449 plants**

That is 12.3% more, not 15.5%. The difference is the plant lost from the end of each
staggered row, plus the fact that rows only come in whole numbers. A flat 15% would
have promised 460.

## Tips and common mistakes

**Staggering is worth it in wide beds, not narrow ones.** The gain only appears when the
tighter row pitch lets an extra row in. Run both numbers before you plan a bed around
it.

**Leave yourself room to reach.** A bed wider than four feet is hard to work from the
sides, whatever the spacing says. Plants you cannot reach do not get picked.

**Spacing assumes the plant reaches full size.** Harvesting young — cut-and-come-again
leaves, baby carrots — lets you plant tighter than any table suggests.

**Tall crops shade short ones.** Put the tomatoes and corn where they will not put the
lettuce in shadow all afternoon, which in the northern hemisphere usually means the
north side of the bed.

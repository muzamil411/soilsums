Plant strawberries **15 inches apart in rows 36 inches apart**, about **2 weeks before your last
frost**. They are grown from bare-root crowns or runners rather than seed, and how you space them
depends on the system: June-bearers fill in as matted rows, day-neutrals are grown in hills with
runners removed.

## How far apart to plant strawberries

Fifteen inches between plants, thirty-six inches between rows.

That row spacing looks wide for a small plant, and it is there because June-bearing strawberries
are meant to fill it. Runners root between the plants and the row widens into a mat over the
first season.

The spacing-derived density is roughly one plant per two square feet. The Cornell square foot
gardening page does not name strawberries, so there is no square foot gardening figure for them —
and a matted row does not fit a grid anyway, since the whole system depends on the plants
spreading.

## Bare-root or potted, and when

The two come with different planting dates, and it is worth knowing which you have bought.

| Type                     | When to plant                                                               |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dormant bare-root crowns | Early, about 2 weeks before your last frost, while the ground is still cold |
| Potted plants in growth  | Later, once growth has started and frost risk has passed                    |

Bare-root is cheaper, establishes well and is what most mail-order suppliers send. Potted plants
are more forgiving if you are late.

**Plant the crown exactly at soil level.** The point where roots meet leaves should sit on the
surface — buried crowns rot, exposed ones dry out. This one detail accounts for a large share of
first-year losses.

Strawberries are not sown from seed, and are not started indoors.

## Two systems, two spacings

**June-bearing, matted rows.** One heavy crop over two or three weeks in early summer. Plants are
set at the spacing above and runners are allowed to root and fill the row. Pinch off the first
year's flowers so the plant builds crown and roots instead — the following year's crop is much
larger for it.

**Day-neutral, hills.** A lighter crop spread from midsummer to frost. Plants are grown
individually and **runners are removed** as they appear, keeping the energy in the parent. These
are usually allowed to fruit in their first year.

Decide which you are growing before you plant, because the runner management is opposite.

## Soil, sun and water

**8 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 5.5 to 6.5**.

That pH range is lower than most vegetables want. Strawberries are mildly acid-loving, so a bed
limed for brassicas is the wrong bed for them. If your soil is well above 6.5,
[how much sulfur to lower soil pH](/blog/how-much-sulfur-to-lower-soil-ph/) covers what that
takes — though for a bed this size, building a dedicated one is often simpler.

Mulch with straw once the plants are established. It keeps the fruit off wet soil, which is the
main defence against grey mould, and suppresses the weeds that shallow-rooted strawberries
compete with badly.
[Best mulch for a vegetable garden](/blog/best-mulch-for-a-vegetable-garden/) covers the options.

## Feeding and renovation

Feed **after harvest, not before**. Nitrogen in spring produces soft leafy growth and soft fruit
that moulds.

Renovate a June-bearing bed once picking finishes: mow the leaves off above the crowns, thin
crowded plants, weed and feed. That is what keeps a matted row productive rather than
progressively more crowded.

Replace plants every three or four years, starting a new bed elsewhere from runners off healthy
plants.

## Harvest

**0.5 to 1 lb per plant** in a good year. There is no days-to-maturity figure, because
strawberries are perennial — the first real crop from June-bearers comes the season after
planting.

Pick every couple of days in the season, with the stem attached, in the morning. A strawberry
does not ripen further once picked.

## What goes wrong

**Grey mould** on fruit touching damp soil. **Slugs**, which mulch unfortunately also shelters —
keep it clear of the crowns. **Birds**, which need netting rather than deterrence. **Declining
yield from old plants**, which is the three-to-four-year replacement cycle.

Spinach, lettuce, onion and thyme are the traditional neighbours. Keep strawberries away from
cabbage, broccoli and tomatoes.

Sow peas **4 weeks before your last frost date**, and only once the soil has reached **45°F
(7°C)**. That second condition matters more than the calendar: peas sown into colder ground sit
unsprouted and rot. Space them **3 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart**, with a support
already in place.

## When to plant peas

Four weeks before your last frost, soil temperature permitting.

Peas are one of the few crops that genuinely want cold conditions, and one of the first things
that can go in. But a frost-free date is only half the instruction — the soil has to be at 45°F
or warmer at sowing depth, or the seed sits in wet cold ground and rots before it germinates. A
cheap soil thermometer settles it in seconds.

[What does last frost date mean](/blog/what-does-last-frost-date-mean/) explains why that date is
a probability rather than a promise, which matters here: peas take a light frost without
complaint, so sowing slightly early costs you little.

In milder regions a second sowing in late summer gives an autumn crop, timed so the plants
mature before hard frost.

## How far apart to plant peas

**3 inches between plants, 24 inches between rows.** Under the square foot gardening method —
Mel Bartholomew's convention, not an extension recommendation — that is nine to a square.

The wide row spacing looks generous for such a narrow plant, and it is there for two reasons:
you need to get in to pick, and airflow between rows is what keeps powdery mildew off late in
the season.

A double row either side of a single trellis is the efficient arrangement, and it halves the
amount of support you have to build.

## Support

Put it in before sowing. Even dwarf varieties crop better with something to climb — pods stay
off the soil, the plants are easier to pick, and air moves through.

Netting, pea sticks, string between posts and wire mesh all work. Match the height to the
variety: a tall pea will go past six feet, and a plant that outgrows its support flops and
becomes a mildew problem.

## Soil, sun and water

Peas want **6 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week** and soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.5**.
They are not fussy about fertility.

What they will not tolerate is waterlogged ground. Root rot in cold wet soil is the other half of
the early-sowing problem, so if your bed sits wet in spring, raise it or wait a week.

## Do not feed them nitrogen

Peas host bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air. A nitrogen feed produces dense leafy vines and
very few pods — the classic mistake with all legumes.

Give phosphorus and potassium if a soil test asks for them, and nothing else. Follow peas with a
hungry crop such as brassicas, which will use the nitrogen the pea roots leave behind. That is
one of the few companion-planting mechanisms with real evidence behind it, as
[does companion planting actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) sets out.

Keep peas away from onions and garlic.

## Harvest

**55 to 70 days**, at roughly **0.2 to 0.4 lb per plant** — which is why peas are grown by the
row rather than by the plant.

Pick often. Every pod left to mature on the vine signals the plant to stop flowering, so
harvesting every couple of days extends the season more than anything else you can do. Shelling
peas are ready when the pods are filled but still bright; snap and snow peas are best picked
young.

## Why the season ends so suddenly

Peas stop setting pods once daytime temperatures stay above about 80°F, and then decline fast.
That is not a failure of care — it is what peas do, and it is why succession sowing them two
weeks apart is less useful than it sounds. Two or three sowings a fortnight apart in early
spring is the sensible limit;
[succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers the crops where the
technique pays better.

Powdery mildew, pea moth and root rot in wet soil are the other things to expect.

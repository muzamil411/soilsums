faqs:
  - question: >-
      Why is my basil flowering, and should I stop it?
    answer: >-
      Basil flowers when it reaches maturity or comes under stress, and once it does the leaves
      turn smaller and less aromatic. Pinch the flower buds out as soon as you see them, along
      with the pair of leaves below. A plant pinched regularly from the start stays bushy and
      productive for months; one left to flower is finished in weeks.
  - question: >-
      Why are my basil leaves turning black?
    answer: >-
      Cold, most likely. Basil is damaged below about 50F and blackens at the leaf edges, which
      is why it sulks if planted out too early. If the blackening is on the undersides as fuzzy
      grey-purple patches, it is downy mildew, which spreads in humid still air and is best
      handled by spacing plants further apart and watering in the morning.
  - question: >-
      How should I harvest basil so the plant keeps growing?
    answer: >-
      Cut whole stems just above a pair of leaves rather than picking individual leaves off. The
      plant then branches from that joint and produces two stems where there was one. Harvesting
      from the top down keeps it compact; stripping the lower leaves leaves you with a
      bare-legged plant.
  - question: >-
      Can I grow basil indoors on a windowsill?
    answer: >-
      Yes, with enough light. Basil needs six hours of direct sun or a grow light to stay
      compact, and a south-facing sill in summer will do it. In winter, a windowsill alone
      usually produces pale, leggy growth. It also dislikes cold draughts, so keep it away from
      the glass on frosty nights.
  - question: >-
      Why does my basil wilt even though the soil is damp?
    answer: >-
      Either the roots are sitting in water, which suffocates them, or the plant is in full sun
      on a very hot day and cannot move water fast enough. Check drainage first. A basil in a
      pot without a drainage hole, or in a saucer of standing water, will wilt and then
      collapse.
  - question: >-
      Should I feed basil?
    answer: >-
      Lightly, if at all. Basil grown in rich soil with plenty of nitrogen produces large leaves
      with noticeably less aromatic oil in them — you get quantity at the cost of flavour.
      Compost worked into the bed is enough. A pot may want an occasional weak feed because the
      mix runs out, but restraint is the rule.

Basil asks for two things: warmth and regular pinching. Give it those and it will produce for
the whole summer. Get either wrong and you have a leggy plant with a flower spike on top by
July.

## Warmth first

Basil is a tender annual from a hot climate and it does not pretend otherwise. It suffers below
50F, blackening at the leaf edges, and a plant set out too early never really recovers.

Sow indoors six weeks before your last frost date and plant out a week after it, once nights
are reliably mild. Direct sowing works too, from about a week after the last frost. Basil
germinates quickly in warm soil and slowly in cool.

Give it the warmest, sunniest spot you have. A south-facing wall or a patio that holds heat
suits it better than an open bed.

## Spacing

Ten inches between plants, 18 inches between rows, or four to a square foot. That seems close
for a plant that gets bushy, and it is deliberate — basil grown with a little company stays
upright. Too crowded, though, and the still humid air between plants invites downy mildew,
which is now the most common serious basil problem.

## Water and food

An inch a week. Basil likes steady moisture and hates sitting wet, which is a real distinction:
a plant in badly drained soil or a pot without a drainage hole will wilt and collapse even
though the soil is damp.

Water the soil in the morning, so any splash on the leaves dries during the day.

Feed sparingly, which is counter-intuitive for a leaf crop. The aromatic oils that make basil
worth growing are diluted by lush, nitrogen-driven growth. Compost worked into the bed before
planting is usually all it needs for a season in the ground.

## Pinching, which is the whole technique

This is what separates a good basil plant from a disappointing one.

Start when the plant has three or four pairs of leaves: pinch out the growing tip just above a
leaf pair. The plant responds by sending out two shoots from that joint. Do the same to each of
those once they have grown, and the plant doubles its branches each time.

Then keep pinching out flower buds the moment they appear, taking the pair of leaves beneath
them as well. A flowering basil redirects everything into seed and the leaves go small and
sharp-tasting.

Harvest the same way — cut whole stems above a leaf pair rather than picking individual leaves.
Every harvest is also a pinch.

## Companions and containers

Basil is traditionally planted with tomatoes, and while the pest-repelling claims are folklore
rather than science, the two genuinely want the same conditions and get harvested together, so
the pairing is practical.

It grows very well in a pot — an eight to ten inch container suits one plant, and a window box
takes three. Container basil needs watering more often and benefits from an occasional weak
feed, since a small volume of mix runs out of nutrients within weeks.

## Ending the season

Basil will not survive frost and does not overwinter outdoors. Before the first frost, cut the
whole plant and use it: pesto freezes well, and basil dries poorly compared with woodier herbs.
Cuttings root readily in water if you want a plant on the windowsill through autumn.

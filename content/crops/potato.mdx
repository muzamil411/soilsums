  - question: >-
      Can I plant potatoes from the supermarket?
    answer: >-
      You can, and it is usually a poor idea. Shop potatoes are often treated to suppress
      sprouting, and more importantly they carry no guarantee against the virus and blight
      diseases that certified seed potatoes are tested for. Introducing those into a bed causes
      problems for years. Certified seed potatoes are inexpensive and worth it.
  - question: >-
      Why are my potatoes green?
    answer: >-
      Light reached the tubers, which produces chlorophyll and, alongside it, solanine. Green
      potatoes taste bitter and should not be eaten in quantity. It comes from planting too
      shallow or not earthing up as the plants grow. Keep the tubers covered with soil or a
      thick mulch throughout the season.
  - question: >-
      What causes rough scabby patches on potato skins?
    answer: >-
      Common scab, a soil-borne problem that gets worse as pH rises. It is cosmetic — the
      potatoes are perfectly edible once peeled — but it is the main reason not to lime a potato
      bed. Potatoes prefer slightly acid soil, around pH 5.0 to 6.5, and are the one crop where
      you deliberately leave the pH low.
  - question: >-
      Do I need to chit potatoes before planting?
    answer: >-
      It is optional and mildly useful. Standing seed potatoes in a cool, light place for a few
      weeks until short green shoots appear gives them a head start, which can bring an early
      crop forward by a week or two. Long pale shoots mean the place was too warm and too dark,
      and those break off easily during planting.
  - question: >-
      When should I harvest potatoes?
    answer: >-
      New potatoes from around the time the plants flower, by feeling gently into the soil
      without lifting the plant. Maincrop potatoes for storage once the foliage has yellowed and
      died back, then leave them two more weeks in the ground to set their skins. Lifting too
      early gives thin skins that will not keep.
  - question: >-
      How many potatoes will one plant produce?
    answer: >-
      Two to five pounds in decent conditions, from a single seed potato. Ten plants is a
      reasonable garden row and will give a household a useful quantity for several weeks rather
      than a year's supply. Containers and grow bags work and yield at the lower end of that
      range.

If you came here meaning the other one, [sweet potatoes](/crops/sweet-potato/) are an unrelated
plant in a different family, grown from slips rather than seed potatoes and planted well after
the last frost instead of before it.

This page has the numbers for potatoes: seed potatoes planted 4 inches deep and 12
inches apart with 30 inches between rows, two weeks before your last frost, an inch and a
half of water a week, and 2 to 5 pounds per plant over a 70 to 120 day season.

One figure sets potatoes apart from everything else in the bed: they want soil at pH 5.0
to 6.5, more acid than any other crop here, because scab gets worse as pH rises. The page
covers that, the earthing up that keeps tubers from greening, and why certified seed
potatoes are worth the money.

## Seed potatoes

Buy certified seed potatoes. They cost a little more than eating potatoes and they are tested
for the virus and blight problems that, once introduced to a bed, are very hard to get rid of.
Supermarket potatoes are frequently treated to stop them sprouting anyway.

Chitting — standing them in a cool, bright place for a few weeks until stubby green shoots
appear — gives a modest head start. Cool and bright is the point; a warm dark cupboard produces
long pale shoots that snap off when you plant.

Large seed potatoes can be cut into pieces with at least two eyes each, left a day or two for
the cut faces to dry, then planted.

## Planting

Plant about two weeks before your last frost date, as soon as the soil is workable. Potatoes
are not frost-hardy above ground, but they take a while to emerge, and a light frost on early
foliage sets them back without killing them.

Set the tubers four inches deep, twelve inches apart, with 30 inches between rows. That row
spacing is not padding: the plants get large and need the room, and you need somewhere to draw
soil from when earthing up.

## Do not lime the bed

Potatoes prefer soil at pH 5.0 to 6.5, more acid than most vegetables want, and scab gets
noticeably worse as pH climbs towards neutral. If a bed is in rotation, apply any lime in the
year it grows brassicas rather than the year it grows potatoes.

On feeding: moderate nitrogen and generous potassium. Too much nitrogen gives magnificent
foliage and modest tubers. Fresh manure is best avoided, as it encourages scab.

## Earthing up

As the shoots reach eight inches or so, draw soil up around the stems to make a ridge, leaving
the top few inches of foliage showing. Repeat once or twice more over the following weeks.

This does two jobs. It keeps developing tubers covered, which prevents them greening in the
light, and it gives the plant more buried stem to form tubers along. A thick straw mulch does
the same job if drawing soil is impractical.

## Water

An inch and a half a week, and it matters most from flowering onwards, when the tubers are
actually forming. Inconsistent watering at that stage produces knobbly, cracked potatoes and
hollow centres. Mulch helps considerably.

Ease off as the foliage begins to die back, which is the plant finishing rather than a problem.

## Harvest

For new potatoes, start feeling into the ridge around flowering time and take a few from each
plant without disturbing the rest.

For storage, wait until the foliage has yellowed and collapsed, then leave the tubers in the
ground for another two weeks so their skins toughen. Lift on a dry day, let them dry on the
surface for a few hours, and store in the dark somewhere cool. Any tuber with a spade wound
should be eaten rather than stored.

## Common trouble

**Colorado potato beetle** where it occurs: hand-pick the adults and scrape off the orange egg
clusters under the leaves.

**Late blight** turns foliage brown and collapses a crop quickly in warm humid weather. Cutting
and removing the foliage at the first sign protects the tubers below.

**Hollow heart and cracking** are water problems, not diseases.

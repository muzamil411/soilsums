Plant broccoli **18 inches apart in rows 30 inches apart**. Start seed indoors **8 weeks before
your last frost** and set transplants out **2 weeks before** it — broccoli takes a light frost
and wants to finish heading before summer heat arrives. Expect a head in **55 to 80 days** from
transplanting.

## How far apart to plant broccoli

Eighteen inches between plants, thirty inches between rows.

That is more room than a young transplant appears to need, and it is the right amount. A broccoli
plant ends up two feet across, and crowded plants produce small heads and hold damp air around
themselves, which is how downy mildew and aphid colonies get started.

The square foot gardening method — Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension
recommendation — gives one plant per square, which is tighter than the row spacing implies. That
works in a deep, rich, evenly watered bed and not much else;
[how does square foot gardening work](/blog/how-does-square-foot-gardening-work/) covers the
trade-off.

## When to plant broccoli

| Step           | Timing                         |
| -------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Sow indoors    | 8 weeks before last frost      |
| Transplant out | 2 weeks before last frost      |
| Harvest        | 55–80 days after transplanting |

Broccoli is a cool-season crop that needs to make its head before the weather turns. Going out
two weeks ahead of the frost date is deliberate — the plants tolerate it, and the head quality
depends on finishing in cool conditions.

For an autumn crop, count backwards from your first fall frost using the same maturity range and
add a couple of weeks for slower growth as days shorten.
[How many weeks before last frost to start seeds indoors](/blog/starting-seeds-indoors-weeks-before-frost/)
has the full schedule.

## Why broccoli buttons, and how to stop it

Buttoning is a small premature head on an undersized plant. It is the characteristic broccoli
failure and it is always a stress response.

The usual causes:

- **A cold snap** on young transplants, particularly below about 50°F for several days.
- **Root-bound seedlings** held too long in their cells.
- **Drying out**, even once.
- **Crowding**, which is the spacing question again.

A buttoned plant does not recover. Harden transplants off properly, plant at the right size, and
keep the water steady.

## Feeding and water

Broccoli is hungry and wants **1.5 inches of water a week** — more than most things in the bed.

Steady nitrogen through the season and unbroken moisture are what produce a large, tight head.
Stress at any point shows up in the head rather than in the leaves, which is why broccoli looks
fine right up until it disappoints.
[How often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the schedule; broccoli sits firmly in the steady-feeding group.

It wants **6 hours of sun** and soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.5**. Keep it at the upper end — clubroot is
much worse in acid soil, and once a bed has clubroot it has it for years.

## Harvesting

Cut when the head is full but the individual buds are still tight and dark green. The moment
yellow shows, the buds are opening and the texture goes.

Cut the main head with several inches of stem and leave the plant in. Most varieties then produce
side shoots for several weeks, which in total can outweigh the main head.

Expect **0.75 to 1.5 lb per plant**.

## What goes wrong

**Cabbage worms** are the constant. Fine mesh over the bed from planting day is more effective
than anything you spray. **Aphids** collect in the head where they are hard to wash out.
**Clubroot** is the reason for rotation and for keeping the pH up.

Broccoli follows peas or beans well, which leave nitrogen behind. Keep it away from tomatoes and
strawberries, and do not follow one brassica with another.

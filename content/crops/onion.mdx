Plant onions **4 inches apart in rows 12 inches apart**. In a square foot bed the square foot
gardening method puts nine to a square, which is the same spacing arranged as a grid. Closer
than four inches and the bulbs compete and stay small — crowding is the single commonest reason
home-grown onions disappoint.

## How far apart to plant onions

Four inches between plants, twelve inches between rows, for a full-size bulb.

If you want small onions for pickling or spring onions to pull young, sow much closer and thin
as you harvest. But for a storage onion, four inches is the figure, and it is worth measuring
rather than eyeballing. Onions do not push each other aside; they simply all stay small.

The square foot gardening method — Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension
recommendation — gives nine onions to a square, which works out to the same four-inch spacing on
a grid. [How does square foot gardening work](/blog/how-does-square-foot-gardening-work/) covers
where that method helps and where it gets in the way.

## When to plant onions

Onions are slow. From seed, start them indoors **12 weeks before your last frost** — earlier than
anything else in the garden — and set the transplants out about **2 weeks before** that date.
They tolerate a light frost, so going out early is fine.

Direct sowing is possible about two weeks before the last frost, but the season is tight in most
of the US and the bulbs end up smaller. Sets go in at the same time as transplants.

## Which variety, and why latitude decides it

This matters more for onions than for almost any other vegetable. Bulbing is triggered by day
length, not by temperature or by how long the plant has been growing.

| Type        | Daylight needed    | Suits                  |
| ----------- | ------------------ | ---------------------- |
| Long-day    | About 14–16 hours  | Northern US and Canada |
| Short-day   | About 10–12 hours  | Southern US            |
| Day-neutral | Around 12–14 hours | A wide middle band     |

A long-day onion grown in Texas grows leaves all season and never bulbs. A short-day onion grown
in Minnesota bulbs far too early and stays tiny. Check the type before you buy, not the picture
on the packet.

## Soil, sun and water

Onions want **8 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, and soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.0**.

Loose soil matters — a bulb has to push outwards as it swells, and it will not do that in
compacted ground. Onions are also shallow-rooted, so they dry out sooner than their neighbours
and compete poorly with weeds. Mulch between rows and weed by hand rather than hoeing deep.

## Feeding, and when to stop

Feed nitrogen early, while the plant is building leaves. Each leaf becomes a ring in the bulb, so
leaf growth in spring is what sets the eventual size.

Then stop. Once the bulbs begin to swell, further nitrogen produces a soft bulb with a thick neck
that will not keep.
[How often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the general schedule; onions are the clearest case on the site of a crop where feeding at
the wrong moment actively costs you the harvest.

## Harvest and curing

**90 to 120 days**, and roughly **0.3 to 0.7 lb per bulb**.

Wait until most tops have fallen over by themselves. Lift on a dry day, leave the tops on, and
cure somewhere warm and airy for two to three weeks until the necks are papery and the outer
skins rustle. Only then trim and store. An uncured onion rots; a cured one keeps for months.

## What goes wrong

**Thrips** rasp the leaves and leave silvery streaks, worst in hot dry spells. **Onion maggot**
is the reason not to plant onions where onions grew last year. **Downy mildew** follows wet
weather and crowded rows. **Splitting bulbs** usually trace back to irregular watering.

Keep onions away from peas, beans and asparagus. Carrots, beets, lettuce and tomatoes are
traditional neighbours — though companion pairings are garden tradition rather than settled
science, and [whether companion planting actually works](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/)
is worth reading before arranging a bed around them.

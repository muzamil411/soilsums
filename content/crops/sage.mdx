Most people searching for sage are not asking how to grow it. They are asking which one this is,
because four different plants are sold under the same word — culinary sage, the ornamental salvias,
Russian sage and white sage — and only one of the four belongs in a kitchen. The label is the only
reliable way to tell.

So this page starts there. Once you know which sage you have, the growing is straightforward and
mostly the same as [lavender](/crops/lavender/) and [rosemary](/crops/rosemary/): sun, sharp
drainage, and no feeding.

## The four things called sage

**Culinary sage — _Salvia officinalis_.** The kitchen herb. A low woody subshrub with soft,
pebbled, grey-green leaves and a strong savoury smell when you rub one. This is the only one of
the four grown to eat, and everything else on this page is about it.

Its named forms are all the same species and all edible: broad-leaved, purple or purpurascens,
golden, and the cream-and-pink tricolour. They differ in vigour and looks rather than in kind. The
plain grey-green one is the toughest and the most strongly flavoured; the variegated ones are
prettier and generally less hardy.

**Ornamental salvias — _Salvia_, many species.** Culinary sage's relatives, bred for flowers.
Some are hardy perennials, some are tender and treated as annuals, and they are sold on the same
bench under the same genus. They are not poisonous but they are not the cooking herb either, and
their leaves mostly taste of very little.

**Russian sage — _Salvia yangii_, long sold as _Perovskia_.** Tall, airy, grey-stemmed, with
clouds of small lavender-blue flowers. It looks more like a lavender than a sage from a distance,
which is where a good deal of the confusion starts. It is not a culinary herb. Botanists moved it
into _Salvia_ recently, which has made the naming worse rather than better.

**White sage — _Salvia apiana_.** A different species again, from the southwestern United States,
with white-grey leaves and a resinous smell. It is the plant burned in smudge bundles, not a
cooking herb, and it is under real pressure from wild collection, so buy cultivated stock if you
want one.

**The rule that gets you through a garden centre:** read the Latin name. _Salvia officinalis_ is
the kitchen herb. Anything else is a decision to look up rather than a substitute.

And one more overlap worth knowing, because it runs the other way: rosemary has been reclassified
as _Salvia rosmarinus_, so rosemary is now genuinely a sage in botanical terms while lavender —
which people confuse with sages constantly — is not a salvia at all. Our [lavender
guide](/crops/lavender/) has the salvia-versus-lavender comparison, and if you are unsure about one
of these plants you are usually unsure about the other.

## Blooming sage, and whether to allow it

Culinary sage flowers in early summer: spikes of purple-blue, extremely popular with bees. Whether
you want them is a real decision rather than a formality.

**If you grow sage to cook with,** cut the flower stems off as they appear. Flowering moves the
plant's energy from leaf to seed, growth slows, and the leaves that follow are coarser and less
useful. A plant kept from flowering stays leafier and more productive.

**If you grow sage for the border,** let it flower. It is one of the better pollinator plants in a
herb bed and the leaves are still perfectly usable, just not at their best.

You can have both by growing two plants and treating them differently, which costs nothing given
how long-lived they are.

## How to plant sage

Sage is planted from a container-grown plant or a rooted cutting, not sown. Seed is slow and
variable, and a seed-grown plant will not be the named form on the label — which is why the quick
facts table above shows no sowing dates at all, with the reason in each row.

UGA gives it as a **spring or fall planting** rather than a number of weeks relative to frost, so
that is what we quote. Spring is the safer of the two on heavy ground, for the same reason it is
with lavender: a new plant sitting through a wet winter before it has rooted properly is the one
most likely to rot.

The planting itself:

1. Pick the sunniest, most open spot you have, in free-draining soil.
2. Dig a hole no deeper than the rootball, so the plant sits level with the surface rather than in
   a dip where water collects.
3. Backfill with the soil you took out. Not compost, not manure.
4. Water once to settle it, then leave it to establish.

**Spacing is 18 inches**, from UGA's herb table. The [plant spacing
calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) turns that into plant counts for a bed. There is no
row figure: UGA's table gives none for any herb, and neither does Minnesota's herb page, so the
quick facts row says so rather than inventing one.

## Sun, soil and why not to feed it

**Full sun.** Six or more hours of direct sun, which the note under the quick facts table explains.
In part shade sage grows longer, softer and sparser, tastes of less, and is more prone to rot
because nothing dries out. It is not that it will die in shade — it is that you will not want to
cook with it.

**Free-draining soil, and poor is fine.** Wet ground in winter is what kills a woody herb, not
cold. On clay, plant on a mound or in a raised bed; [what to fill raised garden beds
with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/) covers the mix, and for sage lean it towards the
gritty end.

**Do not feed it.** Rich soil and nitrogen give a big soft plant with mild leaves, which is the
opposite of the point. This is the same trade-off as [lavender](/crops/lavender/), and it is worth
repeating because every instinct says to be generous.

No publication behind this page gives a pH range for sage specifically. UGA puts most herbs at
about 6 to 7.5 and Minnesota at 6.0 to 7.5, and the quick facts table reports it that way rather
than as a figure for this crop.

## Sage through the seasons

The keyword for this is "sage growing stages", and the honest version is short, because sage is a
perennial rather than a crop with a finish line.

**Year one** is establishment. The plant roots out, makes a modest amount of leaf, and you should
pick lightly or not at all. It may not flower.

**Years two to four** are the good ones. A full-sized plant, flowering each early summer, picked
whenever you want it. This is when an annual light prune matters most, because it is what stops
the woody base forming.

**Year four or five onwards** is decline. The base goes bare and brittle, the centre opens out, the
plant splays and the new growth sits at the ends of long woody stems. Sage will not reshoot from
that bare wood, so cutting into it does not rejuvenate the plant.

The answer is not to fight it. Take cuttings from a good plant in summer, grow the replacement on,
and swap them over. Sage roots readily from soft growth, and layering a low branch into the soil
works too.

**Picking:** take sprigs or individual leaves whenever you want them, through the whole season.
Sage is evergreen enough to pick in winter in mild weather, though growth stops and a plant should
not be stripped then. Flavour is strongest just before flowering.

## Sage companion plants

The useful test, as with every Mediterranean herb, is whether the neighbour wants the same
conditions. Sage needs sun, sharp drainage and poor soil, and it will be killed by the watering and
feeding a moisture-loving plant needs. That puts [rosemary](/crops/rosemary/),
[thyme](/crops/thyme/), [lavender](/crops/lavender/) and [oregano](/crops/oregano/) at the top of
the list — all four share its requirements closely enough that a bed of them can be managed as one
thing.

Among vegetables, sage is traditionally planted near cabbage and carrots, on the claim that its
scent confuses cabbage moth and carrot fly. The mechanism is plausible, the evidence is thin, and
there is no cost to trying it. [Does companion planting actually
work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) separates the well-supported mechanisms from
the repeated ones. What is not in doubt is that sage in flower is heavily worked by bees.

The traditional pairing to avoid is cucumber, which is usually explained by saying that cucumbers
pick up the flavour of aromatic neighbours. That is folklore rather than a finding. The real
argument against it is practical and better: a cucumber wants rich, constantly moist soil and a sage
wants neither, so whichever regime you follow, one of them suffers.

The soft herbs are the same case. [Basil](/crops/basil/), [dill](/crops/dill/),
[cilantro](/crops/cilantro/) and [parsley](/crops/parsley/) all want moisture and feeding, so they
belong in different ground rather than beside a sage.

The note under the companion table on this page is worth reading: these pairings are traditional
garden practice rather than settled science, and getting the drainage right will do far more for a
sage than its neighbours ever will.

## Common problems

**Woody and bare at the base.** Age. Prune lightly every year into leafy growth, and plan to
replace the plant rather than cut into bare wood.

**Mild, weak-tasting leaves.** Too rich, too well fed, or not enough sun.

**Collapsed over winter.** Rot from wet soil. Check where water sits in that spot before
replanting, and raise the next one.

**A white dusty coating on the leaves.** Powdery mildew, from still humid air. Space plants further
apart and improve air movement.

**Bought a "sage" that tastes of nothing.** Check the Latin name — it is very likely an ornamental
salvia rather than _Salvia officinalis_.

faqs:
  - question: >-
      Why do my zucchini rot at the blossom end?
    answer: >-
      Almost always incomplete pollination rather than disease. A fruit that was not fully
      pollinated starts to swell on the plant's reserves, then aborts and rots from the flower
      end. It is common early in the season and in wet weather when bees are not flying. Hand
      pollinating with a soft brush fixes it, and the problem usually resolves itself as the
      weather improves.
  - question: >-
      How many zucchini plants do I need?
    answer: >-
      One or two for most households, which sounds absurd until the first glut arrives. A single
      healthy plant gives six to twelve pounds of fruit over a season and keeps producing as
      long as you keep picking. Two plants is generous insurance against losing one to vine
      borer.
  - question: >-
      Why did my zucchini plant suddenly collapse?
    answer: >-
      Squash vine borer, if you are in its range. The larva tunnels inside the stem near the
      base, and the plant wilts and dies over a day or two even though the soil is moist. Look
      for a small hole with sawdust-like frass near ground level. It is far easier to prevent
      with covers over young plants than to treat once inside.
  - question: >-
      How big should I let zucchini get?
    answer: >-
      Six to eight inches is the sweet spot for flavour and texture. Anything larger gains water
      and seeds rather than taste, and a marrow left to grow tells the plant to slow down. Pick
      every day or two in high season; the difference between a perfect fruit and a baseball bat
      is about seventy-two hours.
  - question: >-
      Can I eat the flowers?
    answer: >-
      Yes, and it is a good way to use a plant that is over-producing. Male flowers, on thin
      plain stems, can be picked freely without costing you fruit. Female flowers have a tiny
      fruit behind them, so picking those is a choice between flower and vegetable. Pick in the
      morning while they are open.
  - question: >-
      Do zucchini grow in containers?
    answer: >-
      They can, in a very large one. A half barrel or a container of at least fifteen gallons,
      with a bush variety, will produce a worthwhile crop. Anything smaller dries out too fast
      for a plant with leaves that size. Expect to water daily and feed regularly.

This page has the numbers for zucchini: 24 inches between plants with 48 inches between
rows — about nine square-foot squares per plant — sown a week after your last frost or
started indoors three weeks before it, an inch and a half of water a week, and 6 to 12
pounds of fruit per plant over a 45 to 60 day season.

That space figure is the one people get wrong. A zucchini planted at one per square foot
smothers its neighbours and mildews from the lack of air. The other two things covered
below are the squash vine borer, which kills a plant in two days, and the blossom-end rot
that comes from incomplete pollination rather than disease.

## Space, honestly

A zucchini plant occupies far more room than its seed packet implies. Allow 24 inches between
plants and 48 inches between rows, and in square-foot terms give it about nine squares per
plant. Planted at one per square, as people often try, it smothers everything around it and
gets mildew from the lack of air.

Plant it at the edge of a bed, facing outwards, so it can spill over the frame rather than over
its neighbours.

## Sowing and planting

Direct sow about a week after your last frost date, once soil is genuinely warm, or start
indoors three weeks before and plant out two weeks after. Zucchini resents root disturbance, so
use a big cell and plant out early rather than letting it get pot-bound.

Two seeds per position, thinned to the stronger one, covers the usual germination losses.

A second sowing in early summer is worth considering. The first plants often succumb to mildew
or borer by midsummer, and a younger plant takes over just as the older one gives up.

## Water and food

An inch and a half a week, delivered to the soil rather than the leaves. Those enormous leaves
lose a lot of water on a hot day, and a zucchini that wilts every afternoon produces poorly.
Mulch generously.

Feed it well. Compost worked in before planting, then a balanced feed when the plant starts
running, and again mid-season. Unlike tomatoes, there is little risk of over-feeding into leaf
at the cost of fruit — zucchini does both enthusiastically.

## Pollination

Zucchini has separate male and female flowers, and both have to be open at once with something
to carry pollen between them. Early in the season a plant often produces only males, which is
normal and passes. Later, in wet or cool weather when bees are grounded, you may get female
flowers that shrivel and rot at the end. A soft brush moved from a male flower to a female one
takes ten seconds and solves it.

Female flowers are the ones with a miniature fruit behind them. Males sit on a plain stem.

## Picking

Every day or two, without exception, once the plant is going. A fruit at six to eight inches is
at its best; one left to become a marrow tastes of very little and signals the plant to stop.
Regular picking is the difference between a plant that produces for two months and one that
produces for three weeks.

Cut with a knife rather than twisting, and mind the small spines on the stems.

## Common trouble

**Squash vine borer** is the serious one where it occurs. Row covers over young plants until
flowering, and mounding soil over the lower stem so it can root along its length, both help.

**Powdery mildew** arrives late and is largely inevitable. Spacing and airflow delay it.

**Squash bugs** cluster at the base and on the undersides of leaves. Squashing the bronze egg
clusters by hand, early, is more effective than anything applied later.

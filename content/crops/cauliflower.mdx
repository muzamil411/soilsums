A loose, grainy curd — **ricey curd** — means the plant met heat or a check while it was making
its head. Cauliflower forms a curd only in cool, unbroken growing conditions, so the cause is
almost always timing rather than feeding. Autumn crops get it right far more often than spring
ones.

## What makes a curd go loose

The curd is an arrested flower head, and it holds together only while the plant is growing
steadily in cool weather. Warm spells push it on towards flowering, and the tight dome separates
into the grainy texture that gives ricey curd its name. A check — drying out, root disturbance at
transplanting, a stall in cold soil — does the same thing by interrupting growth at the wrong
moment.

Neither is reversible. Once a curd has started to separate it will not tighten again, so
everything useful happens before heading: rich soil, unbroken moisture, and a sowing date that
puts the heading weeks in cool weather.

**Grow it in autumn if you only grow it once.** Counting back from your first fall frost using
the maturity range, with a couple of weeks added for shortening days, gives a plant that sizes up
as conditions cool. That is precisely what cauliflower wants, and it is far more forgiving than a
spring crop racing the heat.

## When to start cauliflower seeds

| Step           | Timing                         |
| -------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Sow indoors    | 8 weeks before last frost      |
| Transplant out | 2 weeks before last frost      |
| Harvest        | 55–80 days after transplanting |

Going out two weeks before the frost date is deliberate. Cauliflower tolerates it, and the crop
depends on the plant reaching heading size while the weather is still cool.

Cauliflower is not usually direct sown — it needs an unbroken start that a seedbed rarely
provides.

## How far apart to plant cauliflower

**18 inches between plants, 30 inches between rows** — the same as broccoli.

The square foot gardening method — Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension
recommendation — gives one plant per square, which is considerably tighter than the row spacing
implies and only works in deep, rich, evenly watered ground.

Crowded cauliflower is smaller cauliflower. There is very little slack in this crop.

## Unbroken moisture is the whole game

Cauliflower wants **1.5 inches of water a week**, and it wants it without interruption.

Every characteristic cauliflower failure — buttoning, ricey curds, no head at all — traces back
to a check in growth. A single dry spell during head formation is enough. Unlike most crops,
cauliflower does not recover and carry on; it produces a poor head and stops.

So: mulch, water before the plant needs it rather than after, and do not rely on rain in a dry
month.

converts that 1.5 inches into gallons for your own bed.

## Soil and feeding

**6 hours of sun**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.5**.

Rich soil, worked with plenty of compost before planting, and steady nitrogen through the
season. Cauliflower is hungry, and half-feeding it produces exactly the small loose curd you were
trying to avoid.

Keep the pH at the upper end. Clubroot is markedly worse in acid soil and affects cauliflower as
it does any brassica.

## Blanching

White varieties need their curd shaded or it yellows and toughens.

When the curd is about the size of an egg, gather the outer leaves over it and tie them loosely
with soft string or a clothes peg. Check every few days — a curd can go from egg-sized to ready
in under a week in warm weather.

Self-blanching varieties fold their own leaves over. Purple, green and orange types are meant to
colour and should be left uncovered.

## Harvest

**55 to 80 days** from transplanting, at **1 to 2 lb per head**.

Cut while the curd is still tight and smooth. Once the segments start to separate the texture
goes. Cauliflower does not produce side shoots the way broccoli does, so the head is the crop and
the plant comes out afterwards.

## What goes wrong

**Ricey or loose curds** from heat or stress. **Yellowing from sun** without blanching. **Cabbage
worms**, which mesh from planting day deals with. **Boron deficiency**, which shows as brown
patches in the curd and a hollow stem, commoner on alkaline and sandy soils — confirm with a soil
test before adding any.

Onions, beets and dill are the traditional neighbours. Keep cauliflower away from tomatoes and
strawberries, and do not follow one brassica with another.

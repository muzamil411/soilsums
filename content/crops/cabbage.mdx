Start cabbage indoors **9 weeks before your last frost** and set transplants out **3 weeks
before** it — the longest indoor run of any brassica on this site. Space plants **15 inches apart
in rows 24 inches apart**. Heads take **60 to 90 days** from transplanting and weigh **2 to 4
lb**.

## When to plant cabbage

| Step           | Timing                         |
| -------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Sow indoors    | 9 weeks before last frost      |
| Transplant out | 3 weeks before last frost      |
| Harvest        | 60–90 days after transplanting |

Nine weeks is longer than broccoli or cauliflower need, and it is deliberate: cabbage is slower
to reach transplant size, and it has to make a head before summer heat arrives. Going out three
weeks ahead of the frost date is fine — young cabbage takes frost without complaint.

Cabbage is not usually direct sown in spring. Transplants get ahead of spring weeds and of the
heat that splits heads, which a seedbed rarely manages in time. For an autumn crop, direct sowing
in midsummer works well.
[How many weeks before last frost to start seeds indoors](/blog/starting-seeds-indoors-weeks-before-frost/)
has the schedule for everything else going in at the same time.

## How far apart to plant cabbage

**15 inches between plants, 24 inches between rows.**

Spacing has a direct effect on head size here, more than with most crops. Tighter spacing gives
smaller, denser heads; wider gives larger ones. Fifteen inches is a good middle figure for a
head you can actually finish eating.

The square foot gardening method — Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension
recommendation — gives one plant per square, which is tighter again and produces a smaller head.

## Water, and why it decides everything

Cabbage wants **1.5 inches of water a week**, and more importantly it wants it _steadily_.

Split heads are the characteristic cabbage failure, and they are almost always a watering
problem: the plant dries out, then gets a soaking, and the inside expands faster than the outer
leaves can stretch. The head cracks, and once cracked it will not keep.

If a head is ready and rain is coming, give the plant a quarter turn to snap some roots. That
slows uptake and buys a few days.

## Soil and feeding

**6 hours of sun**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.5**, and steady nitrogen for leaf growth — leaves are
the crop.

Keep the pH at the upper end of that range. Clubroot is much worse in acid soil, and once a bed
has it, it has it for years. If a soil test comes back low,
[when to apply lime to garden soil](/blog/when-to-apply-lime-to-garden-soil/) covers the timing,
and autumn is the right moment for a brassica bed.

## Harvest

Cut when the head feels solid under a squeeze. A head left past that point is where splitting and
bolting start.

Cut with a sharp knife, leaving the stem and outer leaves in place — most plants then produce
several small secondary heads around the cut, which are worth having.

## What goes wrong

**Cabbage worms** are constant through summer; mesh from planting day is the only approach that
is not a daily chore. **Root maggots** attack the stem at soil level. **Clubroot** is the reason
for rotation.

Onions, dill, beets and nasturtium are the traditional neighbours, and dill in particular earns
its place by flowering for the parasitic wasps that attack cabbage worms — one of the few
companion effects with real support, as
[does companion planting actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) explains.
Keep cabbage away from tomatoes and strawberries.

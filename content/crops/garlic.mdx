Plant garlic in **autumn**, about **1 to 2 weeks after your first killing frost**, with cloves
**4 inches apart in rows 12 inches apart** and the tip 2 inches deep. No last-frost offset
describes garlic — it goes in before winter and is lifted the following summer, taking **240 to
270 days** in the ground.

## When to plant garlic

One to two weeks after your first killing frost, a few weeks before the ground freezes hard.

That timing is deliberate. The clove needs long enough to put out roots before winter, but not so
long that it makes much top growth, which would be damaged by cold. Work backwards from your
**first fall frost date**, not your last spring one — garlic is the one crop on this site where
the spring frost date is no help at all.

Then it sits through winter. The cold period is not an inconvenience to be avoided; it is what
triggers the bulb to divide into cloves. Garlic planted in spring skips it and commonly produces
a single undivided round instead of a proper head.

## How to plant it

Break the head into cloves only at planting time — a broken head deteriorates quickly. Plant the
largest cloves and eat the small ones; clove size at planting predicts bulb size at harvest more
reliably than anything you do afterwards.

**Pointed end up**, about 2 inches deep, **4 inches apart in rows 12 inches apart**. The square
foot gardening method — Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension recommendation —
gives nine to a square, the same four-inch spacing on a grid.

Then mulch heavily. Four to six inches of straw or shredded leaves over the bed protects the
cloves from frost heave, which is the main overwinter risk, and suppresses the spring weeds that
shallow-rooted garlic competes with badly.
[Best mulch for a vegetable garden](/blog/best-mulch-for-a-vegetable-garden/) covers what to use.

## Feeding

Nitrogen in **early spring**, as the leaves start growing, then stop by the time scapes appear.

Each leaf becomes a wrapper layer around the bulb, so spring leaf growth is what sets the final
size. Feeding later, once the bulb is forming, gives a soft head that will not store — the same
pattern as [onions](/crops/onion/), and for the same reason.

**6 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.0**. Ease off water
in the last two or three weeks before harvest.

## Scapes

Hardneck varieties send up a curling flower stalk in early summer. Cut it off once it has made a
full curl. The plant then puts that energy into the bulb instead, and the scapes are good to eat.

Softneck varieties do not produce them.

## Harvest and curing

**When about a third to a half of the leaves have browned**, usually midsummer.

Each green leaf is a wrapper layer, so waiting until all the leaves die back gives you a bulb
with no skin left — it splits and will not store. Lifting too early gives an undersized bulb.
Dig rather than pull, and lift a test plant to check.

Cure for two to three weeks somewhere warm, dry and airy, tops still attached, out of direct sun.
Then trim and store. Expect **0.15 to 0.25 lb per bulb**.

## What goes wrong

**Small bulbs from late planting** is the commonest complaint, and it traces back to the autumn
date. **White rot** is a serious soil-borne disease that persists for years — rotate, and never
plant garlic where any allium showed it. **Rust** appears as orange pustules on the leaves late
in the season. **Splitting** follows harvesting too late.

Tomatoes, beets, carrots and strawberries are the traditional neighbours. Keep garlic away from
peas and beans.

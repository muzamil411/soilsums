  - question: >-
      Why does my spinach bolt so fast?
    answer: >-
      Day length as much as heat. Spinach is triggered into flowering by lengthening days, which
      is why a spring sowing can bolt while the weather is still cool. The fix is timing rather
      than care: sow early in spring and again from late summer, and skip the middle of the
      year. Varieties described as slow to bolt buy a couple of weeks, not a season.
  - question: >-
      Why are my spinach leaves small and yellowish?
    answer: >-
      Often soil pH. Spinach wants a near-neutral soil, around 6.5 to 7.5, and struggles in acid
      ground more visibly than most vegetables — pale, stunted plants that never bulk up. A soil
      test is worth doing before blaming feeding. Nitrogen shortage produces similar yellowing,
      but from the older leaves first.
  - question: >-
      Is spinach the same as chard or New Zealand spinach?
    answer: >-
      No, though they are used similarly. Chard is a beet relative and tolerates heat far
      better, so it carries on through summer when spinach has bolted. New Zealand spinach is
      unrelated again and also heat tolerant. Growing true spinach in the cool halves of the
      year and one of the others through summer gives leaves all season.
  - question: >-
      Can spinach survive winter?
    answer: >-
      In many climates, yes, and it is one of the best uses of a cold frame or a length of
      fleece. Sown in late summer, spinach grows slowly through autumn, sits out the cold, and
      then produces a heavy early crop as soon as days lengthen. Overwintered spinach is usually
      the best of the year.
  - question: >-
      How do I harvest spinach so it keeps producing?
    answer: >-
      Take the outer leaves and leave the growing point in the centre. The plant carries on
      producing from the middle for several cuttings. Cutting the whole plant an inch above the
      crown often gives a second flush, but the outer-leaf method gives a longer harvest from
      the same row.
  - question: >-
      Why is my spinach gritty?
    answer: >-
      Soil splashed onto crinkled leaves during watering or rain, which hides in the folds.
      Mulching the bed stops most of it, and watering the soil rather than over the top of the
      plants stops the rest. Savoy-leaved varieties hold more grit than the flatter-leaved ones.

Spinach is a cool-weather crop that will not be argued with. It grows quickly and well in
spring and autumn, and it bolts in summer no matter how carefully it is watered. Accepting that
is most of what there is to know.

## When to sow

Direct sow about six weeks before your last frost date — spinach germinates in cold soil that
most seed would rot in, and the seedlings take frost without harm. Sow again every two weeks
until about three weeks after the frost date, then stop.

Start again in late summer, roughly eight weeks before your first fall frost, for an autumn
crop. In many climates a late sowing will overwinter under fleece or a cold frame and produce
a heavy crop the following spring, which is often the best spinach of the year.

What does not work is sowing through midsummer. Lengthening days trigger flowering regardless
of temperature, and a plant that bolts turns bitter within days.

## Soil and pH

Spinach is fussier about pH than most vegetables. It wants near-neutral soil, roughly 6.5 to
7.5, and in acid ground it stays small and pale however well you feed it. If a bed grows
everything else well but produces poor spinach, test the pH before changing anything else.

It also wants nitrogen, being entirely leaf. Compost worked in before sowing, and a light feed
once the plants are established, suits it.

## Spacing

Four inches between plants with 12 inches between rows, or nine to a square foot. Thin early;
crowded spinach makes small plants that bolt sooner.

Sow half an inch deep and keep the surface damp until the seedlings appear, which takes a week
or so in cool soil.

## Water

An inch a week, consistently. Spinach is shallow rooted and mostly water, so it registers a dry
spell within days — and drought stress is one of the things that triggers bolting, alongside
day length.

Water the soil rather than the leaves. As well as reducing leaf spot, it keeps soil out of the
crinkles, which is where gritty spinach comes from. Mulching does the same job.

## Harvesting

Take outer leaves and leave the centre growing. A row picked this way will give several
harvests over a few weeks. Alternatively cut the whole plant an inch above the crown and many
varieties will produce a second, smaller flush.

Pick in the morning while the leaves are turgid. Spinach wilts fast once cut in the heat of the
day.

Once a plant starts to stretch upward and the leaves become pointed rather than rounded, it is
bolting. Harvest everything worth having immediately and clear the row — there is no way back.

## Common trouble

**Leaf miners** tunnel pale blotches between the leaf surfaces. Remove affected leaves; a fine
mesh over the row prevents the flies laying in the first place.

**Downy mildew** shows as yellow patches on top with grey fuzz beneath, in cool damp weather.
Space generously and avoid working among wet plants.

**Bolting** is the main thing that ends a crop, and it is a calendar problem rather than a
cultivation one.

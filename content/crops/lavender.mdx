This page has very few numbers on it, and that is the honest shape of the answer. Almost
everything that kills lavender is a condition rather than a measurement: the drainage, the
richness of the soil, the amount of sun, and whether anyone has pruned it.

Lavender is a woody Mediterranean subshrub, not a soft herb you sow each spring. Treat it like
basil and it dies. The three things below — sharp drainage, poor soil, annual pruning — are the
whole job, and the first two matter most in winter.

## Drainage comes first

**Winter wet kills far more lavender than winter cold does.** This is the single most useful fact
about the plant. A lavender sitting in ground that stays saturated from November to March rots at
the root and collapses, and the gardener blames the frost. The same variety in gritty, sharply
drained soil survives the same winter in the same garden.

So the question to ask about a planting spot is not how cold it gets. It is where the water goes
after heavy rain. If puddles sit on the surface for hours, that is not a lavender position.

What to do about heavy ground, in order of how well it works:

- **Plant on a mound or a slope** so water runs away from the crown rather than collecting round
  it. Raising the planting position a few inches is often enough on ground that is only slightly
  heavy.
- **Use a raised bed** filled with a free-draining mix. [What to fill raised garden beds
  with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/) covers the mix; for lavender, lean the
  recipe towards the coarse, gritty end and away from compost.
- **Use a pot**, which gives you complete control of the drainage. More on that below.

Digging grit into a planting hole in clay is the one approach that does not work. It makes a
sump: a pocket of free-draining material surrounded by clay, which fills with water and holds it
against the roots. Improve a whole bed or raise the plant, but do not dig a well for it.

## Poor soil on purpose

The second counterintuitive part: **do not feed lavender, and do not plant it in rich ground.**

Rich soil and nitrogen produce fast, soft, sappy growth. That growth carries more water in its
tissues, stays green later into autumn, never hardens off properly, and is the first thing to rot
or blacken in winter. A plant grown hard on poor ground makes short, tight, grey growth that
comes through cold and wet far better and flowers more heavily.

This is the same trade-off the marigold page describes, for the same reason, and it is worth
stating plainly because every instinct says otherwise: **the most common way to look after
lavender badly is to look after it too well.**

None of the publications behind this page gives a soil pH range for lavender, so the quick facts

alkaline ground, and it certainly does not want acid, waterlogged soil — but if your soil is very
acid, [how to raise soil pH](/blog/how-to-raise-soil-ph/) is the page for that, and the figures
there are sourced.

## Sun and position

Lavender wants the most open, sunniest spot in the garden, with air moving through it. Full sun
is not a preference here; it is part of the same system as the drainage. Sun and wind dry the
foliage and the surface of the soil, and a plant that dries out between rains is a plant that
hardens and survives.

In part shade, lavender does not die immediately. It grows longer, softer stems, leans towards
the light, flowers thinly, and then fails in its second or third winter. If the only spot you
have is shaded for half the day, a pot you can move is a better bet than the ground.

## Planting a lavender

Lavender is planted from a container-grown plant, not sown. Seed is slow, variable and will not
give you the named variety on the label, which is why almost nobody does it — and why the quick
facts table above shows no sowing dates at all, with the reason in each row.

Plant in spring once the ground is workable and hard frost has finished, so there is a full
season of root growth before the first winter. On heavy soil, spring is not just better than
autumn but a great deal better.

The planting itself is short:

1. Water the plant in its pot and let it drain while you dig.
2. Dig a hole no deeper than the rootball. Lavender should sit with the top of its rootball level
   with the soil surface or very slightly proud of it — never in a dip.
3. Backfill with the soil you took out, not with compost or manure. Firm it gently.
4. Water once, to settle the soil around the roots.
5. Then stop. A new lavender needs enough water to establish through its first summer and no
   more.

Spacing depends on the mature width of the variety, which is why the quick facts table gives a
range rather than a figure and marks it as an estimate — a compact English lavender and a big
grey-leaved one belong at different distances. Check the label for mature width and use that.
The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) will turn a spacing into plant
counts for a bed once you have one.

## Growing lavender in pots

A pot suits lavender well, and on clay it is often the best option available. The rules are the
same ones, made easier to follow:

- **Use a gritty, free-draining mix.** Ordinary multipurpose compost holds too much water. Adding
  a third of coarse grit or perlite by volume moves it in the right direction.
- **Check the drainage holes are actually open**, and stand the pot on feet or bricks so water
  leaves. Never leave a lavender standing in a full saucer, especially in winter.
- **Terracotta helps**, because it dries through its walls. A plastic pot works if the mix is
  coarse enough.
- **Do not over-pot.** A container much larger than the rootball holds a lot of wet mix the roots
  are not yet using.

The [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a given pot
takes, and the [raised bed soil calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) does the same for
a bed.

Potted lavender needs watering in summer — a pot in full sun dries fast, and that is normal
rather than a problem. The distinction to hold onto is between a pot that dries out between
waterings, which is right, and a pot that stays wet, which is fatal.

## Pruning, and why it cannot be undone

**Lavender does not reshoot from old wood.** Everything else in this section follows from that one
fact, and it is the second most common way people lose a plant.

Left unpruned, a lavender grows a little taller each year and leaves behind a lengthening stretch
of bare brown wood at the base. Eventually the plant is a woody frame with a thin layer of green
on top. The branches splay outwards under their own weight, the middle opens up, and it looks
dead in the centre. At that point it is finished: cutting back into bare wood to make it bush out
produces nothing, because there are no dormant buds there to shoot from.

Preventing it is easy and takes a few minutes a year. Prune every year, from the plant's first
year, and always cut into growth that still carries leaves — never into the bare brown wood
below. Take off the spent flower stems and shorten the soft growth behind them, aiming to keep a
rounded, tight mound rather than an open, leggy one.

A plant that has already split open cannot be rejuvenated. You can sometimes fill the gap by
pegging a low branch down to root, or take cuttings from the green growth and start again, but
the original plant will not come back. Replacing it is not a failure; it is the plant's habit.

## Salvia versus lavender

These two get confused constantly at the garden centre, and often enough for it to be a search
of its own. Both are in the mint family, both make spikes of purple-blue flowers, and both get
sold on the same bench in early summer.

The differences worth knowing:

- **Leaves and scent.** Lavender has narrow, grey-green, strongly perfumed leaves. Most
  ornamental salvias have broader, softer, mid-green leaves with a savoury, herbal smell rather
  than a perfumed one.
- **Habit.** Lavender is woody from the base up and keeps its frame through winter. Many salvias
  are softer and more herbaceous, and several are grown as tender plants replaced each year.
- **Flowering length.** Salvias generally flower for much longer through the season; lavender
  flowers heavily in a shorter window.
- **Soil.** Salvias are far more forgiving of decent, moisture-holding soil than lavender is.

Rosemary makes this worse rather than better: it has been reclassified as _Salvia rosmarinus_, so
rosemary genuinely is a salvia now, while lavender is not and never was. Our [rosemary
guide](/crops/rosemary/) covers that plant.

## What a "lavender tree" actually is

A lavender tree is not a species or a variety. It is an ordinary lavender — very often a tender
one — trained as a standard: a single clear stem with a rounded head of growth on top, produced by
rubbing out the side shoots as the plant grows.

Everything on this page still applies: the same drainage, the same poor soil, the same annual
pruning of the head. What changes is exposure. The head sits up on a thin stem where the wind
gets at it, the pot is usually small relative to the plant, and it dries out and blows over more
readily. Stake the stem, give it some shelter, and expect to water it more often than a plant in
the ground. Many standards sold this way are tender varieties that need protecting over winter —
check the label before leaving one out.

## Lavender leaves: what they tell you

The leaves are the first place trouble shows, and they are searched for on their own.

**Grey-green and narrow** is healthy. Lavender leaves are supposed to look silvery and dry rather
than lush.

**Soft, large and bright green** means the plant is being fed or grown too richly, or has too
little light. Expect thin flowering and poor winter survival.

**Blackening, or browning from the base upwards, with soft stems** is usually rot from wet soil.
By the time the foliage shows it the roots are normally gone.

**Grey, dry, brittle stems in the centre of an older plant** is the woody-base problem in the
pruning section above, not a disease.

## Lavender companion plants

Lavender's real companions are plants that want the same conditions, which is a more useful test
than any traditional pairing list. Anything that needs the sharp drainage, full sun and poor soil
lavender needs will thrive beside it; anything that wants rich, moist ground will either struggle
or, worse, get watered and fed in a way that kills the lavender.

That puts the other woody Mediterranean herbs at the top of the list — [rosemary](/crops/rosemary/),
thyme and sage all share lavender's requirements closely enough to be planted together with no
compromise. Soft herbs are the opposite case: [basil](/crops/basil/) wants warmth, moisture and
regular feeding, so it belongs in a different bed or a different pot rather than next to a
lavender.

Lavender in flower is heavily worked by bees and other pollinators, which is the usual reason it
is planted near a vegetable garden. That is a real effect and an easy one to observe. Claims that
it repels particular pests from neighbouring crops are the weaker end of companion planting — the
pollinator traffic is the part you can rely on.

The quick facts table above lists our companion and keep-apart entries, and the note under it is
worth reading: companion planting is traditional practice rather than settled science, and
getting the drainage right will do far more for a lavender than its neighbours ever will.

## Common problems

**The plant collapsed over winter.** Almost always rot from wet soil rather than cold. Check where
water sits in that spot before replanting, and raise the next one.

**Lots of leaf, few flowers.** Too rich, too well fed, or not enough sun. Stop feeding entirely;
the plant will not need it.

**Bare and woody at the base, open in the middle.** Years without pruning. Not fixable — see the
pruning section.

**Nothing came from the cutting.** Lavender roots readily from soft growth in summer, but cuttings
rot in a mix that holds water. The same rule as the parent plant applies.

**It died in a pot.** Look at the saucer and the drainage holes first, before anything else.

Parsley seed takes **three to four weeks** to germinate because its seed coat holds compounds
that inhibit sprouting until they leach out. That is normal, not a failure. It is also why
parsley goes in **8 weeks before your last frost** — half that run is germination alone.

## Why germination takes three to four weeks

The seed coat contains germination inhibitors, chiefly furanocoumarins, which have to wash out
before the embryo will start. Water moving through the coat does that slowly, and nothing about
a warm sill speeds it up much. A tray showing nothing after a fortnight is a tray working
normally.

Two things help at the margins. Soaking the seed overnight in warm water starts the leaching
early, and keeping the surface evenly damp — never dry, never sodden — keeps it going. Sowing
into cold compost does the opposite.

## When to start parsley seeds

Eight weeks before your last frost, which is the same as peppers and eggplant. Set plants out
**on the frost date**; parsley can also be direct sown then. Space it **10 inches apart in rows
12 inches apart**.

That looks generous for a herb, and it is not. Eight weeks indoors is really four weeks of
germination and four of growth. Sowing later means setting out seedlings that are barely
established.

| Route          | Timing                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| Sow indoors    | 8 weeks before last frost |
| Transplant out | On the last frost date    |
| Direct sow     | On the last frost date    |

Parsley transplants better than most taprooted herbs, so either route works.

has the schedule for everything going in alongside it.

## What a slow tray looks like

Nothing, for a fortnight, and then a scatter of thin seed leaves that come up over another week
rather than all at once. Uneven emergence is normal here — the inhibitors do not leach out of
every seed at the same rate.

A tray that dries out once in week two has to start again, so keep the surface consistently damp
for the whole period. Label it and be patient: parsley trays are thrown out early more often
than any other.

## How far apart to plant parsley

**10 inches between plants, 12 inches between rows.**

The Cornell square foot gardening page does not name parsley, so there is no square foot
gardening figure for it. The spacing-derived density is about 1.4 plants per square foot, which
is arithmetic rather than a recommendation.

The row spacing here is one of the figures the verification report could not confirm against an
extension source, so the quick facts table marks it as an estimate. Twelve inches is a
conventional figure and works in practice; treat it as a starting point rather than a
requirement.

## Soil, sun and water

**5 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.0**.

Parsley is content in partial shade and tolerates a wider range of conditions than most herbs. It
has a long taproot, so a deep pot or a well-worked bed suits it better than a shallow one.

Light nitrogen through the season keeps new leaves coming, the same pattern as any crop where
leaves are the harvest.

## Parsley is biennial

This is the thing most often mistaken for a problem. Parsley makes leaves in its first year,
overwinters, then flowers, sets seed and dies in its second.

So a plant that bolts in its second spring is behaving normally. Sow fresh each year, and let one
second-year plant flower if you want seed or the hoverflies that its small open flowers attract.

## Harvest

**70 to 90 days** to a usable plant, at roughly **0.25 to 0.5 lb** over a season.

Cut outer stems at the base and leave the centre to keep producing. Picking leaf by leaf from the
top slows the plant down; taking whole outer stems does not.

Parsley survives light frost and often keeps producing well into autumn, longer than
[cilantro](/crops/cilantro/) or [basil](/crops/basil/) manage.

## What goes wrong

**Very slow germination** is the main one, and it is not a fault. **Carrot rust fly** attacks
parsley as it does carrots — they are the same family. **Leaf spot** follows wet crowded
conditions.

Tomatoes, carrots, chives and corn are the traditional neighbours. Keep parsley away from
lettuce.

Parsley seed is the slowest of any herb on this site, and that single fact explains most of what
people search for about it. Two to four weeks to come up, unevenly, is normal — not a failure,
not bad seed, and not something you did wrong.

The quick facts table above has the spacing and sowing dates. This page covers the two things it
cannot: why the seed behaves like that, and why a second-year plant runs to flower instead of
making leaves.

## Why parsley seed is slow

The seed coat carries germination inhibitors — compounds that have to break down or be washed out
before the seed will start growing at all. Warmth and moisture on their own do not override them;
they only start the clock once the inhibitors have gone.

Two things follow from that, and both are practical:

- **Germination is slow and staggered**, because the inhibitors do not break down at the same rate
  in every seed. A tray that has produced three seedlings after two weeks is not a failed tray. It
  is a tray in the middle of germinating.
- **The surface has to stay damp for the whole wait**, which is much harder over several weeks than
  over several days. This is the real reason most parsley sowings fail: not the seed, but a dry
  afternoon somewhere in week two.

Parsley seed is also relatively short-lived. Seed kept for several years germinates poorly on top
of being slow, which is a bad combination — if a sowing genuinely produces nothing after a month,
old seed is the first thing to suspect.

## Sowing parsley seeds

Sow into damp, fine soil, barely covering the seed, and then keep that surface damp until the
seedlings appear. A board or a sheet of fleece laid over the row holds moisture and makes the
difference; check daily and water gently with a fine rose rather than a heavy splash that moves
the seed.

Parsley can be started indoors or direct sown, and the quick facts table above gives both dates.
Unlike its relatives [dill](/crops/dill/) and cilantro, parsley transplants reasonably well if it
is moved young, before the taproot has run down — so a tray on a warm windowsill is a genuine
option, and it makes the weeks of damp surface much easier to manage than a row outdoors.

[Starting seeds indoors weeks before frost](/blog/starting-seeds-indoors-weeks-before-frost/)
covers the indoor schedule across crops, and the [planting date
calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) turns your frost date into a sowing date.

**Speeding it up.** Three things help, in descending order of usefulness:

1. **Steady warmth and constant moisture.** Not glamorous, and by far the most effective.
2. **An overnight soak in warm water** before sowing, which starts washing the inhibitors out of
   the seed coat. Expect a few days, not a few weeks.
3. **Fresh seed.** New seed germinates better than old, which matters more here than for most
   crops.

What does not help is sowing deeper, sowing more thickly, or giving up and resowing after ten
days — which usually just restarts the same wait.

## Growing on

Thin or plant out to the spacing in the table above; the [plant spacing
calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) turns that into plant counts for a bed. Parsley
does not need much: reasonable soil, steady moisture, and light feeding through the season rather
than a heavy dose. [How often should you fertilize
vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/) covers the general pattern.

None of the publications behind this page puts a weekly watering figure on parsley, so the quick
facts table says so rather than printing one. In practice a settled plant is far less fussy than
the seedbed was — it is the weeks of germination that demand attention, not the plant afterwards.

Pick from the outside in, taking whole outer stalks at the base rather than snipping leaf tips.
The plant grows from the centre, so working inwards keeps it producing; cutting the middle out
stops it.

**In a pot**, depth matters more than width because of the taproot — aim for at least eight inches
of soil depth. Sow directly into the final pot where you can, and keep the surface damp through the
long germination. A deep pot on a bright sill also works indoors over winter, with slower growth
than outdoors.

## The second year: bolting is not a failure

Parsley is a **biennial**. It spends its first year making leaves, overwinters, and then in its
second year sends up a flower stem, sets seed and dies. That is the plant's complete and normal
life, not a mistake you made.

Knowing that changes what you do:

- **Sow fresh every year.** A continuous supply of leaf comes from having a first-year plant
  going at all times, not from keeping one plant alive. Overlapping sowings a year apart is the
  whole technique.
- **Do not fight the flower stem.** Pinching it out buys very little; the plant has switched.
  Second-year leaves also turn coarser and stronger-tasting before the stem even appears.
- **Let it flower anyway.** Parsley's flowers are small, open umbels, the same shape that makes
  [dill](/crops/dill/) so useful, and they feed the parasitic wasps and hoverflies whose larvae
  eat aphids and caterpillars. A bolting second-year plant left in a corner is doing real work.
- **Collect the seed if you want to.** It will be slow to germinate next spring, exactly like the
  packet.

Early bolting in the first year is a different thing, and usually means the plant was stressed —
transplanted too late with the taproot already long, or dried out badly.

## Parsley companion plants

Tomatoes, corn and chives are the traditional pairings, and parsley is undemanding enough to sit
beside most things without conflict. It tolerates more shade than the woody herbs do, which makes
it useful at the foot of taller crops where lavender or rosemary would sulk.

The genuinely useful pairing is the one the second year gives you: a parsley left to flower is an
insect plant, and that is a mechanism with real support behind it rather than folklore. [Does
companion planting actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) covers which of
these claims stand up.

The pairing to think about is **carrot**. Parsley is a close relative, so the two share pests —
carrot rust fly among them — and planting them together concentrates the problem. Our [carrot
guide](/crops/carrot/) covers that pest. The traditional advice to keep parsley away from lettuce
is much weaker, and we have kept it in the table above as tradition rather than as an
evidence-based claim.

The note under the companion table on this page is worth reading: these pairings are traditional
garden practice rather than settled science, and keeping the seedbed damp will do more for your
parsley than any neighbour.

## Common problems

**Nothing came up.** Almost always the surface dried out during the weeks of germination, or the
seed was old. Wait a full month before writing a sowing off.

**It came up very patchily.** Normal. Parsley germinates unevenly by nature, and the stragglers
catch up.

**It flowered in its second spring.** It is a biennial. See above — that is the plant finishing
its life, not failing.

**Leaves eaten down to the stems.** Black swallowtail caterpillars, most likely. Boldly striped
and easy to spot, and the plant usually regrows.

**Yellowing lower leaves, stunted plants.** Check for carrot rust fly at the roots, especially if
carrots are nearby.

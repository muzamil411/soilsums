Direct sow cilantro **2 weeks before your last frost**, then **every two or three weeks**
through the season. Space plants **6 inches apart in rows 12 inches apart**. Cilantro does not
transplant — it bolts when its root is disturbed — and it bolts in heat anyway, so a supply
comes from sowing often rather than tending one patch.

## When to plant cilantro

Two weeks before your last frost for the first sowing. It takes light frost, so there is no need
to wait.

Then keep sowing. **A short row every two or three weeks** is the whole technique, and it is more
important for cilantro than for any other herb. Each sowing gives you a few weeks of leaves
before it flowers, and the only way to have cilantro in July is to have sown some in June.

The gap worth skipping is the hottest part of summer, when plants bolt almost immediately.
Resume in late summer for an autumn run, which is often the best cilantro of the year.
[Succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers building that rhythm
around your frost dates.

## Why it will not transplant

Cilantro has a taproot, and moving it triggers bolting almost at once.

This is why it is direct sown, why supermarket pot-grown plants so rarely establish in a garden,
and why starting it indoors is a waste of a windowsill. Sow where it will grow.

It is also why a bolted plant cannot be rescued. Once the switch has flipped, the leaf shape
changes to fine and feathery and the flavour goes with it.

## How far apart to plant cilantro

**6 inches between plants, 12 inches between rows** — four plants to a square foot on the
spacing-derived figure.

That four-per-square number is arithmetic: a plant every six inches is four to the square however
you arrange them. The Cornell square foot gardening page does not name cilantro, so there is no
separate square foot gardening figure for it.
[How does square foot gardening work](/blog/how-does-square-foot-gardening-work/) explains the
difference between the two kinds of number.

In practice many gardeners sow cilantro as a thick band rather than spaced plants, cutting the
whole patch young like a salad leaf. That works well, and it is a different crop from growing
individual plants on for larger leaves.

## Soil, sun and water

Cilantro wants only **4 hours of sun** — one of the lowest figures on the site — about **an inch
of water a week**, and soil at **pH 6.2 to 6.8**.

Some afternoon shade in summer genuinely delays bolting, so a spot that gets morning sun and
shade from two o'clock is better for cilantro than the sunniest bed you have.

Feed it almost nothing. Rich soil produces soft growth without extending the leafy phase.

## Harvest

**45 to 70 days** to a full plant, and roughly **0.1 to 0.25 lb of leaf** from each — cilantro is
a garnish crop, not a bulk one, and is grown by the row.

Cut outer leaves at the base, or take the whole plant when the next sowing is ready to replace
it. Once the fine feathery leaves appear the plant has switched to flowering.

Let a late sowing run to seed and you get coriander: collect the seed heads when brown and dry.
The plant also self-seeds readily, which is a reliable way to get a free spring crop.

## What goes wrong

**Bolting in heat** is the defining problem and the reason for the sowing schedule. **Damping
off** takes seedlings in cold wet soil, so do not sow too early or too thickly. **Aphids** appear
on flowering stems.

Tomatoes, spinach and peppers are the traditional neighbours, and letting some cilantro flower is
genuinely useful — the small open flowers bring in hoverflies and parasitic wasps whose larvae eat
aphids, which is one of the better-supported companion effects. Keep it away from fennel.

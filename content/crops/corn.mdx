Plant sweet corn **10 inches apart in rows 30 inches apart**, and plant a **block of at least
four short rows** rather than one long one. Sow **1 week after your last frost**, and only once
soil reaches **60°F (16°C)** — or **65°F (18°C)** for supersweet varieties, which rot more
readily in cold ground.

## How far apart to plant corn

Ten inches between plants, thirty inches between rows.

The row spacing is not about the plant's width — corn is narrow — but about light and access.
Closer rows shade each other, and a block of corn is hard to walk into once it is head height.

The shape of the planting matters more than either number, which is the next section.

## Plant a block, not a row

Corn is wind-pollinated. Pollen falls from the tassel at the top of the plant onto the silks
below, and **every silk that catches a grain becomes one kernel**. A silk that misses leaves a
gap.

A single row, however long, loses most of its pollen sideways. A block gives the pollen somewhere
to land.

| Planting       | Result                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------ |
| One row of 16  | Patchy, half-filled cobs |
| Four rows of 4 | Well-filled cobs         |

Four rows deep is the practical minimum, and more is better. This is also why corn is a poor
choice for a small garden: sixteen plants at ten-inch spacing take about 25 square feet before
you have eaten anything.
[How to plan a vegetable garden layout](/blog/planning-a-vegetable-garden-layout/) covers where a
block like that fits, and the shading it casts.

## When to plant corn

One week after your last frost, **and** once the soil is warm enough.

| Variety type        | Soil temperature  |
| ------------------- | ----------------- |
| Standard sweet corn | About 60°F (16°C) |
| Supersweet          | About 65°F (18°C) |

Supersweet varieties have a shrivelled seed with less stored starch, which makes them far more
likely to rot before germinating in cold ground. Waiting the extra week is cheaper than resowing.

Corn is not started indoors — it resents root disturbance and catches up quickly outdoors.

## Sun, water and feeding

Corn wants **8 hours of sun** and **1.5 inches of water a week**, and it is a heavy nitrogen
feeder.

Side-dress with nitrogen when the plants are knee high, and again at tasselling. Those two
moments are when the crop is building the structure and then filling the cobs, and skimping at
either shows up directly in the harvest.

Water matters most from tasselling through to filling. A dry spell in that window produces
small cobs with poor kernel set, and it cannot be made up later.
[How much water does a vegetable garden need per week](/blog/how-much-to-water-a-vegetable-garden/)
converts that 1.5 inches into gallons for your own bed.

Soil at **pH 5.8 to 7.0** suits it.

## Keep varieties apart

Supersweet corn crossed with a standard variety produces starchy, disappointing kernels. If you
grow both, separate them by a few hundred feet or stagger sowing so they tassel two weeks apart.

## Harvest

**60 to 95 days**, and roughly **0.4 to 0.8 lb of kernels per plant** — usually one good cob,
sometimes two.

About three weeks after the silks appear, they turn brown and dry. Peel back a strip of husk and
press a kernel with a thumbnail: milky juice means pick now, clear means wait, doughy means it is
past its best. Sweetness starts converting to starch within hours of picking, so the gap between
the garden and the pot is worth minimising.

## What goes wrong

**Corn earworm** enters through the silks and eats down into the tip. **Raccoons** know precisely
when corn is ready. **Smut** produces grey galls on the cob — remove and bin them, do not compost.

Corn grows well with beans and squash, the traditional Three Sisters planting, which is one of
the few companion systems with clear mechanisms behind it. Keep it away from tomatoes.

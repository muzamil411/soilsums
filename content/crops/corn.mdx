Plant sweet corn **1 to 2 weeks after your last frost**, **10 inches apart in rows 30 inches
apart**, and harvest in **60 to 95 days**. Sow **1 to 2 inches deep** and always in a **block of
at least four rows**, never a single row — corn is wind-pollinated and a row gives ears with
missing kernels.

## Plant in a block, not a row

This is the single most important thing on the page, and it is why so many first corn crops
produce disappointing ears.

**Corn is wind-pollinated.** The tassel at the top of the plant sheds pollen, which falls onto
the silks emerging from the developing ears below. **Every silk that catches a pollen grain
becomes one kernel.** A silk that catches nothing leaves a gap.

In a single long row, most pollen blows sideways and away. The plants at the downwind end get
some; much of it lands on bare ground. The result is the familiar ear with kernels scattered
across it like a bad chessboard.

**Plant a block at least four rows deep instead** — four by four is a sensible minimum for a home
garden, and more is better. Pollen shed from any plant then lands among its neighbours. A short,
wide block beats a long, narrow one every time, even with the same number of plants.

If you have only room for a few plants, hand-pollinating is a real option: shake the tassels over
the silks on a dry morning when pollen is shedding.

## Sweet corn companion plants

**Good neighbours, and why.**

**Beans** are the classic partner and one of the few with mechanisms in both directions. Climbing
beans use corn stalks as support, and legumes fix nitrogen, which a heavy-feeding crop like corn
appreciates — though most of that nitrogen becomes available after the beans break down rather
than during the season.

**Squash** completes the Three Sisters. Its broad leaves shade the ground between corn stalks,
suppressing weeds and holding moisture, and the prickly stems are said to deter raccoons. The
shading effect is genuine; the raccoon claim is folklore.

**Cucumbers** work on the same principle as squash, occupying ground level under a tall crop.
**Sunflowers** attract pollinators and predatory insects, though they compete for light if
planted too close.

**Bad neighbours, and why.**

**Tomatoes.** Two arguments, both reasonable. They are both heavy nitrogen feeders competing
directly, and corn's height shades a tomato badly. They also share a pest: the corn earworm and
the tomato fruitworm are the same insect, so growing both together supports a larger population
of it.

[Whether companion planting actually works](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) sorts
the mechanisms above from the folklore.

## Spacing and sowing depth

**10 inches between plants, 30 inches between rows**, arranged as a block.

**Sow 1 to 2 inches deep** — the shallower end in cool spring soil, the deeper end in warm or dry
conditions. Corn needs firm contact with moist soil to germinate evenly, and a shallow sowing
into ground that then dries out gives a patchy stand. Deeper than two inches and the seedling
uses too much of its reserve reaching the light.

The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) will work out how many plants a
given block holds.

## When to plant

**1 to 2 weeks after your last frost**, and only once the soil has warmed. Corn is frost-tender
and germinates badly in cold ground.

**Soil temperature is the condition that matters.** Above about 60°F, corn sprouts in 7 to 10
days. Below it, germination stretches to three weeks or more and a large proportion of the seed
simply rots. Sowing early to gain a week routinely loses two.

Supersweet varieties are fussier still and want warmer soil than standard sweet corn. The
[planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) gives the frost-relative date; check
the soil alongside it.

**Successional sowing** every two to three weeks extends a harvest that is otherwise
uncomfortably concentrated — a block of corn tends to be ready all at once.

## Growing in containers

Possible, and rarely satisfying. Corn is tall, wind-pollinated and a heavy feeder, so it wants a
block rather than a pot.

If you try it, use a large container — **at least 12 inches deep** — and grow several plants
grouped together rather than one per pot, so pollination has a chance. Expect smaller ears than
open ground.

## Watering and feeding

Corn is thirsty and a heavy feeder. Water about **an inch a week**, and more during tasselling
and silking, which is the critical window — drought stress then directly costs you kernels.

**Nitrogen matters here in a way it does not for most crops.** Corn is one of the few garden
vegetables that genuinely responds to a generous nitrogen supply, and a side-dressing when the
plants are knee-high pays off. [How to add nitrogen to soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/)
covers the sources and how fast each releases.

**Do not remove the side shoots** at the base. The old advice to strip them is wrong; they
contribute to the plant and removing them wounds it.

## Harvest

**60 to 95 days**, and the window is short — a few days at best.

Three signals together: the **silks have turned brown and dry**, the **ear feels full and rounded
through the husk**, and a **kernel pierced with a thumbnail releases milky liquid**. Clear liquid
means it is too early; doughy means too late.

**Pick immediately before cooking.** Sweet corn converts sugar to starch within hours of picking,
and that conversion is the entire difference between home-grown and shop-bought.

## Common problems

**Ears with missing kernels.** Poor pollination, almost always from planting in a row rather than
a block. Not correctable once the ear has formed.

**Patchy germination.** Cold soil, or sowing too shallow into ground that dried out.

**Corn earworm in the ear tip.** Common and usually confined to the top inch or two, which can
simply be cut off. A few drops of oil on the silks after pollination reduces it.

**Plants blown over.** Corn is top-heavy. Earthing up soil around the base as the plants grow
encourages prop roots and helps considerably.

Corn grows well with [beans](/crops/bean/) and [squash](/crops/squash/) in the traditional
grouping, and should be kept away from [tomatoes](/crops/tomato/).

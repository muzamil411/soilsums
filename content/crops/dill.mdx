This page has the numbers for dill in the table above: spacing in and between rows, direct sown
on your last frost date, and a week or three to germinate. What the numbers do not carry is the
two things that actually decide how dill goes for you.

The first is that it will not transplant, so it has to be sown where it will grow. The second is
that leaf, flower head and seed are three different harvests, weeks apart, and most advice
treats them as one.

## Dill companion plants

Dill is planted beside more vegetables than almost any other herb, and unusually for companion
planting there is a real mechanism behind the main claim rather than folklore.

**The mechanism is the flowers.** Dill's flower head is an umbel — dozens of tiny, shallow,
open flowers on a flat plate. That shape is accessible to small beneficial insects that cannot
feed from deeper flowers: parasitic wasps, hoverflies, lacewings and tachinid flies. Those
insects lay eggs in or near aphids and caterpillars, and their larvae do the work. Attracting
beneficial insects is the best-supported of the companion planting mechanisms, and dill is one of
the plants most often named for it.

Two consequences follow, and both are usually missed:

- **The benefit comes from dill in flower**, not dill as a leafy herb. A patch kept pinched back
  for kitchen use feeds nobody. If you want the pest control, let some plants flower.
- **It is a garden-wide effect, not a next-door one.** The wasps are not confined to the
  neighbouring plant, so dill anywhere near the vegetables works. It does not need to be
  interplanted in the same row.

**Best neighbours.** Cabbage and the rest of the brassicas gain the most, because cabbage worms
and aphids are exactly what those wasps attack. Cucumbers, lettuce and onions are easy
neighbours with no conflict in their requirements. Dill's own needs are undemanding — sun, and
soil that is not rich — so it fits beside most things.

**Keep it away from carrots.** This is the one avoid-pairing worth taking seriously. Dill and
carrot are in the same family, close enough that the two can cross if both are left to flower,
which matters if you save carrot seed. They also share pests, so planting them together
concentrates a problem rather than dividing it. Our [carrot guide](/crops/carrot/) covers those
pests.

**Tomato is the traditional second avoid**, on much weaker evidence. The claim is that mature
dill inhibits tomato growth. It is repeated everywhere and tested almost nowhere. [Does companion
planting actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) goes through which of these
claims hold up and which do not.

One practical caution that is not about companions at all: dill grows tall on hollow stems and
blows over. Put it where it will not fall across a shorter crop, or where neighbouring plants
hold each other up.

## Sowing dill seed

**Direct sow, always.** Dill has a taproot that resents disturbance, and a moved plant either
collapses or bolts straight to flower without making leaves. This is the single most common
reason a garden-centre pot of dill fails in a bed, and it is why the quick facts table above
shows no indoor sowing or transplanting dates, with the reason in each row instead.

Sow on your last frost date and then again every few weeks. Dill runs to flower quickly in warm
weather, so a single sowing gives you a short leaf harvest and then a flowering plant. A short row
every two or three weeks is what keeps leaves coming; the [succession planting
schedule](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers that pattern across crops, and the
[planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) will work the date out from your
frost date.

Sow shallowly — the seed needs barely covering — and keep the surface damp until the seedlings
appear. Thin to the spacing in the table above rather than leaving a dense stand, which produces
weak plants that flop sooner. The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/)
turns that spacing into plant counts for a bed.

Dill will grow in a pot, but it needs depth for the taproot rather than width, and a tall plant in
a light pot blows over. Sow a few seeds directly into the final pot rather than transplanting
seedlings into it.

## Soil, water and feeding

Poor to average soil suits dill. Rich ground and a nitrogen feed give tall, soft, leafy plants
that flop and are more attractive to aphids.

Water is the one place a schedule genuinely does not help. Keep the seedbed damp through
germination, because the seed is sown shallow and a dry afternoon can lose the row. After that,
water in dry spells rather than at fixed intervals: a dry check is what triggers early bolting,
and early bolting is what cuts the leaf harvest short. None of the publications behind this page
puts a weekly figure on it, so the quick facts table says so rather than inventing one.

## Harvesting dill: three harvests, not one

This is where dill differs from every soft herb, and where most advice collapses three things
into one instruction. The plant gives you three distinct crops at three distinct times, and they
trade off against each other.

### Leaf

The first harvest, and the shortest window. Take leaves from young plants before any flower stem
appears, because flowering changes both the amount of leaf and its flavour.

Pick whole outer leaves and side shoots with a short length of stem, working from the outside of
the plant inwards, and leave the growing tip. Stripping individual leaflets off does more damage
for less return. Cutting a whole young plant off a couple of inches above the soil will often
give one more flush of growth.

Once a flower stem rises, the leaf harvest is effectively finished. Pinching that stem out as
soon as you see it buys a week or two, but not a season — the plant is an annual and flowering is
where it is going.

Dill loses its flavour quickly once cut, and more quickly again when dried. It freezes far better
than it dries.

### Flower head

The middle harvest, and the one pickling recipes are actually asking for when they say "dill
heads". Cut the head when it is fully open and still green, before any seed has started to harden
and colour. A green head carries the leafy, aromatic flavour; a dry one carries the sharper
flavour of the seed.

Cut the head with a good length of stem — the stem is aromatic too and goes into the jar with it.

### Seed

The last harvest. Leave heads on the plant and let them go from green to tan, and the seeds from
soft and pale to hard and brown. The seed ripens unevenly across a head, so there is a judgement
call: too early and the seed is soft and poor-flavoured, too late and the head shatters and sows
your bed for you.

The standard method handles the unevenness. Cut the heads with a long stem once most of the seed
has coloured, put them head-down in a paper bag, and hang the bag somewhere dry and airy. The
seed finishes ripening and falls into the bag over a week or two, and nothing is lost to
shattering. Rub the heads gently to release the rest, sieve out the debris, and dry the seed fully
before storing it.

Seed saved this way sows next year's dill, which the plant will otherwise do for you.

## Self-seeding, and what to do about it

Dill self-seeds enthusiastically. A single plant allowed to ripen and shatter its heads will give
you dill in that bed the following year, and usually for years after.

Whether that is a feature or a problem depends entirely on the gardener. In an informal bed it is
a free, self-renewing supply that comes up earlier than anything you sow deliberately. In a tidy
rotation it is a weed that appears in the wrong bed and has a taproot that makes it annoying to
pull once established.

Controlling it is simple: cut the flower heads before the seed hardens. Since the heads are worth
harvesting green anyway, the pickling harvest and the seed control are the same job.

Seedlings that do come up are easiest to remove young, before the taproot goes down.

## Common problems

**It flowered almost immediately.** Heat, a dry check, or root disturbance from transplanting.
Sow again, direct, and keep the young plants watered.

**It flopped over.** Height plus wind plus hollow stems, made worse by rich soil. Grow it in a
block where plants support each other, or somewhere sheltered, or give it a few canes and a loop
of string.

**Aphids on the stems.** Common on soft, well-fed growth. The insects dill's own flowers attract
are among the things that eat them, so tolerate a modest infestation rather than spraying —
spraying kills the beneficials too.

**It came up patchily.** Shallow-sown seed in a surface that dried out. Water gently and daily
through germination.

**It never came up at all.** Dill seed is short-lived. Seed kept for several years germinates
badly; use fresh.

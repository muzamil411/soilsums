Direct sow dill **on your last frost date**, and again every few weeks for a continuous supply.
Space plants **10 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart**. Dill has a taproot that resents being
moved, so it is not started indoors and not transplanted — sow it where it will grow.

## When to plant dill

On your last frost date for the first sowing, then every few weeks.

Dill matures in **40 to 60 days** — the fastest of the herbs on this site — and then flowers.
That short leafy window is why one sowing is never enough, and why the practical approach is a
short row every three weeks rather than a single patch.

Stop sowing about two months before your first fall frost, and let the last batch run to seed.
[Succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers working those dates
out.

## Why it will not transplant

Dill has a taproot, and moving it checks or kills the plant.

That rules out starting it indoors, buying pot-grown plants, and the module-sowing trick that
works for so many other crops. It also means the seedbed itself matters: thin rather than
transplant, and sow into the place the plant will finish.

## How far apart to plant dill

**10 inches between plants, 24 inches between rows.**

The spacing-derived density is about 1.4 plants per square foot, which is arithmetic rather than
a recommendation. The Cornell square foot gardening page does not name dill, so there is no
square foot gardening figure for it — the quick facts table marks that row as an estimate for
exactly that reason.

A block rather than a row has a practical advantage, which is the next point.

## It will flop

Dill reaches three feet or more on hollow stems, and a single row in an exposed spot goes over in
the first summer storm.

Three ways round it: sow somewhere sheltered, sow in a block so the plants lean on each other, or
put in a few canes and a run of string before the plants get tall. The block is the least effort.

## Soil, sun and water

**6 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 5.8 to 6.5**.

**Poor soil suits it.** Dill is one of the few things in the garden that does better without
feeding — rich ground gives soft, floppy growth and no more flavour. This is a crop to put in the
corner that never gets compost.

## Leaf or seed

They are effectively two crops from one plant, and you cannot have both from the same sowing.

**Leaf** comes before flowering. Cut whole stems from the outside and use them fresh; dill loses
most of its flavour dried.

**Seed** comes after. Let the flower heads brown, cut them with a length of stem, and hang them
upside down in a paper bag to catch the seed as it drops.

Expect **0.2 to 0.5 lb** from a plant over its short life.

## Where dill earns a place beyond the kitchen

Dill's small open flowers bring in hoverflies, lacewings and parasitic wasps, whose larvae eat
aphids and cabbage worms. That is one of the genuinely well-supported companion planting
mechanisms rather than folklore —
[does companion planting actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) separates
the two.

So letting some dill flower near a brassica bed is worth doing on its own terms, whatever you do
with the leaves. Cabbage, cucumber, onion and lettuce are the traditional neighbours. Keep dill
away from carrots, which it can cross with, and from tomatoes.

## What goes wrong

**Flopping** without support. **Self-seeding everywhere**, which is either a feature or a
nuisance depending on your tolerance. **Bolting fast in heat**, which is the reason for repeat
sowings. **Aphids** on flowering stems, usually dealt with by the insects the flowers attract.

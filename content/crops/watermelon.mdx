Plant watermelon **about 2 weeks after your last frost**, once soil is above **65°F**, **36
inches apart in rows 84 inches apart**, and harvest in **70 to 100 days**. Good companions are
corn, radishes, nasturtiums and marigolds; keep potatoes away. Ripeness is judged by the dried
tendril and the ground spot, not by tapping.

## Watermelon growing stages

Knowing which stage a plant is in tells you what it needs, and stops the commonest mistakes —
feeding nitrogen during fruit set, or panicking at male flowers that drop.

**Germination, days 7 to 10.** Seed sprouts in warm soil, slower and less reliably below 70°F.
Two seed leaves appear first, then the first true leaf.

**Vining, weeks 2 to 4.** The plant switches from a compact seedling to running vines. This is
the nitrogen-hungry phase, and the point at which the space you allowed becomes obvious.

**Flowering, weeks 5 to 7.** **Male flowers appear first, often a week or more before any
female flowers**, and they fall off. This is normal and alarms people every year. Female flowers
are identifiable by the tiny immature melon at the base of the bloom.

**Fruit set and swelling, weeks 7 to 11.** Pollinated females begin to swell. This is when water
matters most, and when the nitrogen emphasis should already have shifted.

**Ripening, weeks 10 to 14.** The fruit stops growing and starts converting starch to sugar.
Reduce watering here — heavy watering late dilutes flavour and splits fruit.

## Watermelon companion plants

**Good neighbours, and why.**

**Corn** is the traditional pairing and works on structure: tall corn gives light shade and some
wind protection to a sprawling vine, and the two occupy different vertical layers. **Radishes**
are sown between hills and harvested long before the vines need the space, which is a use of
time rather than a chemical effect.

**Nasturtiums and marigolds** earn their place mainly by flowering. Watermelon depends entirely
on insect pollination — a female flower that is not visited drops — so anything that reliably
draws bees increases fruit set. That is a real mechanism, and a better reason to plant them than
the pest-repelling claims usually given. The [marigold page](/crops/marigold/) sets out what is
and is not supported there.

**Bad neighbours, and why.**

**Potatoes.** They need digging, and a watermelon vine sprawling across the bed makes that
impossible without damage. They also share susceptibility to some soil-borne problems.

**Other sprawling vines** — squash, pumpkins, cucumbers in quantity — compete for exactly the
same ground rather than for nutrients. Two crops running across the same surface smother each
other.

## Spacing

**36 inches between plants, 84 inches between rows.**

That is a lot of ground for one plant, and it is not negotiable for full-size varieties — the
vines run several feet in every direction. A single watermelon plant can occupy the space of a
small bed.

**In a small garden, grow them vertically.** A sturdy trellis with a fabric sling under each
developing fruit works well and is the standard answer where ground space is short. Use a
smaller-fruited variety if you do.

The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) will tell you how many a bed
takes, and the [garden yield estimator](/tools/garden-yield-estimator/) gives a realistic harvest
range.

## When to plant

**About 2 weeks after your last frost**, and only once the soil is genuinely warm — above about
**65°F**. Watermelon is among the most heat-demanding crops in the garden and a cold check stalls
it permanently rather than temporarily.

Direct sowing is simplest where the season is long enough. Where it is short, start indoors
**about 3 weeks before planting out** — no earlier, because melons resent root disturbance and a
pot-bound seedling transplants badly. Use biodegradable pots that go into the ground whole.

Black plastic over the bed for a fortnight before planting warms the soil measurably and is worth
doing in cooler regions. The [planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) gives
the frost-relative date.

## Growing in containers

Only with the right variety. Choose an **icebox or bush type** bred for small spaces, and allow
**at least 15 to 20 gallons** of container per plant.

A trellis with slings is close to essential, since the pot cannot supply ground for the vines to
run across. Expect a couple of small melons rather than a crop. Full-size varieties in containers
almost always disappoint.

## Watering and feeding

Water deeply and consistently — about **an inch a week**, more during fruit swelling. Irregular
watering at that stage causes splitting and blossom end rot.

**Then ease off as the fruit ripens.** Heavy watering in the last fortnight dilutes sugar and
splits fruit. This is one of the few crops where deliberately reducing water improves the
harvest.

**Shift the feeding emphasis at flowering.** Nitrogen through the vining stage builds the plant;
continuing it through flowering gives magnificent foliage and few melons. Move towards
phosphorus and potassium once flowers appear. [How to add nitrogen to
soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/) covers the sources and their release rates if you are
feeding organically.

## How to tell when a watermelon is ripe

The hardest question on the crop, and the tap test alone is not reliable.

**Use three signs together.** The **curly tendril nearest the fruit's stem dries and turns
brown** — this is the most reliable single indicator. The **ground spot**, where the melon rests
on the soil, turns from white to a creamy or buttery yellow. And the **skin loses its gloss**,
going from shiny to dull and slightly rough.

**Watermelons do not ripen after cutting.** Unlike a tomato, what you cut is what you get, so
the cost of cutting early is a poor melon and the cost of waiting is usually nothing.

Cut the stem with a knife rather than pulling, leaving an inch or two attached.

## Common problems

**Flowers dropping with no fruit.** Male flowers naturally fall, and they come first. If female
flowers are also dropping, it is poor pollination — hand-pollinate with a brush on a dry morning,
or plant more flowers nearby.

**Huge vines, no melons.** Too much nitrogen continued past flowering.

**Split fruit.** Irregular watering, usually a heavy soaking after a dry spell.

**Hard to judge ripeness.** Covered above — learn the tendril sign, which is the most reliable.

Watermelons grow well beside [sweet corn](/crops/corn/) and should be kept clear of
[potatoes](/crops/potato/).

Plant okra **12 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart** — one plant per square foot on the
spacing-derived figure. Start seed indoors just **1 week before your last frost** and transplant
**2 weeks after** it, or direct sow **2 weeks after** the frost date. Pods are ready in **50 to
65 days** and must be picked young.

## How far apart to plant okra

Twelve inches between plants, twenty-four between rows.

That twelve-inch spacing works out to one plant per square foot, which is arithmetic: a plant
every twelve inches is one to the square however you arrange it. The Cornell square foot
gardening page does not name okra, so there is no separate square foot gardening figure for it.

Okra grows tall — four to six feet for many varieties — so the row spacing is about light and
access rather than root room. Put it on the north side of a bed so it does not shade everything
else from midsummer.

## When to plant okra

| Route          | Timing                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------ |
| Sow indoors    | 1 week before last frost |
| Transplant out | 2 weeks after last frost |
| Direct sow     | 2 weeks after last frost |

One week indoors is the shortest figure on this site, and it is deliberate. Okra grows fast and
resents root disturbance, so a long indoor run gives you a root-bound seedling that transplants
badly. Many gardeners skip the indoor step entirely and direct sow.

Either way, wait for warm soil. Okra germinates poorly in cold ground — that is the usual reason
a row fails to appear, rather than bad seed.

Soaking the seed overnight, or nicking the hard coat with a file, speeds germination.

## Sun, water and feeding

**8 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.0**.

Okra tolerates heat and poorer soil than most things in the garden, which is a large part of its
appeal in the South. Moderate nitrogen and steady potassium are enough;
[how often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the schedule, and okra sits at the undemanding end of it.

It is genuinely drought-tolerant once established, though it produces better with steady water.

## Pick every other day

This is the whole technique, and the thing that separates good okra from a plant full of
inedible pods.

Okra pods go from tender to woody in about **two days** at summer temperatures — faster than
almost any other vegetable. Pick at **three to four inches** long and keep picking. A pod left to
mature also signals the plant to slow down, so frequent picking extends the season as well as
improving the pods.

Cut rather than pull; the stems are tough. Wear sleeves and gloves — fine spines on the pods and
leaves irritate skin, unless you grow a spineless variety.

Expect **1 to 2 lb per plant** over a season.

## Where okra fits a short season

Okra needs heat, not a long season. At 50 to 65 days it is quick, so the constraint in the North
is summer temperature rather than the frost dates.

That also makes it a reasonable crop to follow something early — a bed cleared of spring lettuce
or peas in June is warm enough for okra, which is the sort of second cropping
[succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) is about.

## What goes wrong

**Tough woody pods** from picking late, which is the main one and entirely avoidable. **Poor
germination in cold soil**, which is a timing problem. **Aphids** on growing tips. **Root-knot
nematodes** in warm sandy soils, which rotation helps with.

Peppers, basil, melon and bush beans are the traditional neighbours, and okra has no crops it
needs keeping away from.

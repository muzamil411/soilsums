  - question: >-
      Why are my tomato leaves curling?
    answer: >-
      Most often heat and water stress, which makes leaves roll inward along their length and is
      not harmful in itself. If the curl is tight, twisted and accompanied by distorted new
      growth, suspect herbicide drift or contaminated compost. If the leaves are also yellowing
      from the bottom up, look at watering consistency rather than the leaves themselves.
  - question: >-
      What causes black bottoms on my tomatoes?
    answer: >-
      Blossom end rot, and it is a calcium delivery problem rather than a calcium shortage. Most
      soil has plenty of calcium; the plant cannot move it to the fruit when watering swings
      between soaked and bone dry. Steady moisture and a good mulch fix it. Adding calcium to
      soil that already has enough achieves nothing.
  - question: >-
      Should I prune the suckers off my tomatoes?
    answer: >-
      On indeterminate varieties, yes, at least the lowest ones. Removing side shoots from the
      leaf joints keeps air moving through the plant and puts more energy into fruit than
      foliage. On determinate bush varieties, leave them alone — they set their fruit all at
      once and pruning costs you crop.
  - question: >-
      How many tomato plants do I need for a family?
    answer: >-
      For fresh eating, two or three plants per person is generous. For sauce and preserving,
      count on six to twelve plants for a household, since paste tomatoes cook down to roughly a
      quarter of their fresh volume. The yield estimator above will give you a range for
      whatever number you are considering.
  - question: >-
      Why are my tomato flowers dropping without setting fruit?
    answer: >-
      Temperature, almost always. Tomatoes struggle to set fruit when nights stay above about
      75F or days climb past 90F, and also when nights drop below 55F. The plant is fine; it
      simply pauses. Keep it watered and it will resume setting when the weather moderates.
  - question: >-
      Can I grow tomatoes in a container?
    answer: >-
      Yes, with enough soil. A determinate or dwarf variety wants at least a 12 inch pot, and an
      indeterminate one wants 14 inches or more — the container calculator will tell you how
      much mix that is. The limiting factor is water: a full-grown tomato in a pot can need
      watering daily in high summer.

This page has the numbers for a tomato crop: 24 inches between plants with 36 inches
between rows, seed started six weeks before your last frost and set out a week after it,
an inch and a half of water a week, soil at pH 6.0 to 6.8, and 8 to 15 pounds of fruit
per plant across a 60 to 85 day season. The calculators above apply those to your own
bed and frost date.

Three things account for most disappointing tomato crops, and all three are covered
below: planting out into cold soil, watering that swings between soaked and dry, and
spacing that leaves no air between plants.

## Starting them off

Sow indoors about six weeks before your last frost date, in a tray on a windowsill or under a
light. Earlier is not better: a tomato sown ten weeks out becomes tall, pale and root-bound
long before it can go outside, and never fully recovers from the check. Seedlings want warmth
to germinate and then bright light to stay stocky. If they lean and stretch, they need more
light rather than more heat.

Pot them on once they have their first true leaves. Tomatoes root from buried stem, so each
time you move one up you can set it deeper, which produces a sturdier plant.

## Planting out

Wait until a week or two after your last frost date, and until nights are reliably above 50F.
Tomatoes set out into cold soil sit and sulk, and a plant put out two weeks later often
overtakes one planted early. Harden them off over a week of increasing time outdoors first.

Plant deep — deeper than looks right. Bury the bottom third of the stem, pinching off the
leaves that would be underground, and the buried section will root along its whole length.
Leggy seedlings can be laid almost sideways in a shallow trench with just the top rosette
above ground.

Space them 24 inches apart with 36 inches between rows. That looks absurdly generous in May
and about right in August. Crowded tomatoes get blight and mildew because air cannot move
between them.

Put the support in at planting time, not later. A stake, cage or string driven in beside a
six-inch seedling is easy; the same job around a four-foot plant means broken stems.

## Water and food

Tomatoes want deep, consistent watering — roughly an inch and a half a week, more in heat,
delivered in two or three long soakings rather than a daily sprinkle. Consistency matters
more than quantity: the swings between drought and flood are what cause blossom end rot and
split skins. Mulch two or three inches deep once the soil has warmed, which evens out the
moisture without any further effort.

Water the soil, not the plant. Wet foliage is how early blight and septoria get established,
and both are much easier to avoid than to cure.

On feeding, go easy on nitrogen. Rich nitrogen gives you a magnificent green plant with
little fruit on it. Work compost in before planting, then switch to something lower in
nitrogen and higher in potassium once the first fruits set. A soil test is worth more than
guesswork here, particularly for phosphate, which accumulates in long-cultivated beds.

## Determinate or indeterminate

Worth knowing before you buy, because the two behave completely differently.

**Determinate** varieties grow to a set size, flower, and ripen most of their crop within a
few weeks. Good for sauce, good for containers, and they need little or no pruning.

**Indeterminate** varieties keep growing and fruiting until frost stops them. They need real
support and benefit from having their side shoots removed. Most heirlooms are indeterminate.

## Picking

Pick when the colour has developed and the fruit gives very slightly to a gentle squeeze. A
tomato will finish ripening off the plant, so in a wet spell it is better to pick a
nearly-ripe fruit than to lose it to splitting. At the end of the season, green fruit brought
indoors will ripen slowly in a paper bag, or can be used green.

Do not refrigerate a tomato you intend to eat fresh. Cold permanently flattens the flavour.

Plant kale **12 inches apart in rows 24 inches apart** — one plant per square foot on the
spacing-derived figure. Start seed indoors **8 weeks before your last frost** and plant out **3
weeks before** it, or direct sow **3 weeks before** the frost date. Leaves are ready from **50 to
65 days**.

## How far apart to plant kale

Twelve inches between plants, twenty-four between rows.

That twelve-inch spacing works out to one plant per square foot, which is arithmetic rather than
a recommendation — a plant every twelve inches is one to the square however you arrange it. The
Cornell square foot gardening page does not name kale, so there is no separate square foot
gardening figure for it.

Kale spreads more than a young plant suggests. Crowded plants produce smaller leaves and hold
damp air around themselves, which suits aphids.

## When to plant kale

| Route          | Timing                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| Sow indoors    | 8 weeks before last frost |
| Transplant out | 3 weeks before last frost |
| Direct sow     | 3 weeks before last frost |

Kale is unusual among brassicas in being genuinely good either way. Transplants give an earlier
start; direct sowing avoids the transplant check and is simpler.

The more useful sowing for most gardens is the second one. A midsummer sowing gives autumn and
winter leaves, which are better than spring leaves — see the frost section below.
[Succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers working backwards
from your first fall frost.

## Soil, sun and water

**6 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.5**.

Kale is the least demanding brassica. It tolerates partial shade, poorer soil and more neglect
than cabbage or cauliflower, which is a large part of why it is worth growing. Keep the pH up
all the same — clubroot affects kale like any other brassica.

## Feeding for leaves

Steady nitrogen through the season keeps new leaves coming, because leaves are the crop and you
are harvesting them continuously.

Light and frequent beats one heavy dose. A big slug of nitrogen produces soft leaves that aphids
find first.
[How often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the leafy-crop schedule.

## Why kale improves after frost

Cold triggers the plant to convert stored starches into sugars, which act as antifreeze. The
leaves become noticeably sweeter, and the coarse, slightly bitter edge of summer kale disappears.

This is the argument for the midsummer sowing. A plant that sizes up in autumn and is picked
through the first frosts produces a better vegetable than the same plant picked in July.

In much of the US kale overwinters outright, with some protection in the coldest zones, and
produces again in early spring before bolting as days lengthen.

## Picking for a long season

Take the **lower, outer leaves** and leave the growing point at the centre alone. Picked that
way a single plant produces for months.

Expect **1 to 2 lb per plant** over a season, and more from an overwintered one. Pick leaves at
hand size rather than waiting — large old leaves go tough and the plant slows down.

## What goes wrong

**Cabbage worms** and **aphids** are the usual two, aphids sheltering in the crinkled leaves of
curly varieties where they are hard to wash out. **Flea beetles** pepper young plants.
**Clubroot** follows acid soil and poor rotation.

Onions, beets, nasturtium and dill are the traditional neighbours. Keep kale away from tomatoes
and strawberries, and do not follow one brassica with another.

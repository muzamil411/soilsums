Plant pumpkins **48 inches apart in rows 72 inches apart** — the most ground of any crop on this
site. Start indoors **3 weeks before your last frost** and transplant **on the frost date**, or
direct sow **3 weeks after** it. Expect **90 to 120 days** and **10 to 25 lb per plant**.

## How far apart to plant pumpkins

Four feet between plants, six feet between rows.

That is about seventeen square feet per plant. There is no way around it: a pumpkin vine runs
ten feet or more, and a crowded pumpkin patch produces mildew and small fruit rather than more
pumpkins.

The spacing-derived density is roughly one plant per seventeen square feet. The Cornell square
foot gardening page does not name pumpkins, so there is no square foot gardening figure for them
— and a crop needing seventeen squares does not fit a sixteen-square bed at all.
[How to plan a vegetable garden layout](/blog/planning-a-vegetable-garden-layout/) covers where a
sprawling crop like this belongs, which is usually an edge where the vines can run outward.

## When to plant pumpkins

| Route          | Timing                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------- |
| Sow indoors    | 3 weeks before last frost |
| Transplant out | On the last frost date    |
| Direct sow     | 3 weeks after last frost  |

Three weeks _after_ the frost date is later than most things, and deliberate. Pumpkins need warm
soil, and there is rarely anything to gain from rushing: a June sowing catches up with a May one.

## Working backwards from Halloween

This is the calculation most pumpkin growers actually want.

```
sowing date = target date - days to maturity - 2 weeks
```

For a 100-day variety wanted at the end of October, that puts sowing in early to mid June across
much of the US. The two-week margin covers slower growth as days shorten in September.

Sowing in April, which feels right, produces pumpkins that are ripe in August and rotting by the
time anyone wants them.

## Sun, water and feeding

**8 hours of sun**, **1.5 inches of water a week**, soil at **pH 6.0 to 7.0**.

Nitrogen early while the vines are growing, then switch to phosphorus and potassium once flowers
appear. Continued heavy nitrogen gives enormous vines and very little fruit — the single most
common feeding mistake with pumpkins.

Water at the base, not over the leaves. Powdery mildew on a pumpkin in August cuts the season
short.

## Keeping the fruit off wet soil

A pumpkin sitting on damp ground rots on its underside. Slide a board, a tile or a thick pad of
straw under each fruit once it has set and is big enough to move safely.

This also keeps the skin cleaner and more evenly coloured, which matters if the pumpkin is for
display rather than eating.

## Harvest and curing

Ripe when the rind resists a thumbnail, the colour is fully developed and the stem has gone
corky.

Cut with **three or four inches of stem attached**, and carry it by the body rather than the
stem — a pumpkin whose stem snaps off rots at the scar within weeks. Then cure for a week or two
somewhere warm and dry, and store cool and dry after that.

Two to five fruit per plant for a medium variety. If you want large pumpkins, thin to one or two
per vine once they have set.

## What goes wrong

**A season too short** is the commonest, and it is a planning problem — check days to maturity
against your frost dates before buying seed. **Squash vine borer** tunnels into the stem near the
base and wilts the plant; look for sawdust-like frass at a small hole. **Powdery mildew** follows
crowding and overhead watering. **Rot where fruit sits on wet soil** is preventable with a board.

Corn, beans and nasturtium are the traditional neighbours. Keep pumpkins away from potatoes.
[How much food can you grow in a 4x8 raised bed](/blog/how-much-food-from-a-4x8-raised-bed/) is
worth reading before committing the ground — pumpkins are among the least efficient crops per
square foot in a small garden.

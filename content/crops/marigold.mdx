Marigolds are **annuals**, not perennials, whatever their reappearance each spring suggests.
Sow seed **6 weeks before your last frost**, expect germination in **5 to 7 days**, and space
plants **10 inches apart**. They are the garden's most-planted companion flower, though the
evidence is narrower than the reputation.

## Are marigolds perennial?

**No. They are annuals everywhere**, and the plant dies with the first hard frost.

The reason so many people believe otherwise is that marigolds self-seed prolifically. A plant
left to set seed drops it around itself, and the following spring seedlings appear in the same
patch of ground. The bed looks like it came back. What actually happened is that a new
generation germinated where the old one died.

That is worth knowing because it changes what you do: if you want marigolds in the same place
next year, leave a few plants to finish flowering and set seed rather than deadheading
everything. If you do not, pull them before the seed drops.

## Marigold companion plants

Marigolds are planted beside more vegetables than any other flower, so it is worth separating
what is supported from what is repeated.

**The well-supported effect is on root-knot nematodes.** Certain *Tagetes* varieties release
compounds from their roots that suppress these soil-dwelling pests. The important detail, which
most advice omits: this works when marigolds are grown as a **dense stand across the ground for
a full season and then dug in** — effectively as a cover crop. A few plants dotted between
tomatoes do not deliver it.

**The weaker claim is scent repelling flying pests.** Marigolds are widely said to deter
whitefly, aphids and beetles by smell. Support for this is thin, and where it exists the effect
is modest. [Whether companion planting actually
works](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) sets out how to weigh claims like this.

**The genuinely reliable benefit is flowers.** Marigolds bloom heavily over a long season and
draw hoverflies, parasitic wasps and other predators of aphids and caterpillars. That is a real
mechanism, it is not specific to marigolds, and it is probably the main reason gardens with them
do better.

**Good neighbours, and why.**

**Tomatoes** — the classic pairing, and the one where the nematode effect is most relevant since
tomatoes are badly affected by root-knot nematodes. **Peppers and aubergines** share the same
nematode susceptibility. **Cucumbers and squash** benefit mainly from the pollinator draw, since
both need insect pollination to set fruit at all. **Bush beans** sit happily alongside them.

**Bad neighbours.** There are effectively none. The frequently repeated claim that marigolds
inhibit beans has no support worth citing, and it contradicts the equally common advice to plant
them together — we do not carry it.

## Sowing marigold seed

Marigolds are among the easiest seeds in the garden and germinate fast.

**Sow a quarter of an inch deep** in moist compost. Seeds are long, thin and easy to handle, and
they can be spaced individually rather than scattered.

**Germination takes 5 to 7 days** in warm compost — noticeably quicker than most flowers, which
is why they are a good first sowing for a child. Keep the surface damp but not wet; damping off
is the one thing that reliably kills marigold seedlings.

**Start about 6 weeks before your last frost** for early flowers, or sow direct once the frost
date has passed. They grow quickly enough that direct sowing loses very little time, so there is
no need to start them indoors unless you want colour early. The [planting date
calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) works the dates from your own frost date.

Marigolds are frost-tender. A late frost kills seedlings outright rather than setting them back,
so do not plant out early on a warm week in spring.

## Spacing

**French marigolds 8 to 10 inches apart; African marigolds 12 to 16 inches.**

Clemson gives the two separately because the plants are genuinely different sizes, and a single
figure would crowd one and waste space on the other. It gives no separate row spacing at all, so
none is quoted here — space them the same distance in both directions and you will be following
the factsheet rather than filling in a gap it left.

If you are growing them as a nematode cover crop rather than for display, plant much more
densely than either figure, because the effect depends on root density across the whole area.

The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) will work out numbers for a bed.

## Growing in containers

Marigolds suit pots well and are often the plant used to fill gaps in mixed containers. Allow
**6 to 8 inches of depth** and one plant per 6 inches of width.

The container caveat is the same as the ground one: rich potting mix gives leaf rather than
flower. A standard mix with no added feed is right.

## Soil, water and feeding

**Poor soil suits them.** This is the counterintuitive part and the commonest growing mistake:
marigolds flower best on unremarkable ground. Rich soil, or a generous nitrogen feed, gives a
large dark-green leafy plant with few flowers. [How to add nitrogen to
soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/) covers why excess nitrogen produces leaf at the expense
of bloom — marigolds are a clear case of it.

Full sun. Water about **an inch a week**; they tolerate dry spells better than most bedding
plants.

**Deadhead regularly.** Removing spent flowers before they set seed keeps the plant flowering for
months — unless you want them to self-seed, in which case leave the last flush.

## Common problems

**Lots of leaves, few flowers.** Soil too rich or feeding too generous. Stop feeding; the plant
recovers within a few weeks.

**Seedlings collapsing at soil level.** Damping off, from compost kept too wet or poor air
movement. Sow thinly, water from below, and let the surface dry slightly between waterings.

**Slugs on young plants.** Marigold seedlings are a favourite. They are much less vulnerable once
established, so protection matters mainly in the first fortnight.

**Spider mites in hot, dry spells.** Fine webbing and stippled leaves, usually on plants that
have been dry and sheltered. Watering and airflow help more than treatment.

Marigolds are most often planted alongside [tomatoes](/crops/tomato/) and
[peppers](/crops/pepper/), both of which are susceptible to the nematodes they suppress.

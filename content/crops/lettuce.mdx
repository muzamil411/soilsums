faqs:
  - question: >-
      Why does my lettuce bolt so quickly?
    answer: >-
      Heat and lengthening days. Once a lettuce decides it is summer it sends up a flower stalk,
      the leaves turn bitter and no amount of watering reverses it. Sow in spring and again in
      late summer rather than through midsummer, choose varieties described as slow to bolt or
      heat tolerant, and give afternoon shade once the weather warms.
  - question: >-
      Should I cut lettuce or pull it?
    answer: >-
      Both work and they suit different aims. Cutting the outer leaves and leaving the centre
      gives you a longer, smaller harvest from each plant. Cutting the whole head an inch above
      the crown often produces a second, smaller flush. Pulling the plant frees the space for
      the next sowing, which is usually the better use of a small bed.
  - question: >-
      How often should I sow lettuce?
    answer: >-
      Every two to three weeks through spring and again from late summer. A single sowing gives
      you far more lettuce than anyone can eat in one fortnight, then nothing. A short row every
      couple of weeks is the whole trick to having salad continuously, and it costs no more seed
      overall.
  - question: >-
      Why is my lettuce bitter?
    answer: >-
      Stress, most commonly heat or a plant that has started to bolt. Irregular watering and a
      plant left too long both contribute. Bitterness is not reversible in a bolting plant, but
      harvesting in the cool of the morning gives noticeably better leaves than picking in the
      afternoon.
  - question: >-
      Can lettuce take a frost?
    answer: >-
      Most varieties take a light frost without harm, which is why lettuce can go out three
      weeks before your last frost date and carry on well into autumn. A hard freeze will damage
      the outer leaves. Fleece or a cloche extends the season at both ends considerably for very
      little effort.
  - question: >-
      Does lettuce grow in shade?
    answer: >-
      Better than most vegetables. Four hours of sun is enough, and in high summer partial shade
      is an advantage rather than a compromise — it slows bolting and keeps the leaves sweeter.
      A bed that is too shady for tomatoes may be exactly right for salad.

This page has the numbers for lettuce: 8 inches between plants with 12 inches between
rows, four to a square foot, direct sown four weeks before your last frost or
transplanted three weeks before it, an inch of water a week, and half a pound to a pound
per plant in 45 to 65 days.

The numbers matter less here than the calendar. Lettuce bolts in heat, so the page is
mostly about sowing a short row every two or three weeks rather than one long row in
April, and about when to stop and start again in late summer.

## Sowing

Lettuce seed germinates at surprisingly low temperatures and refuses at high ones, which
shapes everything about how it is grown. Direct sow from about four weeks before your last
frost date, or start indoors six weeks before and transplant three weeks before the frost
date — the seedlings take a light frost without complaint.

Sow shallowly. Lettuce needs light to germinate, so press the seed into the surface and cover
it barely or not at all. Sowing too deep is the usual reason a row fails to come up.

In midsummer, germination becomes the problem rather than bolting. Seed sown into warm soil
may not come up at all. Sowing in the late afternoon, watering the drill before sowing, and
shading the row all help.

## Spacing and thinning

Eight inches between plants with 12 inches between rows suits a full head. For cut-and-come-again
leaves you can sow much thicker and simply cut across the row.

Thin early and thin properly. Lettuce left crowded makes small, floppy plants that bolt
sooner, and the thinnings are the best salad of the season. Four plants per square foot is
the square-foot-gardening figure and it is about right.

## Water and food

An inch of water a week, and more importantly, without long dry gaps. Lettuce is shallow
rooted and mostly water, so it feels a dry spell within days. Bitterness and bolting both
follow drought stress.

Feeding is straightforward: nitrogen for leaf growth, given lightly and often rather than in
one dose. Compost worked into the bed before sowing is usually enough for a crop this quick.
Lettuce is a good candidate for the space between slower crops — a row of lettuce between
young brassicas will be finished before the brassicas need the room.

## Succession, the part that matters

The difference between a gardener who has salad all season and one who has a glut in June is
entirely a matter of sowing little and often. A three-foot row every two or three weeks,
through spring and then again from late summer, keeps a household in leaves without any of it
going to seed uneaten.

Stop sowing through the hottest weeks and start again as nights begin to cool. Autumn lettuce
is often the best of the year: the plants grow steadily, nothing bolts, and pests have mostly
finished.

## Common trouble

Slugs are the main enemy, particularly on seedlings in damp spring weather. Going out after
dark with a torch is unglamorous and more effective than most alternatives.

Aphids collect in the heart of a plant where they are hard to wash out, which is an argument
for picking outer leaves rather than whole heads late in the season.

Tip burn — brown, crisped leaf edges — comes from irregular water in hot weather rather than
from disease. Mulch and consistency are the answer.

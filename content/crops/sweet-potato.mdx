Set sweet potato slips out about **3 weeks after your last frost**, and only once the soil is
**thoroughly warm** — not merely frost-free. Space them **12 inches apart in rows 36 inches
apart**. They are grown from rooted slips rather than seed, taking **90 to 120 days** to a crop
of **2 to 4 lb per plant**.

## When to plant sweet potato slips

Three weeks after your last frost, which is later than almost anything else in the garden.

The frost date is not really the constraint. Sweet potato is a tropical crop and it wants warm
soil throughout, not just at planting. A slip set into cool ground sits and sulks, and the tubers
it eventually makes are smaller for it. Waiting an extra fortnight in a cool spring costs
nothing.

There is no indoor sowing date, because there is no seed. Slips are either bought or sprouted at
home from a tuber suspended in water or half-buried in damp compost about six weeks ahead.

## How far apart to plant sweet potato slips

**12 inches between plants, 36 inches between rows** — one plant per square foot on both the
spacing-derived figure and the square foot gardening method, which agree here.

That square foot gardening figure is Mel Bartholomew's convention rather than an extension
recommendation, though the vines will run well past their square whichever way you count.

Plant each slip deep, with only the top two or three leaves above the surface. Roots form along
the buried stem, and those roots become the crop.

## Loose soil matters more than feeding

This is the point most often missed. Sweet potatoes form tubers by swelling into the soil around
them, and they cannot do that in compacted ground.

A deep, loose, well-worked bed — or a raised ridge, which is the traditional approach — produces
straight, well-shaped tubers. Heavy or compacted soil gives small, twisted ones however well you
feed the plant.

**8 hours of sun**, about **an inch of water a week**, soil at **pH 5.5 to 6.5**.

That pH range is lower than most vegetables want, so a bed limed for brassicas is the wrong bed.
[How to raise soil pH](/blog/how-to-raise-soil-ph/) covers the timing if
you need to move the other way.

## Feed lightly

**Low nitrogen, higher potassium.**

Rich nitrogen grows magnificent vines and thin roots — the characteristic sweet potato failure,
and an easy one to cause by planting into a bed that has just had compost dug in.
[How often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the root-crop pattern, which sweet potato follows closely despite being an unusual plant.

## Harvest and curing

**90 to 120 days**, and lift before the first frost — frost damages the tubers and they will not
store.

Dig carefully with a fork well out from the crown; the skins are thin at harvest and every nick
is a place for rot to start. Handle them like eggs.

Then cure: about a week somewhere **warm and humid**, around 80°F if you can manage it. Curing
heals the skin and converts starches to sugars, which is what makes a sweet potato sweet. An
uncured one is bland and does not keep. After curing, store somewhere cool but not cold — a
refrigerator ruins them.

## What goes wrong

**Cold soil stalling growth** is the recurring theme and the reason for the late planting date.
**Wireworms** tunnel into tubers in ground recently in grass. **Cracked roots** follow uneven
watering, particularly a heavy soaking after a dry spell late in the season.

Bush beans, dill and thyme are the traditional neighbours. Keep sweet potatoes away from squash,
which will smother them.

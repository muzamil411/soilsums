  - question: >-
      Should I grow bush beans or pole beans?
    answer: >-
      Bush beans crop heavily over two or three weeks and need no support, which suits freezing
      and preserving. Pole beans take longer to start but then produce steadily until frost from
      a fraction of the ground area, which suits fresh eating from a small bed. If you have
      anything to climb up, pole beans give more food per square foot.
  - question: >-
      Do beans need fertilizer?
    answer: >-
      Not nitrogen. Beans fix their own from the air through bacteria on their roots, and a
      nitrogen feed gives you a large leafy plant with few pods. Phosphorus and potassium are
      useful if a soil test shows they are low. This is the most common mistake made with beans,
      and it is made by people trying to be helpful.
  - question: >-
      Why did my bean seed rot instead of coming up?
    answer: >-
      Cold, wet soil. Beans are one of the crops most likely to rot rather than germinate if
      sown too early, and no amount of care compensates. Wait until a week after your last frost
      date and until the soil is properly warm. A sowing made two weeks later will usually
      overtake one made too early.
  - question: >-
      How often should I pick beans?
    answer: >-
      Every two or three days once they start. A pod left to fill with seed tells the plant its
      work is done and production drops off sharply. Regular picking is the single thing that
      turns a fortnight's crop into a month's. Pick in the morning when the pods are crisp.
  - question: >-
      Why are my bean flowers dropping without setting pods?
    answer: >-
      Heat, usually — beans set poorly when days are consistently above about 90F. Dry soil
      during flowering makes it worse. The plant is not damaged and will set again when the
      weather moderates, so keep it watered and wait rather than pulling it out.
  - question: >-
      Can I sow beans more than once in a season?
    answer: >-
      Yes, and for bush beans you should. A sowing every two or three weeks until about ten
      weeks before your first fall frost keeps beans coming continuously rather than in one
      overwhelming flush. Pole beans need only one sowing, since they carry on all season by
      themselves.

This page has the numbers for bush beans: 4 inches between plants with 24 inches between
rows, nine to a square foot, direct sown a week after your last frost, an inch of water a
week, and a quarter to half a pound per plant in 50 to 65 days.

One number is deliberately absent. Beans need no nitrogen fertilizer at all — they fix
their own, and feeding them gives foliage instead of pods. The page also covers the
choice between bush and pole, which changes both the spacing above and how long the crop
lasts.

## Bush or pole

This choice shapes everything else.

**Bush beans** grow knee-high, need no support, and produce most of their crop in a two or
three week burst. That concentration is useful if you are freezing or canning, and awkward if
you want beans for dinner through the summer. Sow them in succession every two or three weeks.

**Pole beans** climb six to eight feet, take a few weeks longer to start, and then crop
steadily until frost. From the same square footage they give considerably more over a season,
and picking at standing height is kinder on the back. They need a tepee, a trellis or strings.

## Sowing

Direct sow about a week after your last frost date, into properly warm soil. Beans dislike
transplanting and grow fast enough that there is nothing to gain from starting them indoors.

Sow an inch deep, and resist watering heavily until they are up — a bean seed sitting in cold
wet soil rots. Two seeds per station, thinned to one, covers the losses.

Space bush beans four inches apart with 24 inches between rows; nine to a square foot works in
a bed. Pole beans want four to six inches along the base of their support.

## Why you should not feed them

Beans host bacteria in root nodules that convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form the plant can
use. Given nitrogen fertilizer, the plant takes the easy route: lots of leaf, few pods, and the
nodules do not form properly.

So skip the nitrogen entirely. If a soil test shows phosphorus or potassium are low, address
those. Otherwise a bean bed needs nothing beyond reasonable soil, and the crop leaves the bed
slightly better than it found it — which is why beans are a useful thing to rotate through a
bed before a hungry crop like brassicas.

## Water

An inch a week, with particular attention during flowering and pod set. A dry spell at
flowering costs pods directly. Water the soil rather than the foliage: wet leaves spread rust
and anthracnose, both of which show up on beans given the chance.

Mulching helps, and so does spacing that lets air move between plants.

## Picking

Every two or three days, and pick young. A pod picked while the seeds inside are still small is
tender; one left to bulge is stringy, and worse, signals the plant to stop. This is the whole
technique of getting a long harvest out of beans.

Pick with two hands — one holding the vine, one pulling the pod — or you will pull whole stems
off a pole bean.

If a row gets ahead of you, let the remaining pods fill out completely and dry on the plant, and
use them as shelling or dried beans instead. A crop that has gone past its best for fresh eating
has simply become a different crop.

## Common trouble

**Mexican bean beetle** larvae skeletonise leaves from underneath. Hand-picking the yellow egg
clusters early is the effective response.

**Rust and anthracnose** both appear as spots on leaves and pods in humid weather. Space
generously, keep foliage dry, and do not work among wet plants.

**Nothing setting in a heat wave** is temporary rather than a problem to solve.

One piece of folklore worth ignoring: beans are often said to dislike
[marigolds](/crops/marigold/). There is nothing behind it, and the two grow together perfectly
well.

Beans leave nitrogen behind in their roots when cut at ground level, which makes them a useful
crop to precede a leaf crop — though not a hungry root crop like
[sweet potato](/crops/sweet-potato/), which puts surplus nitrogen into vine rather than tuber.

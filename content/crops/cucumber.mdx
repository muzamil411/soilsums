faqs:
  - question: >-
      Why are my cucumbers bitter?
    answer: >-
      Drought stress, nearly always. Cucumbers are more than 95% water and a dry spell
      concentrates the bitter compounds in the skin and stem end. Consistent watering and a good
      mulch prevent it. Heat makes it worse, and once a fruit is bitter it will not improve, so
      fix the watering rather than the fruit.
  - question: >-
      Should I grow cucumbers up a trellis?
    answer: >-
      Yes, if you have the space vertically. Trellised cucumbers take a fraction of the ground
      area, the fruit hangs straight instead of curling, air moves through the foliage so mildew
      is less of a problem, and picking is far easier. Give a vining variety six feet of netting
      or mesh and it will do the rest.
  - question: >-
      Why do my cucumber plants have flowers but no fruit?
    answer: >-
      Most likely the early male flowers, which appear a week or two before the females on many
      varieties and set nothing. Wait. If female flowers — the ones with a tiny cucumber behind
      them — are forming and then shrivelling, the problem is pollination, and hand pollinating
      with a small brush or encouraging bees will fix it.
  - question: >-
      What is the white powder on my cucumber leaves?
    answer: >-
      Powdery mildew, and on cucumbers it is near-inevitable by late summer. It spreads in humid
      air and crowded foliage. Trellising, generous spacing and watering the soil rather than
      the leaves all slow it down. Removing the worst leaves keeps the plant producing for a few
      more weeks, but expect it to end the crop eventually.
  - question: >-
      How many cucumber plants do I need?
    answer: >-
      Two or three plants keep a household in fresh cucumbers, since a healthy plant gives five
      to ten pounds over a season. For pickling you will want more — six to eight plants, sown
      in two batches a fortnight apart so the fruit arrives in usable quantities rather than all
      at once.
  - question: >-
      Why are my cucumbers curled or fat at one end?
    answer: >-
      Uneven pollination or uneven water. A fruit that was incompletely pollinated grows
      unevenly and ends up curled or club-shaped. It is perfectly edible. Trellising helps by
      letting the fruit hang, and steady moisture during fruit development does the rest.

This page has the numbers for cucumbers: 12 inches between plants when trellised, 48
inches between rows when left to sprawl, direct sown a week after your last frost or
started indoors three weeks before it, an inch and a half of water a week, and 5 to 10
pounds of fruit per plant over a 50 to 70 day season.

Cucumbers fail in two predictable ways, and the page deals with both: they run short of
water, which makes the fruit bitter, and they get powdery mildew, which ends the crop.
Trellising helps with the second and the spacing figures below assume it.

## Sowing and planting

Cucumbers hate cold soil. Direct sow about a week after your last frost date, or start indoors
three weeks before it and plant out two weeks after. They resent root disturbance, so if you
start them indoors use a large cell or a pot and get them out before they are pot-bound —
which happens fast.

Two sowings a fortnight apart is a better plan than one. The first plants crop heavily for a
few weeks and then tire or succumb to mildew; the second batch takes over.

Space them 12 inches apart if trellised, with 48 inches between rows if left to sprawl. That
row figure is the honest measure of how much ground an untrellised cucumber takes.

## Trellis them

This is the single best decision available to a cucumber grower in a small garden. A vining
variety on six feet of netting occupies a foot of ground and produces straighter fruit that is
easier to find and pick. Air moves through the foliage, which delays mildew by weeks. The fruit
hangs clear of the soil, so less of it rots.

Bush and compact varieties exist for containers and small beds, and they do not need support,
but they also produce less.

## Water and food

Cucumbers want an inch and a half a week, and they show a shortfall faster than almost
anything else in the garden — wilting at midday, then bitter fruit. Mulch two or three inches
deep once the soil warms, and water the soil rather than the leaves.

Feed generously by cucumber standards: compost worked in before planting, then a balanced feed
once the vines start running. They are hungry, fast-growing plants and will use what you give
them, unlike tomatoes and peppers where restraint pays.

## Picking

Pick often and pick young. A cucumber left on the vine to go fat and yellow tells the plant its
work is done, and production slows sharply. Going over the plants every couple of days in high
season is what keeps them cropping for two months instead of three weeks.

Cut rather than pull; the stems are more brittle than they look.

## Common trouble

**Cucumber beetles** chew leaves and, more seriously, spread bacterial wilt. A plant that wilts
suddenly and does not recover overnight has probably got it, and there is no cure — remove it.
Covers over young plants until they flower are the main defence.

**Powdery mildew** is a question of when, not if. Spacing, trellising and dry foliage buy time.
Choosing a resistant variety buys more.

**Hollow or misshapen fruit** points at water and pollination rather than disease.

## In containers

Cucumbers grow well in a large pot with something to climb. Use a container holding at least
five gallons of mix — the container calculator will tell you what your pot actually holds — and
expect to water daily in summer. A cucumber in a small pot is a plant that wilts every
afternoon and produces bitter fruit.

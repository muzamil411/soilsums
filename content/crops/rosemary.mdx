This page is mostly about propagation, because that is what rosemary is mostly searched for and
because seed is the wrong way to get a plant. Rosemary seed germinates slowly and unevenly and
gives a variable seedling; a cutting roots in weeks and copies a plant you have already tasted.

The quick facts table above carries almost no dates or figures for this crop, and the reason
sits in each row: rosemary is grown from cuttings rather than sown, so a frost-offset sowing date
does not describe it.

## Why cuttings rather than seed

Rosemary can be grown from seed. It is just a poor idea, and it is worth saying why rather than
leaving it as received wisdom.

- **Germination is slow and erratic.** A low proportion of seed comes up, over a long and
  unpredictable period, which makes a tray hard to manage.
- **Seedlings are slow.** A rosemary from seed takes a long time to become a plant you can pick
  from. A rooted cutting is ahead of it from the start.
- **Seed does not copy the parent.** Rosemary varieties differ in habit, flavour and how much cold
  and wet they tolerate. Seedlings are variable, so you cannot know what you have until it is
  grown. A cutting is genetically the parent plant.

That last point is the one that matters most in a cold or wet garden. If a neighbour has a
rosemary that has survived ten winters where yours have died, a cutting from it is worth more than
any seed packet — you are copying a plant that has already proved itself in your conditions.

## How to grow rosemary from cuttings

The whole process is short, and almost every failure is the same failure: too much water.

**1. Choose the shoot.** Take from the current season's growth, on a healthy plant, choosing a
shoot that is firm but still flexible. The soft floppy tips wilt before they root; the hard brown
wood lower down will not root at all. Avoid a shoot that is flowering — the plant's energy is
going the wrong way.

**2. Cut the length you need.** Four to six inches, cut just below a leaf joint with clean, sharp
snips. Take several. Not every cutting roots, and a handful costs no more effort than one.

**3. Strip the lower leaves.** Pull or cut the leaves off the bottom half of each cutting, leaving
the top ones. Any leaf buried in the mix rots, and that rot spreads to the stem. This is the step
most often skimped.

**4. Fill pots with a gritty mix.** Roughly half compost and half coarse grit, perlite or sharp
sand. Ordinary potting compost alone holds far too much water for rosemary, and a cutting has no
roots to drink it. Small pots are better than large ones for the same reason.

**5. Insert the cuttings.** Push each one in so the bare lower half is buried and the remaining
leaves sit well clear of the surface. Firm the mix gently around the stem — a cutting that wobbles
will not make root contact — and water once to settle it.

**6. Keep them warm, bright and barely moist.** Somewhere bright, out of direct midday sun, and
barely moist rather than wet. **Do not cover rosemary cuttings with a sealed bag or a propagator
lid.** That is standard advice for soft cuttings and it is wrong here: the trapped humidity is
exactly what rots a woody Mediterranean cutting. A little air movement helps.

**7. Check for roots after a few weeks.** At three or four weeks, tug one very gently. Resistance
means roots. Firm new growth at the tip is the other reliable sign. Some take longer; be patient
before writing a pot off, and expect a proportion to fail.

**8. Pot on and harden off.** Move each rooted cutting into its own pot of gritty mix and grow it
on. Keep it sheltered through its first winter before planting out — a young rooted cutting is far
more vulnerable to cold and wet than an established plant, and this is where most home-propagated
rosemary is lost.

Cuttings taken from firm summer growth are the most reliable, and rosemary will also root in water
on a windowsill. Water-rooted cuttings transfer to soil less successfully than ones rooted in a
gritty mix, so the mix is worth the extra step.

## Sun, shade and position

Rosemary wants the sunniest, most open position you have, with air moving around it. This is not
only about growth — sun and wind dry the foliage and the soil surface, and a plant that dries out
between waterings is a plant that hardens off and survives winter.

It will grow in part shade, and it will not look right: longer, thinner stems, sparse foliage,
weaker scent, and a much higher chance of rot. If the only spot available is shaded for half the
day, grow it in a pot you can move into the light rather than planting it out there.

## Cold, wet and what an established plant survives

Rosemary's hardiness is usually discussed as a single number, and that framing is the reason
people lose plants. **Cold on its own is rarely the killer. Cold plus wet, heavy soil is.**

The same variety, in the same winter, in the same garden, will come through in gritty, sharply
drained ground and die in a cold, wet clay bed. Water sitting around the roots through a cold
spell rots them, and the plant browns off from the base. So the useful question about a position
is not how low the temperature drops, but where the water goes after heavy rain.

Three things shift the odds, none of them a temperature:

- **Drainage**, which matters more than everything else together. On heavy ground, plant on a
  mound, in a raised bed, or in a pot. [What to fill raised garden beds
  with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/) covers the mix; for rosemary, push it towards
  the coarse and gritty end.
- **Variety.** Upright types are generally tougher than trailing and prostrate ones, and named
  varieties are sold specifically for cold tolerance. Nursery labels are the place to check.
- **Age.** An established plant with a woody frame and a deep root system is in a different
  position from a rooted cutting in its first year. Give a young plant shelter for its first
  winter.

Where winter is genuinely too much, the practical answer is a pot that moves somewhere bright,
unheated and frost-free, kept only just moist. A rosemary kept warm and watered indoors all
winter usually does worse than one kept cold and nearly dry.

## Growing rosemary in a pot

A pot is often the best way to grow rosemary, because it puts the drainage entirely under your
control.

**The mix.** Free-draining and gritty: about a third coarse grit, perlite or sharp sand mixed into
ordinary compost. A rich, moisture-retentive potting compost is the single most common reason a
potted rosemary dies, and it dies at the roots long before the foliage shows it.

**The pot.** Make sure the drainage holes are open, stand it on feet or bricks so water actually
leaves, and never leave it standing in a full saucer. Terracotta helps because it dries through
its walls. Do not over-pot — a container far larger than the rootball holds a lot of wet mix the
roots are not using.

**Watering.** Let the surface dry between waterings. A pot in summer sun needs water regularly and
that is fine; what is fatal is a mix that never dries. Indoors, still humid air also invites
powdery mildew, so give it some air movement.

## Feeding, pruning and picking

**Feeding: very little.** Rosemary grows on poor, thin soil in its native range and rich feeding
produces soft growth that is more prone to rot and less strongly flavoured. Drainage is worth more
attention than fertiliser.

**Pruning: lightly, and not into bare wood.** Like lavender, rosemary is reluctant to reshoot from
old bare wood, so keep it in shape by trimming growth that still carries leaves rather than cutting
hard into the brown frame. Trimming after flowering keeps a plant compact; an untrimmed one
gradually becomes a sprawling, woody thing with the foliage all at the ends.

**Picking is pruning.** Take sprigs from the tips through the season and the plant stays bushy.
Rosemary is evergreen, so there is no season when there is nothing to pick, though growth
effectively stops in the coldest months and a plant should not be stripped then.

## Rosemary companion plants

As with lavender, the useful test for a rosemary companion is not a traditional pairing list but
whether the neighbour wants the same conditions. Rosemary needs sun, sharp drainage and poor soil,
and it will be killed by the watering and feeding regime that a moisture-loving neighbour needs.

That makes the other woody Mediterranean herbs its natural companions:
[lavender](/crops/lavender/), thyme and sage all share its requirements almost exactly, and a bed
of those four can be managed as one. The soft herbs are the opposite case —
[basil](/crops/basil/), [dill](/crops/dill/) and [parsley](/crops/parsley/) all want moisture and
some feeding, so they belong in different ground.

Rosemary is also traditionally planted near brassicas, on the claim that its scent confuses
cabbage moth and carrot fly. This is one of the weaker companion planting claims: the mechanism is
plausible, the evidence is thin, and there is no cost to trying it. [Does companion planting
actually work](/blog/does-companion-planting-actually-work/) sorts the well-supported mechanisms
from the repeated ones. What is not in doubt is that rosemary in flower is worked hard by bees,
and it flowers very early in the year when little else does.

Note the naming confusion while you are here: rosemary has been reclassified as _Salvia
rosmarinus_, so it is now botanically a salvia. Lavender is not, and never was, despite being
mistaken for one constantly.

## Common problems

**The cuttings rotted.** Too wet, too enclosed, or leaves left on the buried part of the stem. Use
a grittier mix, take the lid or bag off, and strip the lower leaves properly.

**Nothing rooted at all.** Wrong wood — too soft, or too old and brown. Take firm current-season
growth, and take more cuttings than you need.

**The plant browned from the base upwards.** Root rot from wet ground, most likely over winter.
Check where water sits in that spot before replanting.

**Leggy, sparse growth with weak scent.** Not enough light. Move it.

**A white dusty coating on the leaves indoors.** Powdery mildew, from still humid air. Improve
air movement and space plants further apart.

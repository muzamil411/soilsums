  - question: >-
      Why are my carrots forked and stubby?
    answer: >-
      Something stopped the root going down. Stones, a compacted layer, or fresh manure and
      heavy nitrogen, which make the taproot branch. Carrots want loose, stone-free soil at
      least twelve inches deep and nothing rich added before sowing. If your soil is heavy or
      shallow, grow a short stubby variety or use a deep container instead of fighting it.
  - question: >-
      Why did my carrot seed not come up?
    answer: >-
      Carrots germinate slowly — two to three weeks — and the seed is tiny and sown shallow, so
      the surface must stay damp the whole time. One dry afternoon can finish a whole row. Cover
      the drill with a board or a length of fleece to hold moisture, water gently every day, and
      be patient before writing the row off.
  - question: >-
      Do I really need to thin carrots?
    answer: >-
      Yes, and it is the step most often skipped. Unthinned carrots compete and you get a row of
      thin roots instead of a row of carrots. Thin to about three inches apart once the
      seedlings are an inch tall, and do it in the evening in damp conditions, since the crushed
      foliage smell attracts carrot rust fly.
  - question: >-
      What causes green tops on carrots?
    answer: >-
      Sunlight on the shoulder of the root, which produces chlorophyll and a bitter green band.
      It is harmless and easy to prevent: draw a little soil or mulch over the crowns as they
      swell. It is more common in light soils and in varieties that push out of the ground.
  - question: >-
      When should I harvest carrots?
    answer: >-
      From about sixty days for a young carrot, and up to eighty or more for a full-size root.
      Pull one to check rather than guessing from the tops. Carrots hold in the ground well in
      cool weather and sweeten after a frost, so in autumn there is little hurry. In warm
      weather a carrot left too long goes woody.
  - question: >-
      Can I grow carrots in a container?
    answer: >-
      Well, provided it is deep enough. Twelve inches of loose mix suits most varieties, and
      short or round types will manage in eight. A container has the advantage of being
      stone-free and easy to keep damp during that slow germination, which removes the two
      hardest parts of growing carrots.

This page has the numbers for carrots: 3 inches between plants with 12 inches between
rows, sixteen to a square foot, direct sown three weeks before your last frost, an inch
of water a week, and a fifth to a third of a pound per root in 60 to 80 days.

Carrots need one thing the numbers do not capture: twelve inches of loose, stone-free
soil with nothing rich added. The three sections below cover that, the two to three weeks
of damp surface that germination needs, and the thinning step that is skipped more often
than any other in the garden.

## Soil first

A carrot is a taproot trying to go straight down. Anything in the way makes it fork, twist or
stop. What it wants is loose, stone-free soil worked to at least twelve inches, ideally deeper.

What it does not want is richness. Fresh manure and nitrogen-heavy fertilizer both cause
branched, hairy roots — carrots are one of the few crops where adding compost right before
sowing actively hurts. Improve the bed the season before, then sow into settled soil. Phosphorus
and potassium are fine; nitrogen is the problem.

If your ground is stony or shallow, do not fight it. A raised bed or a deep container of loose
mix will give better carrots than any amount of effort on unsuitable ground, and a short,
blunt-rooted variety will manage where a long one cannot.

## Sowing

Direct sow about three weeks before your last frost date, and again every few weeks for a
continuous supply. Carrots do not transplant — the taproot is the crop, and disturbing it
defeats the point.

Sow thinly, about half an inch deep, and firm the soil gently over the drill. Then the hard
part: keeping the surface damp for the two to three weeks germination takes. A board laid over
the row, or a strip of fleece, holds moisture and makes the difference between a row and a
disappointment. Check daily and water gently with a fine rose.

Radish seed sown along the same drill comes up in days, marks the row while you wait, and is
harvested before the carrots need the space.

## Thinning and spacing

Thin to three inches apart once seedlings are an inch or so tall, with 12 inches between rows.
In a square-foot bed, sixteen carrots to a square is the right density.

Thin in the evening, in damp weather, and remove the thinnings from the bed rather than leaving
them. Crushed carrot foliage smells strongly and that smell is exactly what carrot rust fly
navigates by.

## Water and care

An inch a week, steadily. Irregular watering causes splitting: a dry spell followed by a soaking
makes the root swell faster than its skin can grow. Mulching between rows once the plants are
established evens things out and keeps the crowns covered.

Draw a little soil over the shoulders as the roots swell to prevent green tops.

## Harvest and storage

Pull a test carrot at about sixty days to see how they are doing. Loosen the soil with a fork
before pulling in heavy ground, or you will snap them.

In cool weather carrots hold in the ground for weeks and get sweeter after a frost, which makes
them one of the best crops to leave in place and harvest as needed. Where the ground freezes
hard, a thick mulch extends that considerably. For storage, twist the tops off — leaving them on
draws moisture out of the root — and keep them cold and damp.

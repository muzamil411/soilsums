Plant asparagus **crowns in spring, as soon as the soil is workable**, **12 to 18 inches apart
in rows 3 to 5 feet apart**, and take **no harvest in year one** — a light cut in year two, a
full one from year three. Good companions are tomatoes, basil and parsley; keep onions and
garlic out.

## Asparagus is a twenty-year decision

Everything else on this page follows from that. Asparagus is a perennial: you dig the bed once,
plant crowns once, and then it produces for **fifteen to twenty years** in the same spot.

That changes what matters. Siting, drainage and weed control before planting are worth far more
effort than they would be for an annual crop, because you cannot fix them later without
destroying the bed. Pick a spot at the edge of the garden where nothing will need to be dug
around it, and get the perennial weeds out before a single crown goes in.

The other consequence is patience. **Nothing is harvested in the planting year.** A light cut of
about two weeks comes in year two, and a full six to eight week harvest from year three. Cutting
earlier or longer than that starves the crown and shortens the bed's whole life — this is the
one mistake that does lasting damage.

## Asparagus companion plants

**Good neighbours, and why.**

**Tomatoes** are the classic pairing and one of the few with a plausible mechanism in both
directions: tomatoes produce solanine, which is claimed to deter asparagus beetle, while
asparagus roots release a compound reported to suppress root-knot nematodes affecting tomatoes.
The evidence is better than most companion folklore, though not conclusive. Practically, they
also occupy different times of year — asparagus is cut before tomatoes take over the space.

**Basil and parsley** are small, shallow and harvested by hand rather than dug, which is the
real test for anything grown in an asparagus bed. They also flower and draw predatory insects.

**Bad neighbours, and why.**

**Onions, garlic and leeks** are the traditional exclusion and they compete directly for the same
root zone. **Potatoes** fail a different test: they demand deep digging every year, and an
asparagus crown sits only a few inches down. Anything that needs annual cultivation is wrong
here, whatever its chemistry.

## Planting crowns

Buy **one-year-old crowns**. Two-year-old crowns cost more, transplant worse, and do not
actually fruit sooner.

Dig a trench about 8 inches deep and 12 inches wide. Mound a low ridge of soil along the bottom,
set each crown on the ridge with its roots draped down both sides like a spider, and cover with
about 2 inches of soil.

**Then backfill gradually, not all at once.** As the spears grow through, add more soil in
stages over the season until the trench is level. Filling it in immediately buries the crown too
deep to push through.

## How far apart to plant asparagus

**12 to 18 inches between crowns, with rows 3 to 5 feet apart.**

Both figures are ranges because the two extension services behind this page disagree, and the
disagreement is worth seeing rather than averaging away. University of Minnesota spaces crowns
about 12 inches apart in furrows at least 3 feet apart; University of Maryland gives 18 inches
in rows 4 to 5 feet apart. Either works — the closer spacing suits a narrow bed and the wider
one gives easier access and better airflow.

Maryland also describes a **wide bed of three rows with plants 18 inches apart in every
direction**, which suits a raised bed better than a single furrow does.

The wide row spacing reflects what the plant becomes: by midsummer the ferns are four or five
feet tall and need air moving through them. A crowded bed gets fungal disease.

Use the [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) to work out how many crowns
a bed of a given length takes. Twenty-five crowns is a reasonable starting bed for a household.

## When to plant

**In spring, as soon as the soil can be worked**, while the crowns are still dormant. They go
in early — the crown establishes roots before it is asked to produce anything above ground.

No frost offset is given here on purpose. Neither source expresses asparagus planting as a
number of weeks from the last frost: Minnesota gives a local window of early May to early June,
which is a Minnesota calendar rather than something that transfers. Judge it by the soil, not by
a date.

Autumn planting works in mild areas.

## Soil, water and feeding

Free-draining soil above everything else. Asparagus crowns rot in ground that sits wet in
winter, and that is what usually kills a bed. They prefer a pH near neutral, around **6.5 to
7.0**, which is higher than most vegetables — if your soil is acid, [how to raise soil
pH](/blog/how-to-raise-soil-ph/) covers the correction, and it is best done before planting
since you cannot work lime deeply into an established bed.

Water about **an inch a week** in the first two years while the root system establishes. A mature
bed is fairly drought-tolerant.

**Feed after the cutting season, not before.** Once you stop harvesting, the ferns run all
summer building reserves for next year's spears, and that is the growth worth supporting. A
spring feed before cutting mostly feeds the weeds you cannot hoe out without damaging crowns.
[How to add nitrogen to soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/) covers which materials release
on a useful timescale.

## Harvest

From year three, cut for **six to eight weeks** and then stop.

Cut or snap spears at ground level when they are about 7 to 9 inches tall and the tip is still
tight. Once the tip opens into a fern, the spear is woody. In warm weather a bed needs picking
daily — spears go from cuttable to too far in about two days.

**Stopping on time is the whole discipline.** When spears start coming through noticeably
thinner, the crown is running down its reserves and the harvest is over for the year. Let
everything after that grow into ferns, and leave the ferns standing until they yellow in autumn
before cutting them down.

## Transplanting an established bed

It can be done, and it costs a year.

Move crowns in **early spring while fully dormant**, before any spear shows. Lift as much root
as you can — the roots run much wider than the crown suggests — and replant immediately at the
same depth in prepared ground. Do not let the roots dry out between lifting and replanting.

Expect little or nothing from the following season while the plant re-establishes. This is worth
doing to rescue a bed in genuinely the wrong place, and not worth doing to tidy the garden.

## Common problems

**Thin spears.** Either the bed is young, or it has been over-cut. Stop harvesting and let the
ferns run — a crown rebuilds over a season or two if you let it.

**Asparagus beetle.** Small, dark, striped beetles that chew spears and ferns. Hand-picking is
effective on a garden-scale bed; clearing the old ferns away in autumn removes overwintering
sites.

**Weeds in the bed.** The recurring problem of a perennial crop, because you cannot dig. Mulch
heavily and hand-weed, and get the perennial weeds out before planting.

**Crowns rotting.** Wet ground. Not fixable in place — the answer is drainage or a raised bed,
decided before planting.

Asparagus shares its bed well with [tomatoes](/crops/tomato/), and its preference for a nearly
neutral pH with [cabbage](/crops/cabbage/) and the other brassicas.

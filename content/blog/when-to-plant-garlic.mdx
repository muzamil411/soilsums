Garlic is the vegetable garden's rule-breaker: while almost everything else goes into the ground in spring, garlic is planted in fall for harvest the following summer. Knowing when to plant garlic is the single biggest factor in how large the bulbs grow — get the timing right and the cloves grow a strong root system before the soil freezes, rest through winter, and swell into full bulbs by midsummer. The shared rule across every university extension source is simple: roots established before the ground freezes, shoots staying below ground until spring. So when to plant garlic comes down to soil temperature and your freeze date, not a fixed calendar day.

## When to plant garlic by zone

Plant too early and tender top growth emerges before winter and gets damaged; plant too late and the roots never develop before the freeze. The question of when to plant garlic has the same answer everywhere: it depends on your soil, not your state. South Dakota State University Extension gives the most portable rule — wait until the soil has cooled to about 50°F at four inches deep. For South Dakota that lands two to three weeks after the first frost, around mid-October, but because the rule keys off soil temperature rather than a date, it travels to any climate ([source](https://extension.sdstate.edu/planting-garlic)).

Regional extension timings cluster around the same window:

- **Iowa State University Extension:** October to early November ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden))
- **Penn State Extension:** mid- to late October ([source](https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic))
- **University of Illinois Extension:** mid-October is "about the perfect time" for central Illinois, giving cloves time to root and begin sprouting before freezing temperatures set in ([source](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/garden-scoop/2021-10-09-fall-garlic-planting))
- **University of Vermont Extension:** late October, about a month before the ground freezes — timing that lets roots establish while keeping shoots below ground level ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic))

In mild-winter regions the same soil-temperature rule pushes planting later into the season: plant when the soil cools to the target, not when the calendar says October. That is the whole answer to when to plant garlic: roots down before the freeze, shoots up after it. ## Start with the right cloves

Buy seed garlic — bulbs sold for planting — from a local grower, garden center, or seed catalog. Do not plant cloves from the grocery store. Iowa State University Extension warns that grocery-store garlic is usually softneck cultivars from warmer regions that are not adapted to cold climates, and is often treated to prevent sprouting, so it may rot before it grows ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden)). South Dakota State University Extension adds that grocery-store garlic may harbor bacterial, viral, or fungal diseases that do not affect cooking quality but will prevent the crop from developing properly ([source](https://extension.sdstate.edu/planting-garlic)). The University of Illinois Extension notes that seed garlic typically gives better quality at harvest, since supermarket varieties may have been bred for storage life at the expense of taste ([source](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/garden-scoop/2021-10-09-fall-garlic-planting)).

Size matters twice. Every source agrees: the largest cloves produce the largest bulbs, so sort your seed garlic and plant the biggest cloves. And do not break bulbs into individual cloves until just before planting — the University of Illinois Extension reports that early separation has been shown to reduce mature bulb size at harvest. When you do divide them, keep the papery peel and the basal plate (the old root end) intact; South Dakota State University Extension warns that peeling exposes cloves to fungal diseases and rot if the soil gets wet.

## How deep and how far apart to plant garlic

Depth is the next decision after when to plant garlic, and it tracks winter severity — the colder the climate, the deeper the clove:

- **1 to 1.5 inches deep** — Iowa State University Extension ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden))
- **1 to 2 inches deep** — Penn State Extension and the University of Illinois Extension ([source](https://extension.psu.edu/growing-and-using-garlic))
- **About 4 inches deep** — University of Vermont Extension ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic))
- **4 to 6 inches deep** — South Dakota State University Extension, where the extra depth helps cloves overwinter without hard freezing ([source](https://extension.sdstate.edu/planting-garlic))

Every source agrees on the orientation: pointy end up, root end down. A clove planted upside down will still grow, but it wastes energy reorienting itself.

Spacing is more forgiving. Iowa State University Extension recommends 3 to 5 inches apart in rows 18 to 24 inches apart; Penn State Extension says 6 to 7 inches apart in rows a foot apart; the University of Vermont Extension plants 8 inches apart; South Dakota State University Extension and the University of Illinois Extension both note that a block or grid layout — cloves 4 to 5 inches apart in all directions — saves space in small gardens. Firm the soil after planting so no air pockets remain around the cloves, and water thoroughly to settle them in.

## Prepare the bed

Once you have settled when to plant garlic, prepare the bed a few weeks ahead: garlic wants full sun and fertile, well-drained soil high in organic matter. Iowa State University Extension cautions that garlic grown in heavy clay often produces misshapen bulbs, and recommends improving clay soil with compost or well-rotted manure ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden)). South Dakota State University Extension advises cultivating at least eight inches deep and working compost into the soil before planting, warning that garlic does not tolerate hard-packed ground ([source](https://extension.sdstate.edu/planting-garlic)). Two more bed-prep details from extension sources. The University of Vermont Extension gives garlic's preferred soil pH as 6.2 to 7.0, with lime, phosphorus, and potassium added per soil-test recommendations before planting ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic)). And South Dakota State University Extension recommends not planting where onions or garlic have grown in the past three years, to reduce soil-borne disease problems.

## Mulch after planting

Once you have settled when to plant garlic and the cloves are in the ground, mulch is what carries them through winter. A winter mulch is standard practice in every northern source. Iowa State University Extension recommends mulching fall-planted garlic in November with a 4- to 6-inch layer of weed-seed-free straw to prevent winter injury, then moving the straw between the rows in early spring so the foliage can emerge ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden)). South Dakota State University Extension goes heavier — 6 to 8 inches of straw or leaf mulch applied once the soil has frozen, which prevents frost heave during freeze-thaw cycles ([source](https://extension.sdstate.edu/planting-garlic)). Penn State Extension suggests a light straw mulch to prevent winter heaving and suppress weeds, while the University of Vermont Extension recommends a thick layer of clean straw after planting ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic)).

One caution from Vermont: in a wet spring, mulch can keep the soil from drying out and encourage soil-borne disease — if the ground stays saturated, pull the mulch away from the plants.

## Hardneck or softneck?

The two types suit different gardens. **Hardneck** garlic grows a hard central flower stalk, produces four to five large, easy-to-peel cloves per head, and has the stronger flavor; it grows best in colder climates with a cool, damp spring (South Dakota State University Extension). Iowa State University Extension adds that hardnecks do not store as long as softnecks. **Softneck** garlic — the braidable kind in grocery stores — has no stalk, packs up to 15–18 smaller cloves per head, tastes milder, and stores well (South Dakota State University Extension). The University of Vermont Extension's advice for northern gardeners: choose varieties hardy and adapted to your region, from a local grower or a seed company that serves your climate, then save your largest, healthiest bulbs to build your own seed stock. Whichever type you choose, when to plant garlic in the fall follows the same soil-temperature rule — the timing does not change with the variety, only the variety's winter hardiness matters.

## Spring care and harvest

Garlic is a heavier feeder than many vegetables. Iowa State University Extension recommends working 1 to 2 pounds of an all-purpose garden fertilizer such as 10-10-10 per 100 square feet into the bed before planting, plus one more pound per 100-foot row banded four inches to the side of the plants three to four weeks after they emerge in spring ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden)). The University of Vermont Extension instead top-dresses about 3 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet when shoots are about six inches high, and Penn State Extension simply says to fertilize in March ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic)). South Dakota State University Extension adds one restraint: do not fertilize at planting time.

Water about an inch per week during dry weather, and stop irrigating in late July so the foliage can die down before harvest — Iowa State University Extension notes this also reduces disease spread and staining on the bulb wrappers. Keep weeds down with shallow cultivation or mulch between the rows; garlic's shallow roots compete poorly.

If you grow hardneck types, remove the scapes — the curled flower stalks — while they are young. Penn State, Iowa State, and South Dakota State extensions all agree this directs the plant's energy into the bulb rather than flowers and bulbils. The scapes are edible, with a mild garlic flavor good in pesto and stir-fries.

Harvest cues are consistent across sources: Iowa State University Extension picks when plants show 4 to 5 dry lower leaves with only 3 to 4 green upper leaves (July or August in Iowa); Penn State Extension harvests in early to mid-July when leaves begin to brown but at least four are still partly green; the University of Vermont Extension waits until the lower third of leaves yellows, usually in late July. Dig bulbs with a fork or spade rather than pulling the stems, cure them in a warm, dry, well-ventilated spot for a few weeks, then trim tops an inch above the bulb. Properly dried garlic stores three to six months at 32 to 40°F and 65–70% relative humidity (Iowa State University Extension). Set aside your largest, most uniform bulbs — that is next year's seed garlic.

## Can you plant garlic in the spring?

If you missed the fall window for when to plant garlic, spring planting is possible but disappointing. The University of Vermont Extension explains why: garlic requires a cold treatment to induce bulbing, so cloves planted in spring typically produce only a small single bulb without cloves ([source](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-great-garlic)). Iowa State University Extension allows spring planting as soon as the ground can be worked — late March to early April in central Iowa — but is blunt that the highest yields come from the largest cloves planted in the fall ([source](http://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-garlic-home-garden)). Small cloves planted late can also produce unsegmented "rounds" — a single solid bulb instead of a head of cloves. If you must plant in spring, do it at the earliest workable date and keep expectations modest.

## Worked example: a mid-October planting

This worked example puts when to plant garlic on October 20 for a hypothetical central-Iowa garden. Treat every date below as a hypothetical illustration, not a forecast — substitute your own frost dates.

| Step | Timing | Detail |
| ---- | ------ | ------ |
| Check soil | ~October 18 | Soil thermometer reads about 50°F at 4 inches deep (the South Dakota State rule); first fall frost was around October 10 |
| Divide bulbs | October 20, just before planting | Break into cloves now, not earlier (Illinois); keep the largest, peels intact |
| Plant | October 20 | 1.5 inches deep, pointy end up, 4 inches apart in rows 18 inches apart (Iowa State/Illinois ranges); firm soil, water in |
| Mulch | Early November | 5 inches of weed-seed-free straw (Iowa State's 4–6 inch range) |
| Spring | At emergence | Pull straw back between rows; side-dress per Iowa State's schedule; water 1 inch/week in dry spells |
| Scapes | June | Snap off young curled scapes (hardneck types) |
| Harvest | July | Dig when 4–5 lower leaves are dry and 3–4 upper leaves still green; cure, then store at 32–40°F |

Get when to plant garlic right and the rest of the crop largely takes care of itself: the right cloves, at the right depth, under a blanket of straw, with one spring feeding. Few vegetables repay so little effort with so much flavor.

Most feeding advice is given as a calendar. That is the wrong frame. A plant needs different
things at different stages of its own development, and those stages arrive at different times
depending on your season. Feed according to what the plant is doing and the timing takes care of
itself.

## Start with a soil test, once

This is not a delaying tactic, it is the thing that makes all the rest sensible. A test tells
you whether your soil is short of phosphate and potash, which accumulate, or only of nitrogen,
which leaches away each year.

Most established gardens have plenty of phosphate. Many have adequate potash. Almost all need
nitrogen. If you feed a balanced fertilizer every year without testing, you are adding two
nutrients you probably do not need in order to deliver the one you do.

A test through an extension service costs a few pounds or dollars and lasts three years.

## The three stages of feeding

**At planting.** Work compost into the bed, and a starter fertilizer if the soil test calls for
phosphate. Phosphate moves very slowly through soil, so unlike nitrogen it needs to be where the
roots will be, not on the surface.

**During vegetative growth.** This is when nitrogen matters most: the plant is building leaves
and stems and it can use everything you give it. Side-dress along the row every three to four
weeks and water it in.

**At flowering and fruiting.** Here the emphasis shifts. For fruiting crops, ease back on
nitrogen and lean on potassium. Continued heavy nitrogen at this stage gives you a magnificent
plant with very little on it — the most common feeding mistake in a vegetable garden.

## By crop group

**Fruiting crops** — tomatoes, peppers, aubergines, cucumbers, squash. Moderate nitrogen early,
then switch to a lower-nitrogen, higher-potassium feed once fruit has set. Feed every three to
four weeks; they crop over months and will use it.

**Leafy crops** — lettuce, spinach, chard, kale. Nitrogen throughout, since leaf is the crop.
Light and frequent beats one heavy dose, which can make leaves soft and attractive to pests.

**Root crops** — carrots, beets, parsnips, radishes. Go easy, and avoid fresh manure and heavy
nitrogen entirely. Both cause forked, hairy roots. Phosphorus and potassium matter more here
than nitrogen.

**Legumes** — beans and peas. No nitrogen at all. They fix their own through root bacteria, and
a nitrogen feed gives foliage instead of pods. Phosphate and potash only, if a test says so.

**Brassicas** — broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale. Hungry, and they want steady nitrogen
through the whole season plus even moisture. Stress produces small, loose heads that bolt.

**Corn** — a heavy nitrogen feeder. Side-dress when knee high and again at tasselling.

**Garlic and onions** — nitrogen early for leaf growth, then stop once bulbs begin to swell.
Continued feeding at that stage gives soft bulbs that store badly.

## Side-dressing, practically

Side-dressing means putting fertilizer alongside a growing plant rather than mixing it into the
whole bed.

Draw a shallow furrow three to six inches out from the stem, scatter the fertilizer along it,
cover it with soil and water it in. Keep granules off the stems and the leaves — a granule
resting against a stem will scorch it.

Water is not optional here. Dry fertilizer on dry soil does nothing, and can draw moisture out of
roots. If rain is not coming, water it in.

## How much

Work from a rate, not a handful. A common figure for a mixed vegetable plot is one pound of
actual nitrogen per 1,000 square feet across the season, split into two or three applications.
Converting that into pounds of whatever product you bought is one line of arithmetic, and the
[fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) will do it and also show you what
phosphate and potash the same dose delivers.

Weigh it. Densities differ enough between products that a cup of one is not a cup of another, and
a kitchen scale removes the guesswork.

## When to stop

Stop feeding nitrogen a few weeks before your final harvest. Late nitrogen delays maturity,
softens tissue and makes crops store badly. For onions and garlic in particular it produces
bulbs that rot in storage.

For perennials and overwintering crops, stop by late summer so growth hardens off before cold
weather arrives.

## Compost changes the arithmetic

A bed that receives an inch of compost every year needs much less bought fertilizer than one
that does not. Compost supplies a little of everything, improves structure and water holding,
and feeds the microbial life that makes soil nutrients available in the first place.

It will not usually supply enough nitrogen on its own for a heavy feeder through a long season.
But it moves the baseline considerably, and it is the one amendment that improves a soil rather
than just feeding a crop.

## The short version

Test once. Compost every year. Nitrogen while plants are growing leaves, potassium once they are
setting fruit, and nothing at all for beans and peas. Side-dress every three or four weeks and
water it in. Stop a few weeks before the end.

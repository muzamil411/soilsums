An amaryllis in full bloom looks like it should be hard work — a bulb the size of a fist, a stalk as tall as your forearm, flowers as wide as your hand — and then it does it all again next year if you let it. The whole of how to care for amaryllis fits in one sentence: plant the bulb shallow in a snug pot, keep it warm and barely moist until it grows, then give the leaves a long sunny summer and the bulb a cool rest in the fall. That is the entire cycle, and every section below is that sentence unpacked with the numbers the extension services actually give.

This guide follows the extension sources, not the plant tag. Everything below comes from five sources — Iowa State, Illinois, and Clemson extensions, NC State Extension, and the ASPCA — verified on the source pages themselves. Where the sources disagree — and they do, on how deep to plant the bulb, how long the rest should be, and how often to feed — you will see the spread, and the planning figure this guide uses.

Before the care details: the amaryllis on your windowsill is not, botanically, an amaryllis at all. The bulbs sold for the holidays are *Hippeastrum* hybrids, native to Central and South America, per the University of Illinois Extension. The true *Amaryllis* is a different plant, the belladonna lily, native to South Africa. The name stuck anyway, and this guide uses it the way the shops do — but knowing which plant you have matters, because the South American bulb wants a warm start and a cool rest, and that rhythm is the whole of how to care for amaryllis.

## How to care for amaryllis: the five rules

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember these five rules — they are the whole of how to care for amaryllis in one paragraph each.

**Plant it shallow, in a snug pot.** A pot 1 to 2 inches wider than the bulb, with drainage holes, and the top third to half of the bulb above the soil. A buried bulb rots; a pot-bound bulb blooms.

**Keep it warm and barely moist until it grows.** Water thoroughly once, then keep the mix barely moist at 70 to 75°F until you see growth. A bulb with no leaves uses almost no water, and wet mix is the fastest way to lose it.

**Give it sun once it grows, and turn it.** A sunny window once growth appears, turned every day or two so the stalk grows straight. When the flowers open, move it cooler and out of direct sun so the blooms last.

**After it blooms, grow the leaves hard.** Cut the faded stalk, keep every leaf, feed every two to four weeks, and give it sun all spring and summer. The leaves are what rebuild the bulb for next year.

**Rest it cool in the fall.** Fifty to 55°F for 8 to 10 weeks, dry and dark, then water and warmth to wake it. That rest is what times the next flowers for winter.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules does three things a neglected one does not. First, it flowers on time: potted in early to mid-November, a Dutch-grown bulb blooms in 6 to 8 weeks, per Iowa State University Extension — flowers for Christmas, not a stalk of leaves in February. Second, its flowers last: kept cool once open, the blooms hold for three to four weeks, per NC State Extension, instead of collapsing in a week on a warm mantelpiece. Third, it comes back. A bulb that gets a summer of leaf growth and a cool fall rest can bloom again the next winter — and the year after that. The five rules in how to care for amaryllis are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the bulb's own calendar, followed indoors.

## Is yours a Hippeastrum? Check before you plan

The bulb in the shops in November is almost always a *Hippeastrum* hybrid — a plant bred from Central and South American species, per the University of Illinois Extension, which counts around 90 species in the genus. NC State Extension puts it higher, at over 100 species and hundreds of cultivars, and the difference is counting, not a contradiction: whatever the exact number, the holiday bulb is a hybrid, bred for big flowers in a small pot. The flowers are the point: trumpet-shaped, 4 to 10 inches across, on hollow stalks 1 to 2 feet long, per the University of Illinois Extension. Iowa State University Extension gives the stalk as 18 to 30 inches tall and the flowers as 8 to 10 inches across at their largest. Each bulb produces one or two flower stalks, and each stalk produces two to five flowers that open in quick succession, per Clemson Cooperative Extension; NC State Extension gives two to six flowers per stalk. Colors run red, pink, orange, salmon, white, and bicolors, single or double, with miniature varieties too. Knowing the flower count helps you judge the bulb in the shop — a bulb that promises two stalks is a bulb worth the price, and that judgment is a small part of how to care for amaryllis.

### Hippeastrum vs. true amaryllis

The true *Amaryllis* is a different plant, and confusing the two is the one naming mistake that changes the care. True amaryllis, the belladonna lily, is native to South Africa, per the University of Illinois Extension, and is usually grown outdoors in mild climates — it is the plant in the genus *Amaryllis*, of which there are only two species, per NC State Extension. The holiday bulb is *Hippeastrum*, from tropical and subtropical areas of the Americas, per Iowa State University Extension. If your bulb came in a box or a kit from a garden center in November, it is the holiday kind, and everything in how to care for amaryllis applies to it. Do not plant it in the garden expecting the belladonna lily's habits — in most of the United States it will not survive the winter outdoors, and the section on planting outside explains why.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Two numbers decide how good a bulb will be, and both are visible in the shop. The first is size: amaryllis bulbs run 2 to 5 inches in diameter, per the University of Illinois Extension, and size matters because larger bulbs hold more stored energy, produce more flowers, and grow thicker, stronger stems. Iowa State University Extension says the largest bulbs usually produce 2 or 3 flower stalks. The second is firmness: the bulb should feel solid and heavy, with no mold, decay, or injury, per the University of Illinois Extension. A soft or light bulb has already spent its reserves or started to rot, and no amount of care will bring it back. Buy the biggest, firmest bulb you can find — it is the cheapest upgrade in how to care for amaryllis, and it is decided at the till, not at the windowsill.

## Choosing a bulb that will actually flower

Not every amaryllis bulb blooms, and the check that catches the duds takes ten seconds in the shop. Clemson Cooperative Extension says it is critical to purchase bulbs with a visible flower bud to ensure that the bulb flowers. On a vigorous bulb, the flower buds emerge before the leaves. If only leaves are coming out of the center of the bulb, do not expect flower buds to follow. The buds emerge off center on the top of the bulb, one on each side of the neck. Flower buds are triangular, thicker than leaf buds, and have a faint line down the middle.

That check is worth more than any brand or price tag, because a bulb without a bud is a bulb that will give you leaves and nothing else this winter. Leaf buds are thin and flat; flower buds are fat and triangular. Look for the fat triangle on each side of the neck, and if you cannot see one, pick another bulb. A bulb that has already started to bloom in the shop is also a poor buy for the same reason in reverse — you are paying for flowers someone else will enjoy, and a waxed bulb that has already started to bloom may barely flower at all at home, per Iowa State University Extension. Choose a bulb that is still asleep, firm, and showing a bud, and the rest of how to care for amaryllis is straightforward. That ten-second check is the best money you will spend on how to care for amaryllis.

### Kits, pre-potted bulbs, and waxed bulbs

Most amaryllis arrive in one of three forms, and the form decides the first job. A loose bulb needs potting, covered in the next section. A pre-potted bulb is ready to go, but check the pot before you water it: Iowa State University Extension says that if the container does not have drainage holes, remove the bulb, drill small holes in the bottom, and replant — a pot with no drainage is a bulb that will rot. A kit bulb comes with a compressed disk of coir to use as potting media; Iowa State University Extension says the disk frequently does not provide enough potting media to pot the bulb adequately, and it is usually easier to forget the disk and use general, all-purpose, well-drained potting soil. Clemson Cooperative Extension adds that you should replace the plastic pot in a kit with a ceramic pot, because the heavier pot counterbalances the weight of the flowering bulb and stops it tipping over. Swap the pot before you water, and the kit bulb starts on the same footing as a loose one — that swap is the first job in how to care for amaryllis. Waxed bulbs are a third form entirely — no pot, no soil, no water — and they get their own section below, because the care is different and the expectations should be too.

## Planting the bulb, step by step

Planting is the one step where a small error costs the whole season, because a bulb planted too deep rots before it ever grows. Follow the sources in order, and the bulb does the rest.

### Pick the pot

Select a pot approximately 1 to 2 inches wider than the diameter of the bulb, per Iowa State University Extension. The University of Illinois Extension gives the same idea more tightly — a pot with drainage holes about one inch wider than the bulb, and ideally about twice as tall as the bulb, so there is adequate space for root development. Clemson Cooperative Extension says only that the container should be slightly larger than the bulb. The planning figure this guide uses is 1 to 2 inches wider than the bulb: every source agrees the bulb likes to be slightly snug, and a pot much larger than that stays wet too long. A snug pot is the first rule of how to care for amaryllis, and it is decided here. The container may be clay, ceramic, or plastic, per Iowa State University Extension, but it must have drainage holes in the bottom. A heavy ceramic pot is worth choosing over a light plastic one, because Clemson Cooperative Extension warns that the flowering bulb is top-heavy and a heavier pot counterbalances it — flower stalks may need staking even in a good pot.

### Set the depth

This is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. Iowa State University Extension says that when finished potting, the upper one-half of the bulb should remain above the soil surface. The University of Illinois Extension says to plant the bulb so at least the top one-third of the bulb is sticking out of the potting mixture. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to plant with the top one-third to one-half of the bulb exposed above the soil. NC State Extension says to bury only half the bulb below the surface, with the narrow part of the bulb — the neck — well above the soil line. The planning figure is the top third to half above the soil: that range satisfies all four sources, and it keeps the neck dry, which is the point. Burying the neck causes rot, and a bulb planted with its nose underground is a bulb that will not flower. Get the depth right and the rest of how to care for amaryllis is easy; get it wrong and nothing else matters. Leave about one inch between the soil surface and the pot's rim, per Iowa State University Extension and the University of Illinois Extension, so water does not spill out while watering.

### Fill and water

Plant the bulb in a well-drained potting mix — the University of Illinois Extension specifies a soil-less, well-draining potting mix. Put a small amount of mix in the bottom of the pot, center the bulb, and add mix around the roots and bulb, firming it gently, per Iowa State University Extension. Our [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs, so you buy one bag instead of three. Then water thoroughly and allow the excess water to drain from the pot — the University of Illinois Extension suggests doing this in a sink — and place the plant in a warm spot at 70 to 75°F, per Iowa State University Extension. That warmth is what starts the roots and the growth, and a cool room will leave the bulb sitting still for weeks. Warmth plus drainage is the combination that starts how to care for amaryllis on the right foot.

### Then wait — and water barely

After the initial watering, water just enough to keep the potting mix barely moist, per Iowa State University Extension. This is the step most people get wrong in how to care for amaryllis, because the instinct is to water a newly potted plant generously. A bulb with no leaves uses almost no water, and a bulb sitting in wet mix rots from the base up. Restraint now is the hardest part of how to care for amaryllis, because the bulb looks like it needs help and it does not. Keep it barely moist until growth appears, and only then water more. If you planted in early to mid-November, that growth should show within a couple of weeks, and the flowers follow on the schedule in the next section. That patience — one thorough watering, then restraint — is the cheapest insurance in how to care for amaryllis.

### A worked example: potting one bulb today

Here is the planting as a single job, for a bulb bought on Monday, 16 November 2026. The bulb is a Dutch-grown 'Red Lion', 4 inches across, firm, with a fat triangular bud on each side of the neck. The pot is 6 inches across — 2 inches wider than the bulb — and about 8 inches tall, which is twice the bulb's height, per the University of Illinois Extension, with drainage holes in the bottom. A snug pot and the right depth are the two decisions that make how to care for amaryllis easy from here on. You put an inch of well-drained potting mix in the bottom, center the bulb, and fill around it until the top third of the bulb is above the mix, leaving an inch of headroom to the rim. You water it thoroughly in the sink, let it drain, and set it in a warm spot at 70 to 75°F. For the next two weeks you water only enough to keep the mix barely moist, per Iowa State University Extension. Growth appears, you move it to a sunny window, and because it is a Dutch-grown bulb, the flowers follow in 6 to 8 weeks — late December into early January, in time for Christmas and the New Year. That is the whole first act of how to care for amaryllis: one afternoon of potting, two weeks of restraint, and six to eight weeks of waiting.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Once growth appears, the care changes: the bulb stops being a storage organ you are trying not to rot, and starts being a plant that wants light, water, and warmth in that order.

### Light

Move the plant to a sunny window when growth appears, per Iowa State University Extension. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to keep plants in bright indirect sunlight until blooming starts. The University of Illinois Extension says to place the plant in a sunny window after potting, and after flowering, a window with southern exposure is likely the best place. The practical reading is a bright window, south-facing if you have one, with the plant turned frequently to keep the stalk growing straight — Iowa State University Extension says to turn plants frequently, and during flower stalk elongation, to turn the pot daily. A stalk that leans toward the window is a stalk telling you it wants more light, and turning the pot every day or two is the fix that costs nothing. Turning the pot is the smallest daily job in how to care for amaryllis, and the one that keeps the display straight. If the stalk leans badly, stake it, per Iowa State University Extension — a thin stake and a soft tie, placed before the stalk gets tall enough to flop.

### Water

The rule changes with the growth stage, and the sources agree on the direction even though they phrase it differently. Before growth, barely moist — Iowa State University Extension. Once growth appears, water more frequently — Iowa State University Extension. The University of Illinois Extension gives the check to use: wait to water until the top two inches of soil feel dry, and keep the soil moist but not soggy. In bloom, Clemson Cooperative Extension says the potting mix should be moist but not wet, and to allow the top of the potting mix to dry before watering. After flowering, Iowa State University Extension says to water when the soil surface is nearly dry. The planning figure is the finger test: water when the top two inches are dry while the plant is growing and flowering, keep it barely moist before growth starts, and never let the pot sit in a saucer of water. That one rule, applied at the right stage, is most of how to care for amaryllis. Empty the saucer every time — standing water undoes everything else in how to care for amaryllis.

### Temperature

Warm to start, cooler to bloom — that is the whole temperature story in how to care for amaryllis. Iowa State University Extension says to place the newly potted bulb in a warm spot at 70 to 75°F, and when the amaryllis begins to bloom, to move it to a slightly cooler location at 65 to 70°F that does not receive direct sun, to prolong the life of the flowers. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same in fewer words: bright indirect sunlight until blooming starts, then moderate light once in bloom. The University of Illinois Extension says to move the plant out of direct sunlight and into a cooler location once the flower buds start to show color, because moving it helps retain the blossoms for a longer period. Keep the plant away from radiators, heat vents, and drafty doors in both stages: heat shortens the flowers, and a cold draft checks the growth. The temperature split — 70 to 75°F to grow, 65 to 70°F to bloom — is the detail that turns a week of flowers into three or four, and it is the most useful number in how to care for amaryllis.

## While it is in bloom

The flowers are the payoff, and the care in bloom is mostly about not undoing them. Move the plant to its cooler, shadier spot as soon as the buds show color, per the University of Illinois Extension, and keep it there while the flowers are open. Water when the top of the mix dries, keep the mix moist but not wet, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and empty the saucer every time. Snip off each flower as it fades, and the University of Illinois Extension explains why: cut the faded flowers off to prevent seed formation, because allowing seeds to form depletes the bulb's energy and can lead to reduced blooming in subsequent years. That is a small job with a large return — a plant allowed to set seed is a plant spending next year's flowers on this year's seed pod.

### How long the flowers last

How long will it last? NC State Extension says the large, showy blooms last for three to four weeks, which is the planning figure to use. The spread in timing is the same as the spread in planting: Iowa State University Extension says South African-grown bulbs typically bloom 3 to 5 weeks after potting and Dutch-grown bulbs typically bloom 6 to 8 weeks after potting, while Clemson Cooperative Extension gives 6 to 8 weeks from planting and NC State Extension says bulbs potted between mid-autumn and late winter bloom approximately five weeks later. Those numbers are not in conflict — they describe different bulbs. Most bulbs sold in the United States are Dutch-grown, so 6 to 8 weeks is the figure to plan around, and a bulb potted in early to mid-November should be in flower for Christmas, per Iowa State University Extension. That timing is the payoff for getting the planting right, and it is the date to plan around in how to care for amaryllis. A bulb that is still only leaves after eight weeks has a different problem, covered in troubleshooting, and it is usually a bulb that was never going to flower or one that spent the summer in the dark. Enjoy the flowers where they are, keep them cool, and do not move the plant back into the hot sun until the last flower has faded — that return to the sun is the first step of the next section, and the most important one in how to care for amaryllis.

## After the flowers fade: the leaves do the work

This is the section most people skip, and it is the section that decides whether the bulb ever flowers again. The flowers are over, but the plant is not done — it has just spent a large part of its stored food, and the leaves are the only way to put it back.

### Cut the stalk, keep the leaves

After the flowers fade, cut off the flower stalk with a sharp knife or pruners, making the cut 1 to 2 inches above the bulb, per Iowa State University Extension — and do not damage the foliage. The University of Illinois Extension adds a timing detail worth following: wait to remove the flower stalk until it begins to yellow, because the flower stalk can photosynthesize, like leaves, and create energy and food that will be stored for future flower growth. Either way, the rule for the leaves is absolute: leave them. Iowa State University Extension says the strap-like leaves manufacture food for the plant, and the plant must replenish its depleted food reserves in order to bloom again next season. The University of Illinois Extension says the same — as with other bulbs, like tulips and daffodils, the leaves are needed to create energy and food so the plants can bloom again. A bulb with its leaves cut off is a bulb that will not flower next year, however well you water it. Keeping the leaves is the non-negotiable rule in how to care for amaryllis after flowering.

### Feed and water through the growing season

Place the plant in a sunny window and water when the soil surface is nearly dry, per Iowa State University Extension, and fertilize every 2 to 4 weeks with a dilute fertilizer solution — a balanced all-purpose fertilizer at half strength works well. The University of Illinois Extension gives a lighter schedule — fertilize monthly with an all-purpose houseplant fertilizer, following the directions on the label — and the planning figure this guide uses is every 2 to 4 weeks at half strength while the plant is actively growing, which sits inside both sources. Keep this up all spring and summer: the leaves made over those months are what fill the bulb with energy for the next flowers, and a plant starved in summer is a plant with leaves and no flowers in winter. That is the deal at the center of how to care for amaryllis — the flowers you see in December are paid for by the leaves you grow in June. Feed on schedule, water on the finger test, and the bulb will do the rest — that is the whole summer routine in how to care for amaryllis.

### Give it a summer outside

The amaryllis can be moved outdoors in late May, per Iowa State University Extension. Harden it first: place it in a shady, protected area, and after 2 or 3 days, gradually expose it to longer periods of direct sun. Once hardened, select a site in part sun, dig a hole, and set the pot into the ground. Outdoors, continue to water during dry weather, and continue to fertilize once or twice a month through July. The University of Illinois Extension gives the same move with a brighter target: take the plant outdoors once there is no longer a risk of frost, place it in filtered sunlight at first, then gradually move it to an area where it will get a minimum of six hours of sunlight a day. Bring the plant indoors in mid-September, per Iowa State University Extension — or, per the University of Illinois Extension, before the first frost. Plants left indoors should be kept in a sunny window and watered and fertilized in the same way as those grown outdoors. A summer outside is not required, but it is the easiest way to give the leaves the light they need, and a bulb that spent the summer in a bright garden is a bulb that comes back into the house fat and ready to rest. A summer outside is the easiest version of how to care for amaryllis through the growing season. That summer is the quiet half of how to care for amaryllis, and it is where the second bloom is actually made.

## How to get amaryllis to bloom again

Reblooming is the part everyone asks about, and the honest answer is that it is a two-step process: a summer of growth, then a cool rest. Skip either step and the bulb will grow leaves and no flowers.

### Step one: a summer of growth

The bulb must rebuild its food reserves before it can flower again, and only the leaves can do that. Iowa State University Extension says that when growth resumes, some bulbs produce foliage but no flowers, because they have not been able to store adequate food reserves in their bulbs in spring and summer — and the fix is to fertilize at a higher rate or more frequently in the spring and summer months, and to be sure to provide adequate light. If your bulb grew leaves all summer and still did not flower, it almost always needed more sun and more food, not a different rest. Get the summer right first, and the rest does the rest. That is the order of operations in how to care for amaryllis: summer first, rest second.

### Step two: the cold

In order to bloom, amaryllis bulbs must be exposed to temperatures of 50 to 55°F for a minimum of 8 to 10 weeks, per Iowa State University Extension. NC State Extension gives the same numbers: move the plant to a cool place at 50 to 55°F, semi-dark, and let it be dormant for 8 to 10 weeks, then move it to a well-lit, warm place at 70 to 75°F and resume watering. The University of Illinois Extension gives a slightly wider rest — a cool place at 45 to 55°F, dark, for approximately 8 to 12 weeks, with no water during that time — and adds the important qualification that amaryllis do not require a dormant period to rebloom at all. The rest is how you control when the flowers come, not whether they come. The planning figure is 50 to 55°F for 8 to 10 weeks, which satisfies Iowa State and NC State and sits inside the Illinois range. That range is the number to write on the calendar in how to care for amaryllis.

### Two ways to give it the cold

Iowa State University Extension describes both, and you should pick the one that fits your house. The first is a proper rest: in late September, place the plant in a cool, semi-dark location and withhold water. Cut off the foliage when the leaves turn brown. Then place the dormant bulb in a 50 to 55°F location for at least 8 to 10 weeks. After the cool requirement has been met, start the growth cycle again by watering the bulb and placing it in a well-lighted, 70 to 75°F location. The second skips the dry rest: place the plant in a well-lit, 50 to 55°F location in the fall, maintain it as a green plant from fall until early to mid-winter, and after the cool requirement has been met, move it to a warmer place at 70 to 75°F. The first method suits a house with a cool basement or garage; the second suits a house with a cool, bright room. Either way, do not water during the rest, and do not feed — a resting bulb with wet mix rots, and a resting bulb with fertilizer is a bulb being fed while it is trying to sleep. Once the leaves yellow, they can be removed, per the University of Illinois Extension, and when the bulb shows signs of growth, resume watering and fertilizing — flowers usually develop about 4 to 6 weeks after the dormant period, per the University of Illinois Extension. That 4-to-6-week count is how you time the second bloom, and it is the last number you need in how to care for amaryllis.

### The rebloom plan, step by step

If you want the plan as a checklist, here it is, following the sources exactly. In late September, move the plant to a cool, semi-dark place at 50 to 55°F and stop watering. When the leaves turn brown, cut them off. Keep the dormant bulb at 50 to 55°F for at least 8 to 10 weeks, with no water and no fertilizer, checking it now and then — if it begins to grow on its own, move it to a sunny window, per the University of Illinois Extension. After 8 to 10 weeks, water the bulb thoroughly and move it to a warm, bright spot at 70 to 75°F. Resume watering and feeding when growth shows, and expect flowers in 4 to 6 weeks. If the bulb grows leaves and no flowers, give it another summer — it has not rebuilt enough reserves yet, and Iowa State University Extension says to continue to grow and care for the bulb, because flowers should emerge the following season. Patience is the whole method: a bulb that misses one winter is not a dead bulb, it is a hungry one, and the cure is sun and food, not a new bulb. That patience is the long game in how to care for amaryllis, and it is what separates a one-season bulb from a plant that flowers for years.

### A worked example: one amaryllis, bought today

Here is the plan as a calendar, for a Dutch-grown bulb bought and potted on Monday, 16 November 2026. It is potted in early to mid-November, inside the window Iowa State University Extension gives for Christmas blooms, in a pot 2 inches wider than the bulb, with the top third of the bulb above the mix, watered once and kept warm at 70 to 75°F. Growth appears within a couple of weeks, the plant moves to a sunny window, and because it is a Dutch-grown bulb, the flowers follow in 6 to 8 weeks — late December 2026 into early January 2027, in flower for Christmas and the New Year. That first Christmas is the reward for the autumn's work, and the start of the year-long cycle in how to care for amaryllis. The blooms last three to four weeks, per NC State Extension, into late January. The faded flowers are snipped off, the stalk is cut 1 to 2 inches above the bulb once it yellows, and every leaf is kept. Through spring the plant sits in a sunny window, watered when the surface is nearly dry and fed every two to four weeks at half strength.

### Spring and summer outdoors

In late May 2027 it moves outdoors after hardening off, gets part sun building to six hours a day, and is fed once or twice a month through July. In mid-September it comes back inside, before the first frost. ### The rest, and the second bloom

In late September it goes into its rest — a cool, semi-dark spot at 50 to 55°F, no water, no food — for 8 to 10 weeks, so roughly late September into early December. In early December the bulb is watered and moved to a warm, bright spot at 70 to 75°F, and flowers follow in 4 to 6 weeks, per the University of Illinois Extension — late December 2027 into January 2028. That is the full year inside how to care for amaryllis: one winter of flowers, one summer of leaves, one cool rest, and a second winter of flowers thirteen months after you bought the bulb, costing you nothing but a sunny window and a cool corner.

## Waxed amaryllis bulbs

### What a waxed bulb is

Waxed bulbs are the no-pot version sold as gifts, and the care is different because there is almost nothing to do — which is the point, and also the catch. When bulbs are waxed, the roots are removed and a wire is inserted into the bottom of the bulb, per the University of Illinois Extension, so the bulb can stand up on its own. The bulb is then sealed in wax, often red, green, or white. Since the bulbs are sealed in wax, they do not need to be watered or placed in soil or a pot. Iowa State University Extension says the same: waxed bulbs require no water or soil, just a place in bright, indirect light, and flower stalks emerge in 4 to 6 weeks, sometimes less, using the stored carbohydrates in the large bulb. Rotate the bulb every few days to keep the stalk growing straight. Often two, sometimes three or four, flower stalks will grow, producing blooms for up to three weeks. No watering, no soil, no pot — that simplicity is the whole appeal, and the whole limit, of how to care for amaryllis in wax.

### Enjoy it as a one-season display

Enjoy that display for what it is, because it is designed to be a single season. Iowa State University Extension says waxed bulbs are intended to be grown as single-use bulbs, and when flowering is done, to discard the bulb — removing the wax before composting, because some waxes and the glitter embedded in them may not break down in a compost pile. The University of Illinois Extension says the same: these plants are typically intended to be thrown away after blooming. Do not buy a waxed bulb that has already started to bloom or has spent flower stalks, per Iowa State University Extension — it may not bloom in your home, or will have a greatly reduced length of bloom. Treat it as a bouquet with roots, and you will enjoy it exactly as it was sold — that expectation is the honest starting point for how to care for amaryllis in wax.

### Can you save a waxed bulb?

Saving a waxed bulb is possible but not guaranteed, and the sources are candid about the odds. Once the last flower stalk has faded, leaves will slowly emerge. Carefully remove the wax from the outside of the bulb, taking special care not to damage any small emerging roots at the base, per Iowa State University Extension — sometimes those roots are embedded in the wax and break off easily. You may be surprised to see that the wax shell is now larger than the bulb inside: the stored carbohydrates were used to produce the flowers, and with no water or soil available the plant had a greatly reduced ability to replace them, so the bulb shrinks significantly in size. Inspect the bulb carefully for sunken, soft, or dark areas that indicate disease, and discard any bulb that shows signs of rot.

### What to do with the saved bulb

If the bulb is still firm, the University of Illinois Extension says it can be planted and cared for like other amaryllis bulbs — pot it the same way, upper half above the soil, in a pot 1 to 2 inches wider than the bulb, and give it plenty of light and fertilizer in the spring and summer months. But Iowa State University Extension warns it is not always possible to save waxed bulbs and get them to rebloom: occasionally the bulb is so spent after flowering that it does not recover, and sometimes it grows healthy leaves but will not produce blooms the following year, because it has not built up enough carbohydrate reserves. Continue to grow and care for the bulb, and flowers should emerge the following season — or accept the waxed bulb as a one-season gift, which is what it was sold as. That acceptance is the realistic half of how to care for amaryllis when the bulb came dipped in wax.

## Troubleshooting

Most amaryllis problems have one of four causes: a bulb that was never going to flower, too much water before growth, too little light or food in summer, or a rest that was too warm or too short. Work through the list below before you reach for a spray or a repot. Each fix below is a piece of how to care for amaryllis, applied to one symptom at a time.

### Leaves but no flowers

This is the most common complaint, and the causes are specific. First, check the purchase: Clemson Cooperative Extension says it is critical to buy bulbs with a visible flower bud, and if only leaves are coming out of the center of the bulb, do not expect flower buds to follow. A bulb bought with no bud will give you leaves and nothing else this winter, and no care will change that — buy a bulb with a bud next time. Second, check the summer: Iowa State University Extension says bulbs that fail to bloom may not have been able to store adequate food reserves in their bulbs in spring and summer, and the fix is to fertilize at a higher rate or more frequently in the spring and summer months and to provide adequate light. A bulb kept in a dim room all summer, or never fed, will grow leaves and no flowers. Third, check the rest: Iowa State University Extension says bulbs occasionally fail to flower because they have not been exposed to the proper temperatures, and to provide 50 to 55°F for 8 to 10 weeks to initiate flower development. A bulb rested at room temperature has not had the cold it needs. Check in order: bud at purchase, summer light and food, then the rest temperature. One of those three is almost always the answer in how to care for amaryllis when a healthy plant refuses to flower.

### Stalk leans or flops over

A leaning stalk is almost always a light problem, and the fix is free. Iowa State University Extension says to turn plants frequently to keep the stalk growing straight, and during flower stalk elongation to turn the pot daily. A stalk that leans toward the window is growing toward the light, and a quarter-turn every day or two straightens it. If the stalk leans badly, stake it — Iowa State University Extension says to stake flower stalks that lean badly, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says flower stalks may need staking. Use a thin stake and a soft tie, and place it before the stalk gets tall enough to flop under the weight of the flowers. A heavy ceramic pot also helps: Clemson Cooperative Extension warns that the flowering bulb is top-heavy, and a light plastic pot will tip over entirely. Turning, staking, and a heavy pot — that order of fixes is the habit at the heart of how to care for amaryllis when the display starts to lean.

### Bulb does not grow, or feels soft

No growth after potting usually means the bulb is sitting in mix that is too wet or too cold. Iowa State University Extension says to water just enough to keep the mix barely moist after the first watering, and to place the newly potted bulb in a warm spot at 70 to 75°F. A bulb in a cool room will sit still for weeks, and a bulb in wet mix will rot from the base. Check the warmth first, then the moisture: move the pot somewhere warm, let the mix dry until it is barely moist, and wait. A bulb that feels soft, sunken, or dark, or smells bad, is rotting and cannot be saved — Iowa State University Extension says to discard any bulb that shows those signs. Do not try to dry it out and replant it; the rot is inside the bulb by then. Prevention is the whole story here: one thorough watering, then barely moist, then warmth. Those three preventions are the cheapest insurance in how to care for amaryllis.

### Leaves turn yellow in the fall

Yellow leaves in the fall are usually dormancy, not a problem, and the calendar is the diagnostic tool. If the leaves yellow in late September after you stopped watering, the plant is going into its rest, and the rebloom section above tells you what to do — cut the foliage when it turns brown and keep the dormant bulb cool at 50 to 55°F. Yellow leaves in winter are different: they mean the plant is too warm, too wet, or finished flowering, and the fix is to check the temperature and the watering in that order. Yellow leaves in spring, while the plant should be growing hard, mean too much water or too little light — let the surface dry before watering again, and move the plant to a brighter window. Telling fall yellowing from winter yellowing is a matter of the calendar, and the calendar is the diagnostic tool in how to care for amaryllis.

### Flowers fade in a few days

Flowers that collapse quickly were kept too warm or in too much sun while open. Iowa State University Extension says to move the plant to a slightly cooler location at 65 to 70°F that does not receive direct sun once it begins to bloom, to prolong the life of the flowers. The University of Illinois Extension says to move the plant out of direct sunlight and into a cooler location once the buds show color. A plant left in a hot, sunny window while in flower will burn through its blooms in days; the same plant in a cool, bright room holds them for three to four weeks, per NC State Extension. Keep it away from radiators and heat vents while it is in bloom, and do not move it back into the hot sun until the last flower has faded. That move — warm to grow, cool to bloom — is the temperature habit at the heart of how to care for amaryllis.

### Pests

Amaryllis do not have many pests, but check anyway. NC State Extension says mealybugs are occasional visitors, and to watch for snails and slugs if the plants are outdoors. Check under the leaves and at the base of the bulb before you bring a new plant home, and check again before you move it next to your other houseplants — your [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) and your [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) on the same windowsill will thank you. A few mealybugs can be dabbed off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. A heavy infestation on a bulb that is also failing to bloom is usually a sign to discard the bulb rather than to treat it — the bulb is cheap, the pests are not worth spreading. Checking for pests before the plant joins the collection is the inspection step in how to care for amaryllis.

## Is amaryllis poisonous to pets?

Yes — and unlike the Christmas cactus, this is a plant to keep out of reach, which is an important safety note in how to care for amaryllis. The ASPCA lists amaryllis as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, and Clemson Cooperative Extension names the toxic compounds as lycorine and other alkaloids, with symptoms of vomiting, depression, diarrhea, abdominal pain, hypersalivation, anorexia, and tremors. The bulb is the most poisonous part, so a pet that nibbles a leaf usually vomits and recovers, while a pet that eats a whole bulb is in real danger — the ASPCA notes that large ingestions can cause tremors, convulsions, low blood pressure, and heart rhythm problems. Iowa State University Extension puts it plainly too: both the bulb and the plant are poisonous. That plain fact is the safety rule in how to care for amaryllis: treat every part as poisonous, and keep the whole plant out of reach.

### Keep it out of reach

Keep the plant on a high shelf or in a room pets cannot reach, never leave a spare bulb or a discarded waxed bulb where a dog can chew it, and never leave a repotted bulb where a dog can dig it up. If a pet eats any part of the plant, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. Children are at risk too — every part of the plant is poisonous if eaten — so keep the plant out of reach of toddlers as well. That makes the amaryllis one of the most toxic plants on the holiday windowsill — compare it with the other plants on the same sill: the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is non-toxic, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating, and amaryllis and [paperwhite bulbs](/blog/how-to-force-paperwhite-bulbs/) are toxic too. The amaryllis is the one you must keep out of reach. That safety is the one real drawback of the plant in how to care for amaryllis, and it is worth knowing before you buy one for a house with a curious cat.

## The short version

How to care for amaryllis, in one paragraph: buy the biggest, firmest bulb you can find, with a visible flower bud; plant it in a pot 1 to 2 inches wider than the bulb, with drainage holes, the top third to half of the bulb above a well-drained mix, and an inch of headroom to the rim; water thoroughly once, then keep the mix barely moist at 70 to 75°F until growth appears; move it to a sunny window, turn the pot daily, and water when the top two inches are dry; when the buds show color, move it cooler, to 65 to 70°F out of direct sun, and snip off each faded flower so the plant does not set seed; after it blooms, cut the stalk 1 to 2 inches above the bulb once it yellows, keep every leaf, feed every two to four weeks at half strength, and give it sun all spring and summer, outdoors after the last frost if you can; in late September, rest the bulb cool and dark at 50 to 55°F for 8 to 10 weeks with no water, then water and warm it to 70 to 75°F for flowers in 4 to 6 weeks. Do that, and the bulb you buy this November will flower for Christmas — and, with a little patience, again next winter. If you want a second holiday plant for the same windowsill, [paperwhite bulbs](/blog/how-to-force-paperwhite-bulbs/) take only three to six weeks and need no chilling at all, and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) blooms on the same cool nights with none of the toxicity worry.

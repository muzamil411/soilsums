A dracaena is the houseplant that survives the office, and that is the first lesson in how to care for dracaena. It stands in a bright corner with long, arching leaves in green, cream, yellow and red, it grows two feet tall or ten feet tall depending on the kind, and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: bright, indirect light, a pot that drains, and water only when the surface has dried. Then the same plant sits for a month with brown, crispy tips on every leaf in the hands of an owner who waters it on time, because how to care for dracaena is not the same as keeping a plant watered. A dracaena is a tropical forest plant from Africa and Asia that grows under a canopy, and it wants bright light filtered through leaves, warm air, and a soil that drains — and it wants water without fluoride in it, which is the one thing most owners never think about. Give it those, and it stands in the corner for years with glossy leaves that get longer and brighter every spring. Give it a dark corner, a deep pot without holes, and a weekly watering from a fluoridated tap, and the tips brown from the edges in until the whole plant looks scorched.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A dracaena that needs water does not hide it: the leaves droop a little, the stems feel light, and the pot feels light when you lift it. A dracaena that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and then they drop, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its new leaves coming out narrower and paler than the old ones, because NC State Extension says if light levels are too low, the leaves will narrow. A plant moved suddenly into strong direct sun scorches in round, dry patches, and a plant that is fed too much burns at the tips and margins. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, dropping leaves, yellow leaves, narrowing leaves, and brown tips — is the whole of how to care for dracaena, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

## How to care for dracaena: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and keep it out of direct sun.** The brightest spot you can spare, an east-facing window or a few feet back from a south or west window, with bright light all day and no direct sun on the leaves, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for dracaena, because the plant is a forest plant that grows under a canopy in bright, filtered light — and a plant in a dim room survives but narrows its leaves and grows slowly, while a plant moved into direct sun in one day scorches in round, dry patches.

**Let the surface dry, then water thoroughly — and read the leaves.** Water only when the top inch of the mix has dried, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for dracaena.

**Keep it warm and steady, and never let it catch a cold draft.** Warm room air all year, a spot away from cold windows, exterior doors and heating vents, and outside only in the warm months. Cold and drafts are the third rule of how to care for dracaena — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a sudden change in temperature drops its leaves in a week.

### The last two rules in how to care for dracaena

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed in spring and summer, once a month at the most, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding does not make a dracaena grow faster; it burns the leaf tips and margins, and the salts build up in the mix. Feeding is the fourth rule of how to care for dracaena.

**Keep the fluoride out, and the water clean.** Rainwater, filtered water or distilled water, a mix without much perlite, and no superphosphate fertilizer — because dracaena is one of the few houseplants that is genuinely sensitive to fluoride, and the fluoride in a fluoridated tap, in perlite and in superphosphate browns the leaf tips from the edges in. Clean water is the fifth rule of how to care for dracaena, and it is the rule the other guides skip.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for dracaena does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stands tall and glossy: a corn plant four to five feet tall on stout tan stems, or a dragon tree up to ten feet tall with slender arching leaves edged in purple, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, with leaves that stay green to the tip instead of browning from the edges in. The five rules in how to care for dracaena are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the forest floor, followed indoors.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for dracaena, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on watering depth. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the decision that shapes the plant more than any other in how to care for dracaena, because the plant answers to it in its leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says most dracaenas grow best in bright, indirect light, but many are also tolerant of lower light, and that if a plant that has been growing in dim light is moved to a brighter spot, the new leaves will be thicker and stronger and growth rate will increase. NC State Extension says the corn plant wants bright to moderate, filtered light, that direct sun can burn the foliage, and that if light levels are too low, the leaves will narrow. NC State Extension says the same for the genus: the plants prefer bright, indirect sunlight. The spread is narrow and the direction is the same: bright, indirect light is best, direct sun burns, and shade is survived, not enjoyed. The planning figure is an east-facing window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, with bright light all day and no direct sun on the leaves. A dracaena in a north-facing window will survive and grow slowly; a plant in the same window will narrow its leaves, because the leaves need light to stay broad — and a plant moved into direct sun in one day scorches, because Clemson Cooperative Extension says round dry patches and streaks on the leaves may be caused by excessive sunlight on the foliage.

The first warning that the light is wrong is narrowing, and it comes in one form in how to care for dracaena. A corn plant in too little light does not die — it narrows. The new leaves come out thinner and paler than the old ones, the growth slows, and the plant looks thin instead of full. The fix is the same as the fix for yellow leaves: move it to a brighter spot, with no direct sun, and give it a season, because the narrow leaves will not widen again — but the new leaves will come out broad and glossy, and they are the sign the light is right. The new growth will show whether the light is right — thick, firm leaves, broad and bright — and the old narrow leaves are the record of the year in the dark.

The second warning is faster, and it is the one owners cause in how to care for dracaena. A plant moved suddenly into strong direct sun scorches in round, dry patches across the leaves that caught the sun, and the patches do not heal — move it back a few feet from the window and leave the marked leaves alone, because they still feed the plant. Clemson Cooperative Extension says round dry patches and streaks on the leaves may be caused by excessive sunlight on the foliage. NC State Extension says direct sun can burn the foliage. That warning covers the south window in July, the balcony in a heatwave, and the plant set outside for the summer without a week in the shade first. A plant that sits in direct sun goes pale and scorched, and the scorched leaves drop — so give it bright light with a sheer curtain between the plant and the glass, and a week of shade before full sun outdoors.

A grow light is a fair answer when there is no bright window in how to care for dracaena, and the plant is honest about whether it is working. ### Water

Water is the decision that kills the most dracaenas in how to care for dracaena, and the sources are blunt about it — and they disagree on one point, which this guide shows honestly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to allow dracaenas to dry slightly between waterings, to wait until the soil surface is dry to the touch, and then to water them thoroughly, and to avoid watering with cold water. NC State Extension says the same for the genus: it is best to allow the top inch of the soil to dry out between watering. NC State Extension says for the corn plant to keep the soil moist during the growing season, and to cut back on the water in the winter. The spread is real: one source says let the surface dry, another says keep the soil moist in the growing season — and the finger test plus the leaves settle it. Push a finger into the mix. If it feels damp an inch down, wait. If the top inch feels dry and the leaves are starting to droop, water thoroughly until it runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The corn plant likes it a little moister than the dragon tree, because it is the broad-leafed one, and the dragon tree likes it a little drier, because it is the thin-leafed one — but both of them rot in a pot that never dries out.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for dracaena

The rule is dry, then soak, and the order matters in how to care for dracaena. A little water every few days keeps the top of the soil wet and the bottom dry, and the roots in the middle rot while the top looks fine. A thorough soak, only when the mix has dried through the top inch and the leaves are starting to droop, wets the whole root ball and then lets it drain, and the roots get water and air in turn. That is why all three sources say the same thing in different words: water infrequently, let it drain, and never let the pot stand in water. ### How often to water

The honest answer to how often is that the calendar does not know, and in how to care for dracaena the leaves do. ### The drooping leaves

Drooping leaves are the plant's own watering signal in how to care for dracaena, and they are worth learning to read. A dracaena that is thirsty goes soft: the leaves droop and feel limp, the stems feel light, and the pot feels light when you lift it. Water it thoroughly and the leaves firm up again within a day. ### Temperature

Humidity is the rule the sources agree on in direction in how to care for dracaena, and the numbers are worth showing. NC State Extension says the genus wants moderate to high humidity. ## The fluoride problem: why the tips turn brown

Brown tips are the signature problem in how to care for dracaena, and they are the one the other guides get wrong, because they blame the watering and miss the water. Iowa State University Extension lists the causes in the order to check: raise the humidity first, because low humidity is the most common reason for brown leaf tips, and the humidity in homes is often low, especially during the winter months. Then look at the watering, because inconsistent watering, in particular allowing plants to dry out too long between waterings, will cause leaf edges and tips to turn brown. Then reduce the fertilizer and salt build-up in the soil, because excess salts build up from too much fertilizer, which can cause brown leaf edges — so flush the soil, reduce fertilizer rates and frequency, and consider repotting. Then look at the water quality, because too much chlorine and other chemicals in the water, such as fluoride, can cause brown leaf tips, especially over an extended period of time on sensitive species like spider plant, ti plant, dracaena, prayer plant, and calathea — and Iowa State University Extension says to use a different water source if this issue is suspected.

### Fluoride: the cause owners miss

Fluoride is the cause owners miss in how to care for dracaena, and the sources are unusually blunt about it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says dracaena is very sensitive to fluoride, and that the symptoms include yellowing of the tips or margins of the leaf or dead, scorched areas. NC State Extension says the corn plant is sensitive to fluorides and built-up salts, so if leaves turn brown, try watering with non-fluoridated water and flush salts monthly. The fluoride comes from three places, and the sources name all three: the tap water, in fluoridated areas; the potting mix, where a high percentage of perlite supplies fluoride; and the fertilizer, where superphosphate often has high levels of fluorine. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to avoid potting soils that have a high percentage of perlite, to keep the soil pH between 6.0 and 6.5 to prevent fluoride from causing injury, and not to use fertilizer which contains superphosphate, since it often has high levels of fluorine. Our own [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) guide says the same thing from the other side: perlite contains some fluoride, so it should not go into mixes for fluoride-sensitive species such as spider plants or dracaenas, where fluoride can scorch leaf tips.

### The worked example: diagnosing brown tips in order

Work the list in order on a real plant in how to care for dracaena, and the cause shows itself. Take a corn plant in a bright east window with brown, crispy tips on the oldest leaves and clean green new growth. First, check the air: if the heating has been on for a month and the room feels dry, the humidity is the likeliest cause, per Iowa State University Extension — so run a humidifier or stand the pot on a tray of damp pebbles, and watch the new leaves. Second, check the watering: push a finger an inch into the mix, and if it is bone dry all the way down, the plant has been left too dry for too long — so water thoroughly and keep the top inch from drying out for weeks at a time. Third, check the salts: look for a white or grey crust on the soil surface or the rim of the pot, and if it is there, flush the pot with plain water until it runs clear, and cut the feeding back. Fourth, check the water: if the tips keep browning after the humidity, the watering and the salts are all fixed, and the plant is watered from a fluoridated tap in a perlite-heavy mix, the fluoride is the cause — so switch to rainwater, distilled water or filtered water, repot into a mix without much perlite, and stop using superphosphate fertilizer. Trim the brown tips with clean scissors, leaving a thin brown edge rather than cutting into green, because Iowa State University Extension says trimming improves appearance temporarily, but new browning will occur if the original issue is not addressed — and a brown tip will not turn green again, so judge the fix by the new leaves, not the old ones.

### What the brown tip is telling you

Read the brown tip itself in how to care for dracaena, because where the brown sits narrows the cause before you change anything. Dry, crispy tips with no yellow band between the green and the brown point at fluoride or dry air, and the fluoride version spreads inward from the very tip. Yellow tips and margins point at fluoride or heavy feeding, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, because leaf tips and margins may burn or become yellow if plants are fertilized too heavily. Round, dry patches in the middle of the leaf point at sun scorch, not at water, because Clemson Cooperative Extension says round dry patches and streaks may be caused by excessive sunlight. Brown, soft leaves from the base up point at overwatering and root rot, not at the tips at all. The tip is the plant's report, and the four causes are the four lines of it — so read the tip first, and then work the list, and the plant will tell you which one it is.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once and live with for a year in how to care for dracaena, and the sources agree on what it should do. Clemson Cooperative Extension says a standard commercial houseplant potting mix may be used. NC State Extension says the genus wants a moist, well-drained potting mix with a slightly acidic pH, and that the corn plant grows in commercial potting soil in a pot with drainage holes. The mix should hold a little water and then let go of it: a bagged houseplant mix on its own works, or a general [potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) cut with coarse bark — and the test is what happens after you water. If the mix is still soggy three days after watering, it is too dense, and the plant will rot; if it dries out in a week in a bright room, it is right for this plant, because a dracaena wants to dry out. A mix that is dry on top in a few days and ready for water when the leaves droop is right, and that is the mix to keep.

### An airy mix that drains — without much perlite

The air in the mix matters as much as the water in how to care for dracaena, because the roots breathe — and this plant has roots that drown quickly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot usually results from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or from overly frequent watering. NC State Extension says the same for the genus: the mix must be well-drained. The fix is in the mix, not in the watering can: coarse bark or pumice mixed into the potting soil opens air pockets that stay open after watering, and a pot with drainage holes lets the excess water out. Perlite is the exception for this plant, and it is the one the other guides get wrong: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to avoid potting soils that have a high percentage of perlite, because perlite can supply fluoride, and our own perlite guide says the same. Coarse bark is the safer opener for a dracaena, and a mix of potting soil with bark and a little charcoal drains well without adding fluoride. Clay is a good choice for a dracaena that is watered too often, because the clay breathes and the mix dries faster — and plastic is a poor choice for this plant, because it holds moisture longer and the roots sit wet. Either way, the drainage hole is non-negotiable — a pot without one is a pot that will rot the plant.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the second half of the soil decision in how to care for dracaena, and the rule is simple: big enough to stand up, never large. A large pot holds a lot of wet mix that the root system cannot drink, and that wet mix rots the roots — which is why a plant repotted into a large pot often dies faster than a plant left pot-bound. A tall dracaena also needs a heavy pot, because a ten-foot cane in a light plastic pot topples at the first draught — so a clay pot does double work here, steadying the plant and drying the mix faster. Choose a pot one size larger than the last, with drainage holes, and fresh loose mix — and if the plant is a dragon tree with a thin, bending stem, choose a pot you can stake it in, because the stem bends under its own weight as it grows. ### Repotting: when the plant fills the pot

Repotting is rare work for a dracaena in how to care for dracaena, and the plant tells you when it is time. NC State Extension says to repot the corn plant into a larger pot when the plant starts to lift out of the pot or sends roots out of the drainage hole. A young plant in a small pot may need a new pot every two or three years; a mature plant may go longer in the same pot. The method is the same either way: water the plant a few days before, so the mix is barely damp and holds together; tip the plant out gently, because the cane is heavy and the roots are brittle; shake off the loose mix; trim any brown, mushy roots with clean scissors; set the plant at the same depth in the new pot, with fresh loose mix around the roots; and water lightly, then leave it alone for a week. Do not feed a newly repotted plant for a month — the fresh mix already has enough food in it, and a stressed plant does not want fertilizer. A plant that drops a leaf or two after repotting is normal; a plant that drops many leaves was repotted too roughly, or into a pot too large, and the fix is to leave it alone in a bright, warm spot and let it settle — and to root the fallen stems as cuttings, because they are not waste.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the rule owners overdo in how to care for dracaena, and the sources are restrained about it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to feed dracaenas with liquid foliage plant fertilizer once a month during the spring and summer months, and that time release fertilizer pellets may be used also. Clemson Cooperative Extension also says not to use fertilizer which contains superphosphate, since it often has high levels of fluorine — and that leaf tips and margins may burn or become yellow if plants are fertilized too heavily. The spread is narrow — a light feed in spring and summer at the most, nothing in autumn and winter — and the reason is that the plant is a slow-growing forest plant that grows steadily on very little food. A dracaena in fresh loose mix, watered well and kept in bright, indirect light, grows steadily on almost no food, and the signs of too much food are worse than the signs of too little: burned tips and margins, soft growth, and a white crust on the soil surface.

### The planning figure: monthly in spring and summer, nothing in winter

The planning figure this guide uses in how to care for dracaena is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half strength, once a month in spring and summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or a single slow-release granular feed in spring, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. That figure follows Clemson's once a month in spring and summer, cut to half strength, because a full-strength feed on a dry dracaena burns the roots, so water the plant first, then feed, and never feed a plant that is wilted, newly repotted, or sitting in the dark in winter. A plant in lower light gets fed not at all — because it is barely growing, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that brown the tips. If the leaves go yellow at the tips after a feed, stop feeding and flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear.

## Pruning a plant that grows tall

Pruning is the maintenance that keeps a dracaena at the height you want in how to care for dracaena, because a plant that is never pruned grows into a bare cane with a tuft of leaves at the top. Clemson Cooperative Extension says if the plant's stems become too long and bare, cut them off at the desired height and new leaves will soon appear, and that for the dragon tree you cut back the stems to force the plant to branch. A dracaena that grows for five years without pruning becomes a bare cane with long, thin stems and a tuft of leaves at the ends — and the bare cane will not sprout new leaves along its length, because the plant only grows from the tips and from the nodes below a cut. The fix is to cut the cane back, and the cuttings are not waste: they are new plants, and the pruned cane branches below the cut.

### The worked example: cutting a six-foot dragon tree back to three feet

Work it on a real plant in how to care for dracaena, and the arithmetic is the owner's, not the plant's. Take a dragon tree that has grown six feet tall in a bright corner, with a bare cane for the bottom three feet and a tuft of leaves at the top. In spring, when the plant is growing, cut the cane at three feet — the height you want the plant to stand at — with a clean cut from sharp, clean pruners, and the cane below the cut will branch, often with two new shoots where there was one. The top three feet you cut off is not waste: cut it into two sections, each with a node or two, strip the lower leaves, and set them in damp mix or in water in bright, indirect light, and they root in a few weeks — so one six-foot plant becomes one three-foot plant with two shoots, plus two new plants. The plant will look bare for a month, and then the shoots will open, and by summer it will be fuller than it was before the cut. Do not prune in autumn or winter, when the plant is barely growing, because a cut made then heals slowly and the plant drops leaves instead of branching — and wear gloves, because the cut drips sap, and let the cut end dry before you handle it.

### Prune in spring, back to the height you want

Prune in spring, when the plant is growing, and cut the long, bare canes back to the height you want in how to care for dracaena. A clean cut with sharp pruners, just above a leaf node, and the cane below the cut will branch, often with two new shoots where there was one. Cut a long, bare cane back by a third, or by half if it is very bare, and the plant will fill out from the nodes below the cut over the summer. ## Propagation: cuttings, air layering, and basal shoots

Propagation is the reward for a healthy plant in how to care for dracaena, and Clemson Cooperative Extension lists three methods: air layering, tip or stem cuttings, and the removal and rooting of basal shoots in spring or late summer. Cuttings suit a young plant with plenty of stem tips; they also suit an old plant that is going leggy, because the tips are still healthy even when the base is not. All methods work, and all are done in spring or late summer, when the plant is growing — because a cutting taken in winter roots slowly, and a cutting in a wet winter mix rots before it roots.

### Tip and stem cuttings

Take a stem tip six to eight inches long, strip the leaves from the lower third, and set it in damp potting mix or in water in how to care for dracaena. Clemson Cooperative Extension says dracaenas are easy to propagate by tip or stem cuttings, and NC State Extension says the corn plant propagates by stem cuttings. Keep the cutting in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, keep the mix barely damp but not wet, and wait: roots form in a few weeks, and the first new leaves are the sign that the cutting has taken. A bare cane left after pruning is not waste either: cut it into sections with a node or two each, lay them in damp mix, and they root the same way. A cutting taken in spring or late summer roots fastest, when the plant is growing — and a single healthy stem gives you several cuttings, which is how one dracaena becomes the next one, and the one after that.

### Air layering and basal shoots

Air layering is the second method in how to care for dracaena, and it suits a tall plant you do not want to cut down all at once. Clemson Cooperative Extension lists air layering first among the methods, and the technique is the same as for any cane plant: wound the stem where you want roots, wrap the wound in damp sphagnum moss, cover it with plastic to hold the moisture in, and wait for roots to show through the moss before cutting below them. Basal shoots are the third method: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to remove basal shoots and root them in spring or late summer, and NC State Extension says the genus propagates by rhizome division as well. When a plant has thrown up shoots from the base, tip it out of the pot in spring, pull the shoots gently apart into pieces that each have roots and stems, and pot each piece up on its own. A divided plant is a full plant at once, not a cutting that has to grow — and a plant divided in spring fills its new pot by summer.

## Problems: brown tips, yellow leaves, and bare canes

Problems in how to care for dracaena are almost always water, light, or cold, and the sources name the same three causes for nearly every symptom. Work through them in order — water first, then light, then cold — and the plant usually answers within a week. A plant that is checked weekly never gets a bad problem; a plant that is never checked gets one that takes a month to clear.

### Leaf drop: the plant's loudest complaint

Leaf drop is the first problem in how to care for dracaena, and it is the one owners panic about most. Clemson Cooperative Extension says a sudden loss of many leaves can be caused by changes in temperature, drafts, too much water, poor drainage or heavy insect infestations. If the leaves are dropping from a plant in bone-dry mix after weeks without water, it is underwatering: water thoroughly and the firm leaves that remain will hold. If the leaves are dropping from the bottom of a tall cane while the top still looks healthy, that is the plant's normal habit, not a problem — so cut the cane back at the height you want, and it will branch below the cut. A leaf that has already dropped will not reattach, but the stem above it is still alive, and a cutting from that stem is the next plant.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the second problem in how to care for dracaena, and they are the fluoride and feeding signal. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf tips and margins may burn or become yellow if plants are fertilized too heavily, and that dracaena is very sensitive to fluoride, with yellowing of the tips or margins of the leaf or dead, scorched areas. Bare canes are the third problem in how to care for dracaena, and they are the age signal, not a disease. A plant that is pruned every spring never looks old, and a plant that is never pruned looks tired by year five. The bare cane is not a failure; it is the plant's record of the years it grew, and the cuttings are the next generation.

Scale and mites are the first pests in how to care for dracaena, and they are easy to miss until you know what to look for. Gnats mean the mix is staying wet — let it dry out, and the gnats leave on their own. Spider mites cluster on the undersides of dry leaves; wash them off with plain water and raise the humidity around the plant. A plant in a dry, bright room with a loose mix gets few pests — and a plant in a damp, dark room gets all of them.

Root rot is the disease to watch for in how to care for dracaena, and the fix is in the pot, not in a spray. ### Keep the leaves clean

Washing the leaves is the maintenance that keeps a dracaena healthy in how to care for dracaena, and the sources both name the habit in general terms. Dust blocks the light the leaves need, and a dusty plant grows slower than a clean one — so rinse the plant gently with plain water every few weeks, or wipe the leaves with a damp cloth, and let it drain completely before you put it back. A clean plant is a plant that can use its light, and a plant that can use its light grows — and every leaf that falls off in washing is a cutting, so lay it in the mix and let it root.

## Is dracaena poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know in how to care for dracaena, because this is one of the few houseplant groups a pet owner has to place with care. NC State Extension says that if ingested, dracaenas are toxic to cats and dogs, and that some species have been reported as toxic to humans as well, if ingested. The ASPCA lists dracaena as toxic to dogs and cats, and lists the corn plant as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, and names saponins as the toxic compounds. The signs the ASPCA lists for a cat or dog that has eaten dracaena are vomiting, sometimes with blood, depression, loss of appetite, drooling, and dilated pupils in cats. The ASPCA also says the consumption of any plant material may cause vomiting and gastrointestinal upset for dogs and cats, so a pet that grazes on a dracaena may still get an upset stomach even from a small bite. The plant is worth keeping, and the risk is worth managing — but only if you have checked the name on the label against the list, and only if the plant stands where a pet cannot graze on it all day.

### Do not confuse the names

The names are the trap in how to care for dracaena, because three different plants share one name. Lucky bamboo is a dracaena, Dracaena sanderiana, and it is toxic to pets, while true bamboo is a grass and is not a dracaena at all — so a "bamboo" that grows in water on a desk is a dracaena, and it belongs out of a pet's reach. Corn plant is a dracaena, Dracaena fragrans, and it is toxic to cats, dogs and horses — while sweet corn, the vegetable, is a different plant entirely, and the name is only a resemblance, not a relation. The snake plant is the third trap: most shops still sell it as Sansevieria, but NC State Extension says it is now classified as Dracaena trifasciata, and it is toxic to cats and dogs just like the rest of the genus. Read the botanical name on the label, not the common name on the shelf, and the pet question answers itself.

### If a pet eats a piece anyway

If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any dracaena and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. A pet that chews a dracaena is expected to vomit and go off its food, and a cat may show dilated pupils, because the ASPCA lists those signs for dracaena — but a pet that eats a lot of any plant material can still vomit, and a dracaena with no ASPCA entry is an open question, not a cleared plant. Keep the label from the shop, so you can give the vet the botanical name, and keep the plant where a pet cannot graze on it all day. A dracaena on a high shelf is a safe plant; a dracaena on the floor where a puppy can chew it all afternoon is a plant that will be eaten, listed or not.

Do those five, and the plant stands two to ten feet tall, with glossy leaves in green, cream, yellow and red that get longer and brighter every spring — and the cuttings from one pruning become the next plant.

### And a second plant for the same bright room

A second plant for the same bright room in how to care for dracaena is the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/), and the two belong together because they are the pair that share the fluoride problem. A spider plant wants bright, indirect light and watering when the top inch dries, per the same rules, and it hangs where a dracaena stands — and it is just as sensitive to fluoride in tap water, so the same rainwater that saves the dracaena saves the spider plant too. A [pothos](/blog/how-to-care-for-pothos/) is the third plant for the same room, and it wants the same bright spot, and it trails where the dragon tree stands — but a pothos is toxic to cats and dogs, so it hangs high, out of reach, while the dracaena stands on the floor. A [philodendron](/blog/how-to-care-for-philodendron/) is the fourth, and it wants the same bright, indirect light, and its heart-shaped leaves fill the shelf where the dracaena fills the corner. All four are easy, all four want bright light and a pot that drains, and three of the four are toxic to pets — and all four reward the same five rules, with the watering turned down a notch for the ones that like it drier. Start with the dracaena, add the spider plant, and then the pothos, and the bright room fills itself.

## Key figure spreads to present honestly

- Watering: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to allow dracaenas to dry slightly between waterings, to wait until the soil surface is dry to the touch, and then to water thoroughly, and to avoid watering with cold water; NC State Extension says for the genus it is best to allow the top inch of the soil to dry out between watering; NC State Extension says for the corn plant to keep the soil moist during the growing season and cut back on the water in the winter. Planning figure: water when the top inch of the mix feels dry, soak until water runs from the drainage holes, empty the saucer, and water less in winter — the corn plant likes it a little moister than the dragon tree, and both rot in a pot that never dries out.
- Light: Clemson Cooperative Extension says most dracaenas grow best in bright, indirect light, but many are also tolerant of lower light, and that if a plant that has been growing in dim light is moved to a brighter spot, the new leaves will be thicker and stronger and growth rate will increase; NC State Extension says the corn plant wants bright to moderate, filtered light, that direct sun can burn the foliage, and that if light levels are too low, the leaves will narrow; NC State Extension says the genus prefers bright, indirect sunlight. Planning figure: east-facing window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, bright light all day, no direct sun; variegated forms need more light than plain green ones to hold their stripes.
- Temperature: Clemson Cooperative Extension says dracaenas are best grown in normal house temperatures of 60 to 70 °F during the day, and that ideally, night temperatures should be about 10 °F cooler; NC State Extension says the genus thrives in warm temperatures between 60 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit, and that the corn plant thrives in temperatures of 70 to 80 degrees F, and to keep the temperature above 50 degrees F if possible; UF/IFAS Extension says the outdoor dracaenas are hardy in zones 10B through 11, and NC State Extension says the genus is hardy in zones 10 to 12 and needs to be protected from frost. Planning figure: 65 to 75 °F by day, a few degrees cooler at night, and never below 50 °F.
- Humidity: NC State Extension says the genus wants moderate to high humidity; Clemson Cooperative Extension says if the humidity in the house is below 30 to 40 percent, which it probably is during winter, plants will benefit from an occasional misting of their foliage; NC State Extension says the corn plant's leaves can turn brown at the tips if indoor humidity is too low, and that a humidifier may help to prevent leaf tip browning, especially in the winter when the house is heated. Planning figure: average household air with a humidifier in a dry winter room as a help, not a requirement; misting is a supplement, not a solution.
- Fluoride and brown tips: Iowa State University Extension lists the causes of brown leaf tips in the order to check: low humidity first, then uneven watering, then fertilizer salts, then the water itself, and names spider plant, ti plant, dracaena, prayer plant, and calathea as the species sensitive to chlorine and fluoride in water; Clemson Cooperative Extension says dracaena is very sensitive to fluoride, with yellowing of the tips or margins of the leaf or dead, scorched areas, and that the fluoride comes from tap water, from potting soils with a high percentage of perlite, and from superphosphate fertilizer, which often has high levels of fluorine; NC State Extension says the corn plant is sensitive to fluorides and built-up salts, so if leaves turn brown, try watering with non-fluoridated water and flush salts monthly. Planning figure: work the list in order — humidity, watering, salts, water — and switch to rainwater or filtered water, repot into a mix without much perlite, and stop using superphosphate; trim the brown tips leaving a thin brown edge, and judge the fix by the new leaves.

Planning figure: balanced liquid at half strength once a month in spring and summer, and nothing in autumn and winter, or one slow-release granular feed in spring, and nothing in winter, or no feed at all.

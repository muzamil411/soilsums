Ask a group of gardeners when to prune roses and you will get four different answers — and they can all be right, because the rule for when to prune roses depends on the type of rose. Prune a hybrid tea in late winter and it rewards you with a season of big blooms; prune a once-blooming old garden rose at the same time and you have just pruned away every flower it would have made that year. The only timing rule that fits every rose is a living cue rather than a calendar date: prune when the leaf buds swell and take on a pink or reddish hue, after the hard frosts are over. Every recommendation below comes from university extension services — Oregon State, Iowa State, UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, Texas A&M AgriLife — so you can trust the timing behind each rule.

## The short answer to when to prune roses

For the repeat-blooming modern roses most people grow — hybrid teas, grandifloras, and floribundas — the best time to prune is late winter to early spring, after winter protection comes off and before the leaf buds open. An eXtension expert panel's rule of thumb is the most useful version: watch the leaf buds, and when they begin to swell and turn pink or reddish, it is time to prune. The calendar date moves with your climate. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources puts pruning at mid-January in mild climates while buds are still dormant; Oregon State Extension says mid-February to early March for its region, warning that pruning before the last hard frost can cause dieback that needs repruning; Iowa State Extension places it in late March to mid-April in Iowa. If you remember one thing about when to prune roses, make it the bud cue, not a month.

## When to prune roses by type

### Repeat-blooming modern roses: hybrid teas, grandifloras, floribundas

These are the roses the standard spring pruning advice is written for. They bloom on new wood — the growth they make this season — so cutting them back in late winter does not cost any flowers. Iowa State Extension describes the sequence: after winter protection is removed in late March to mid-April, remove the winter-killed wood, then shape. OSU's sizing guidance: prune hybrid teas and grandifloras to 12 to 18 inches tall, floribundas a little taller at 24 to 36 inches. When the weather turns warm in spring, feed them — our [how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/) guide covers fertilizer timing, and our [fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) can scale the amount to your bed size.

### Climbing roses: know whether yours reblooms

Climbers are the one type where the timing rule splits, and the extension sources are honest about the disagreement. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension treats climbers as once-bloomers on the previous year's wood: except for dead or diseased growth, do not prune until after flowering, then prune out weak or damaged stems and remove the oldest canes, leaving five to seven strong canes untouched. Oregon State Extension covers the repeat-blooming kind: train the main canes horizontally to promote maximum bloom, prune only the lateral branches in spring (leaving 3 to 5 buds on each), and deadhead through the season for rebloom. Iowa State Extension adds the reason for the split: some climbing roses bloom on new wood and others on old wood, and cultivars reliable in northern climates typically bloom on the current year's growth because the old-wood canes die back over winter. Before deciding when to prune roses on a climber, check whether your cultivar reblooms.

### Once-blooming old garden roses: prune after flowering

Old garden roses bloom once, in late spring, on old wood. Iowa State Extension's guidance is simple: prune any wayward or overgrown canes after the blooms fade. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension says the same — prune immediately after bloom to keep old roses under control — and adds a warning: do not prune them hard. Hard pruning can ruin their graceful shape and severely reduce flowering; remove no more than one-third of each bush.

### Knock Out and other shrub roses: lighter touch

Shrub and landscape roses need the lightest hand. Iowa State Extension says to prune just as buds begin to break in spring (early to mid-April), starting by removing any stems killed by winter, then removing crowded stems from the interior if needed. An eXtension expert panel advises leaving Knock Out types unpruned for the first two years to build vigor, then removing one-third of the oldest canes each year alongside any dead, diseased, or dying wood. For shrub roses generally, Texas A&M recommends removing up to one-third of the canes — the oldest, woodiest stems — cutting them back to the crown, and reducing height by up to one-third.

### Newly planted roses: prune at planting, then wait

First-year roses are pruned for roots, not flowers. Oregon State Extension says to prune bare-root canes to about 6 inches at spring planting to encourage good root growth and establishment during the first growing season. After that, go easy: Texas A&M recommends only light pruning, if any, for roses three years old and younger. Iowa State adds a deadheading note for the first growing season: remove faded flowers above the uppermost three-leaflet leaf, because removing more foliage reduces the young plant's ability to manufacture food and can slow its growth.

## How to make the cut

### The 45-degree cut, a quarter inch above an outward bud

Every extension source describes the same standard cut, and the agreement is worth stating precisely: make a clean 45-degree cut on a downward slant, away from the bud, about a quarter inch above an outward-facing bud (Oregon State). UC Agriculture and Natural Resources phrases it identically, and Iowa State and the eXtension panel match. Leaving that quarter inch of cane above the bud lets the wound heal properly, and cutting above an outward-facing bud trains the new shoot to grow away from the plant's center. (One nuance, honestly noted: a non-extension rose-society guide questions whether the 45-degree angle itself is proven superior — but every extension source teaches it, so it stays the recommendation.)

### Remove the dead wood first

Before shaping, clear out the problem wood. Oregon State's sequence: start by removing dead, dying, or diseased canes, cutting back to white or pale green live pith — brown coloration means the cane is dead or dying. Texas A&M's version of the same check: the pith should be creamy white, not brown or gray, and if the inside of the stem is brown, prune at least one inch farther down. Iowa State says the same about the one-inch margin below dead areas. The eXtension panel adds a thickness rule: remove canes thinner than a pencil. Also remove any crossing or rubbing canes, and cut out any canes growing directly over the center of the plant — airflow through the middle of the bush is part of the plan. Texas A&M notes that on cuts at least pencil-thick, you can seal the wound with wood glue to keep cane borers out.

### Shape a vase: 3 to 7 healthy canes, open center

The goal shape is an open-centered vase that lets air and sunlight reach the middle of the plant. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources describes it as a vase-shaped bush with five to seven healthy, well-spaced canes; Oregon State selects three to six strong canes from the previous year's growth. Texas A&M's version: cut out all but three to five of the healthiest, most vigorous canes, pruned to 15 to 18 inches from ground level. The eXtension panel phrases the same idea as three to five evenly spaced canes in a V formation. However many you keep, the open center is doing the disease-prevention work: air circulation dries foliage faster, and dry foliage gets less fungal disease.

### How much to take off

How hard to prune is the one technique point where extension advice genuinely varies, and the variation is regional. An eXtension expert recommends pruning hard in spring — removing one-half to two-thirds of the plant's height and all old woody stems. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources gives one-third to one-half as the guideline in mild climates. Texas A&M frames it as a trade-off: prune hard for fewer but larger blooms, less hard for more but smaller blooms. And Iowa State offers the cold-climate correction: where winter damage is extensive, remove all the dead wood and save as much live tissue as possible — in mild-damage winters, pruning the canes back to within 8 to 12 inches of the ground is enough. In other words, the "how far" question depends on how much winter your roses survived.

### Sanitize the tools between plants

Roses share diseases — viruses especially — and dirty pruners spread them. Oregon State Extension recommends sanitizing by dipping the blades into 70 percent isopropyl rubbing alcohol or using a disinfectant spray before working on each plant. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources recommends Lysol spray on the tools between roses. Iowa State's version is simpler but emphatic: use sharp, clean tools, because dull tools crush canes and clean sterile tools help prevent the transfer of disease pathogens. Note what is absent: no extension source verified the common "10 percent bleach" recipe, so it is left out here.

## What not to do in the fall

Of everything gardeners ask about when to prune roses, the fall question has the clearest extension answer: don't. Iowa State Extension is direct: do not prune roses in the fall, because pruning stimulates new growth, that new growth cannot harden off before winter, and the result is more winter damage. Pruning late in the growing season, the same source notes, can reduce winter survival rates — and even properly hardened growth will see some stem dieback over winter, so Iowa State suggests leaving all stem material in place in case of a mild winter, which means less dead material to prune out in spring.

Oregon State allows one narrow exception: take several inches off hybrid teas, floribundas, and grandifloras to reduce wind damage to the canes over winter. That is a wind-rock control measure for cold-wind climates, not a license to do your spring pruning early. So the honest answer to when to prune roses is never "in fall" — put the pruners away, which is also the point at which a bed benefits more from a mulch blanket than from a haircut. ## Deadheading: the summer job, and when to stop it

Deadheading — cutting off spent flowers — is the summer companion to spring pruning. For repeat-blooming roses it genuinely works: Oregon State says repeat-blooming roses, including climbers, bloom more profusely if faded blossoms are removed; Iowa State says deadheading through the summer months promotes additional blooms; Texas A&M defines deadheading as cutting off old, faded flowers to encourage new ones, with the cut made diagonally just above the next five- or seven-leaflet leaf. For one-time bloomers there is no point — Iowa State notes there is usually no need to deadhead old-fashioned roses, and Texas A&M adds that you can simply leave the spent blooms to form hips for fall and winter color.

Stopping matters as much as starting. Iowa State Extension's FAQ is exact: stop deadheading hybrid teas, grandifloras, and floribundas in September; the spent flowers then develop into hips, and hip development slows plant growth and helps prepare the roses for winter. The same timing holds for shrub roses — stop by late summer (late August to early September) to let plants prepare for winter and to allow ornamental rose hips to form. Every September spent bloom you leave on the plant is one more signal telling the rose to wind down for the season.

## Worked example: a spring pruning session

Here is how the timing and technique come together on a Saturday in early spring — use your local bud cue, not the date. Start with a hybrid tea you have not pruned yet this year. First, look at the canes and check for life: scratch down from the tip, and wherever the pith under the bark is brown, plan to cut at least one inch farther down until you see creamy white pith (Texas A&M, Iowa State). Next, remove the obvious losers: anything dead, diseased, thinner than a pencil, or crossing through the center (eXtension panel, Oregon State). Now pick three to six of the strongest remaining canes (Oregon State), spaced evenly around the plant, and cut each one about a quarter inch above an outward-facing bud at a 45-degree slant, bringing the bush to roughly 12 to 18 inches tall. Dip your pruners in 70 percent isopropyl alcohol before moving to the next rose (Oregon State). Finish by mulching the bed — our [mulch calculator](/tools/mulch-calculator/) tells you how many bags that takes — and feeding in spring with guidance from our [compost](/blog/why-is-my-compost-not-breaking-down/) and fertilizing articles. The whole session takes one morning, and learning when to prune roses this way is the single biggest lever you have over the season's bloom.

That is the full answer to when to prune roses: spring, when the buds swell; after flowering for once-bloomers and most climbers; lightly for new plants; and never in fall. The rose is not waiting for a month on your calendar — it is waiting for its own buds.

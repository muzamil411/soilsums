Apply lime to garden soil in **autumn**, as soon after the last harvest as the bed is clear.
That gives the limestone the whole winter to dissolve before the growing season, and lets the
freeze-thaw cycle work it into the soil. Early spring is the next best window, several weeks
ahead of sowing. Lime takes **six months to a year** to act fully.

## How long does lime take to raise soil pH?

Six months to a year for the full change, and longer on dry or coarse soil.

Ground limestone is not very soluble. It has to dissolve in soil water before it can neutralise
anything, so the rate depends on how finely it was ground, how moist the soil is, and how well it
was mixed in. Nothing about that process is fast.

| Applied             | Meaningful change by     | Full effect by       |
| ------------------- | ------------------------ | -------------------- |
| Autumn              | The following spring     | The following autumn |
| Early spring        | Midsummer                | The following spring |
| Midsummer, dry soil | Barely at all until rain | Slower still         |

This slowness is the main argument for autumn application, and the main reason to apply less than
you think you need and retest, rather than more.

## Why autumn is the best time

Three reasons, in order of importance.

**It has the winter to work.** Six months of moisture and freeze-thaw does more to dissolve and
distribute limestone than any amount of raking.

**The bed is empty.** Lime should be worked into the top six inches, which is easy on a cleared
bed and awkward around growing plants.

**It separates lime from feeding.** Nitrogen goes on in spring. Liming in autumn puts several
months between the two, which matters for the reason below.

## Keep it away from nitrogen

Leave several weeks between liming and applying ammonium fertilizer or fresh manure.

At high pH, ammonium converts to ammonia gas and escapes. Lime and nitrogen applied together
means losing a good part of the nitrogen to the air — you have paid for both and get the benefit
of one. Phosphate and potash are unaffected.
[What the three numbers on fertilizer mean](/blog/what-the-three-numbers-on-fertilizer-mean/)
covers which form of nitrogen you are actually holding.

## How to apply it

**Work it in.** Limestone barely moves through soil under its own steam. Raking or digging it
into the top six inches puts it where the roots are; left on the surface of an undug bed it can
take years.

**Spread it evenly.** Patchy application gives patchy pH, which is worse than a uniformly
slightly-low pH. Split the quantity in half and make two passes at right angles.

**Split a large correction.** Penn State advises that no single application to turf exceed 100 lb
per 1,000 sq ft, splitting anything larger into two or more applications four to six months
apart — in practice, spring and autumn. Colorado State is stricter on established turf at 50 lb.

**Wear a mask.** Even ordinary ground limestone is a fine dust that is unpleasant to breathe.

## Before you apply anything: the measurement that matters

A pH reading tells you the current acidity. It does not tell you how strongly the soil will
**resist** being changed, which is what decides how much lime you need.

That resistance is buffering capacity, and it comes mostly from clay content and organic matter.
A sandy soil at pH 5.5 might need a third of the lime a clay soil at the same pH needs, because
there is far less in it holding the acidity in place.

This is why a soil test reporting **buffer pH**, or giving a direct lime recommendation, is worth
far more than a meter reading. A meter tells you there is a problem; a proper test tells you how
big it is. Extension services in the US and Canada run them cheaply.

## Do not try to do it all at once

Lime requirement is treated as linear in the pH change for convenience, and that holds up
reasonably over about one unit. Beyond about 1.5 units it stops being trustworthy.

For a large correction — pH 4.8 to 6.5, say — apply roughly a unit and a half's worth, wait a
season, retest, and apply the rest if it is still needed.

Overshooting is the real risk. Above about pH 7.0, iron, manganese and zinc start to become
unavailable, producing yellowing between the leaf veins that looks like a feeding problem and is
not. Bringing pH back down means elemental sulfur and another season of waiting —
[how much sulfur to lower soil pH](/blog/how-much-sulfur-to-lower-soil-ph/) covers what that
involves.

## Crops to time it around

**Potatoes.** They prefer pH 5.0 to 6.5, and scab worsens as pH rises towards neutral. If a bed
is in rotation, lime it in the year it grows brassicas rather than the year it grows
[potatoes](/crops/potato/).

**Brassicas** appreciate the upper end, partly because clubroot is less severe in less acid soil.
They are the right crop to follow a liming.

**Blueberries, rhododendrons, azaleas and camellias** want pH 4.5 to 5.5 — far below anything you
would lime for. Liming near them actively harms them.

## One thing lime will not fix

Lime raises pH. It does nothing for compaction, drainage or fertility except indirectly, by
making nutrients already present available again.

If a bed grows everything poorly, check pH by all means — but check depth and drainage too. A
shallow bed on a compacted base is a more common problem than an acid one.

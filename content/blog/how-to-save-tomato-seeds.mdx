Late October is the moment for learning how to save tomato seeds. The season's last ripe fruits are still on the vine, and each one holds next year's plants — free, already adapted to your garden, and carrying the exact variety you liked enough to grow. Saving tomato seeds is not difficult, but tomatoes need one step most vegetables do not: the seeds come wrapped in a gel coat that has to be removed before they will store and sprout reliably. This guide follows how to save tomato seeds the way university extension services teach it — ferment the gel off, dry the seeds completely, and store them labeled — so the seeds you tuck away this fall are ready to plant in spring.

## Why tomato seeds need fermenting

Cut open a ripe tomato and you will see every seed sitting inside a slippery, jelly-like sac. That gel is not packaging; it is a chemical lock. The Chicago Botanic Garden explains that the gel surrounding a tomato seed contains chemicals that prevent germination — without those inhibitors, nothing would stop a tomato seed from sprouting inside the fruit while it is still on the parent plant. In nature, a fallen tomato rots on the ground, the gel sac breaks down, and the seeds are left free to germinate. Understanding that natural cycle is the key to how to save tomato seeds well: fermentation just runs that rot on purpose, in a jar, on your schedule.

Fermentation does three jobs at once. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Cooperative Extension, Santa Clara County) lists them: it makes the seeds easier to separate from the gel, it helps sort out the bad seeds, and it reduces some seed-borne illnesses while eliminating the germination inhibitor. The Chicago Botanic Garden adds that the fermentation process kills many seed-borne diseases. That is why every extension source recommends fermenting even though, as UC Agriculture and Natural Resources notes, you do not absolutely have to — and why the same source calls fermenting good etiquette if you ever trade seeds with other gardeners. The smell is the price: the Chicago Botanic Garden warns the jar will ferment, bubble, and smell absolutely awful, and suggests keeping it outside.

## Save seeds only from open-pollinated varieties

Before you squeeze a single seed, check what kind of tomato you grew. This is the one decision in how to save tomato seeds that cannot be fixed later. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) puts it plainly: make sure the variety is open-pollinated, not hybrid. All heirloom tomatoes are open-pollinated, and open-pollinated varieties come true from seed — the plants you grow next year will match the parent. Hybrid varieties are created by seed companies carefully crossing two parent plants, so their seeds will not breed true.

What does "not breed true" look like in practice? The Chicago Botanic Garden says seeds saved from hybrid fruits generally revert to the undesirable parent varieties — that is, if they germinate at all. The Old Farmer's Almanac agrees: hybrid seeds are unpredictable, may resemble one parent or a distant relative, and are often sterile, so you may get no plants at all. Reading the packet settles most cases. As the Almanac notes, a hybrid will be labeled F1 somewhere on the packet; "heirloom" always means open-pollinated. If the packet is long gone, typing the variety name into a search box will tell you within a minute. Saving seed from a hybrid is fine as an experiment, but if you want next year's tomatoes to taste like this year's, only save from open-pollinated varieties. That single check is the highest-leverage decision in how to save tomato seeds.

## Choose the right tomatoes to save from

The seed is only as good as the plant it came from, so be selective. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) advises choosing the best examples of what you are saving: the biggest or healthiest plant, the biggest, tastiest, most interesting tomato. If one plant is sickly and another is healthy, take your tomato from the healthy one — the sickly one may be carrying some weakness in its genes, and this is not the time for "waste not, want not." Choose tomatoes in their prime, the same source says: not over-ripe or under-ripe, not diseased or misshapen. The Old Farmer's Almanac gives the same test in plainer terms: pick an overripe tomato that is soft but not mushy, fully ripe and as close to perfect as possible.

Two more rules from UC Agriculture and Natural Resources round out the selection. First, save seeds from multiple tomatoes, and preferably from more than one plant of the same variety, to maintain good genetic diversity — a batch drawn from a single fruit is a narrower genetic base than one drawn from several. Second, label everything from the start: scoop each variety into its own small container labeled with the variety name, because all tomato seeds look identical once they are out of the fruit. Since you are only taking the seeds, the same source notes, you still get to eat most of the tomato — so this is not much of a sacrifice. Careful selection is the cheapest part of how to save tomato seeds, and the one most beginners skip.

## What you need

How to save tomato seeds takes no special equipment. Gather a knife, a spoon, a few small glass jars or cups, masking tape and a permanent marker for labeling, a fine-mesh strainer, and something to dry the seeds on — a fine screen, a paper plate, or a coffee filter. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) lists nearly the same kit for its seed-saving class: marker and masking tape, knife and spoon, jars, a fine strainer, paper plates for drying, and letter envelopes for storage. You will also want paper towels or cheesecloth to cover the jars, and small paper envelopes for the finished seeds. That is the whole shopping list.

## How to save tomato seeds, step by step

With ripe, open-pollinated tomatoes selected and jars labeled, the active work of how to save tomato seeds takes about ten minutes spread over a week — the calendar does the rest. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) gives the standard method, and UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) adds useful timing details from its Master Gardener seed-saving class. Follow it variety by variety — never mix varieties in one jar.

### Day 0: squeeze and soak

Cut each tomato in half and scoop or squeeze the seeds and gel into your labeled container. Set the rest of the tomato aside for eating. Add water: UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) says to add one-quarter to one-half cup of water, while the Stanislaus County class says to fill the jar half full of water with the seeds in it. Cover the jar loosely with a paper towel or cheesecloth — the Old Farmer's Almanac suggests a coffee filter held on with a rubber band, which also keeps fruit flies out — and set the container aside at room temperature, out of direct sunlight. The Chicago Botanic Garden keeps its jar outside because of the smell.

### Day 2: skim the floaters

UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) says that two days in, you should skim off the floating seeds and remove them. Empty, unviable seeds float; the seeds you want are at the bottom of the jar. This early skim is the first round of the sorting that fermentation does for you.

### Days 3 to 5: watch for the film

A moldy film will probably form on the surface, and the mixture may bubble. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) says that is okay — the film is the visible sign that fermentation is happening. Give the jar a gentle stir once a day. Three to five days is the standard window across the extension sources; the Stanislaus County class describes it as waiting "a few more days" after the day-two skim until the film forms. Do not let the jar sit much longer than that. Once the gel is gone, the germination inhibitor is gone too, and seeds left sitting in water can start sprouting in the jar — at which point the batch is ruined.

### Rinse: sinkers stay, floaters go

Carefully remove the moldy film from the surface, then add fresh water and stir. Now comes the satisfying part: the good seeds sink to the bottom while bits of pulp, gel, and unviable seeds float. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) says to pour off the water and the floating bits, then repeat — add water, stir, pour — until all the pulp is gone and only clean seeds remain. The Chicago Botanic Garden describes the same separation: the fungus and unviable seeds float to the top while the good seeds settle to the bottom. Finish by pouring the clean seeds into a fine-mesh strainer, rinsing them thoroughly under clean water to remove any remaining gel, and letting them drip dry.

## The no-ferment shortcut

If the idea of a moldy jar on the counter for five days puts you off, there is a faster way — with a trade-off. The Chicago Botanic Garden describes the alternate method: put the seeds with a little water in a sieve and run cool water over them, swishing so the gel runs out through the mesh. You will not get all of the gel off, but a thorough rinsing is sufficient for home use. Let the seeds drip dry, then spread them on a paper towel to finish. The seeds will stick to the paper, the Garden notes, but the towel can be rolled up to store the seeds in a paper bag.

Be honest with yourself about the trade. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) is clear that fermentation is what reduces seed-borne illness and removes the germination inhibitor completely, which is why it recommends fermenting even though it is technically optional. Seeds saved without fermenting tend to have lower, less even germination. For seeds you plan to share or trade, ferment — the same source calls it good etiquette. For a quick backup batch of your own favorite, the rinse method is acceptable. Either way, the drying and storage steps of how to save tomato seeds stay exactly the same — only the gel-removal method changes.

## Drying: the step that decides everything

More saved-seed batches are lost to damp storage than to any other mistake. Drying is the quiet half of how to save tomato seeds — fermentation gets all the attention, but a batch that goes into storage even slightly damp can mold and be lost entirely. Treat drying as seriously as fermenting.

### How long drying takes

Spread the rinsed seeds in a single layer on a drying surface and give them time. How much time depends on whom you ask: UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) says to spread seeds on a labeled plate until dry for a few days, while the Old Farmer's Almanac says to dry on a fine screen, out of direct sun, for a week or two until completely dry. The practical rule is to dry until the seeds pass the test below, however long that takes in your conditions — warm, airy, and out of direct sunlight dries fastest.

### What to dry the seeds on

The drying surface matters, and two good sources disagree on one detail, so here is the honest version. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) says to dry seeds on a screen or a paper plate, and warns explicitly: do not use plastic or ceramic plates, because the seeds need water wicked away from them — paper towels are acceptable but the seeds tend to stick. The Chicago Botanic Garden, meanwhile, says to spread the seeds on a glass or ceramic pan and let them dry completely. Both agree on the underlying point: water must be able to leave the seed. A fine screen is the safest choice because air circulates around every seed; a paper plate or coffee filter wicks moisture away well. Whatever you use, label it — UC Agriculture and Natural Resources reminds savers working with multiple varieties to label every plate, because dried tomato seeds all look alike.

### The snap test

The finish line is the snap test from the Old Farmer's Almanac: pick up one seed and try to bend it. If it snaps cleanly in two, the batch is dry. If it just bends, the seeds need more drying time. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) gives the same rule in plainer terms: only move seeds to storage once they are thoroughly dry. When in doubt, give them a few more days. Storing even slightly damp seeds invites mold, and mold can take the whole batch.

## How to store saved tomato seeds

Storage is the last step of how to save tomato seeds, and the simplest — provided the seeds going in are truly dry. Once the seeds pass the snap test, storage is simple but unforgiving of moisture.

### Containers and conditions

UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) says to place thoroughly dry seeds in an airtight container — a small glass jar works well — and notes that tomato seeds remain viable for years even stored at room temperature. For extra protection, the same source says you can store them in the refrigerator or freezer, with one critical caution: let the jar come to room temperature before opening it, so condensation does not introduce moisture to the dry seeds. Small packets of silica gel in the jar help absorb excess moisture. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) adds the storage mantra in four words: cool, dry, dark place.

### How long saved seeds keep

How long will they keep? The extension sources cluster tightly: UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Stanislaus County) says up to four years in a cool, dry, dark place; the Chicago Botanic Garden says tomato seeds may remain viable for up to four years; and UW–Madison Extension's seed viability chart lists tomatoes at four years. The Old Farmer's Almanac gives a slightly more generous up to five years, with germination rates decreasing as seeds age. Treat four years as the planning figure and test anything older before you count on it.

### Two details that ruin stored seed

Two storage details are easy to get wrong. First, label every container with the variety name and the year you saved the seeds — the Chicago Botanic Garden insists on both, because "mystery tomato, 2026" is not a planting plan. Second, mind where the jar lives. The Chicago Botanic Garden warns that if you are saving seeds through the summer, the storage area must stay cool and dry even on hot days — a garage that bakes in August is not a cool place. UW–Madison Extension adds a subtle one: do not store seeds near aging fruits or vegetables, which release ethylene gas that harms germination. A bedroom closet or a kitchen cabinet away from the stove beats the garage every time.

## Worked example: test your saved seeds before spring

### The calendar

Here is how to save tomato seeds as a calendar, using the figures above. Say you save seeds from three tomatoes of one open-pollinated variety in late October 2026 — multiple fruits for genetic diversity, per UC Agriculture and Natural Resources. You ferment for 3 to 5 days, rinse, and dry for about two weeks until the seeds pass the snap test. In early November the labeled, dated envelope goes into an airtight jar in a cool closet. Come February, before you start anything indoors, you run the seed roll test from UW–Madison Extension to check what four months of storage did.

### The germination test

The test itself is simple arithmetic. Moisten half a paper towel, lay ten seeds along one edge, roll it loosely, and seal it about 70 percent shut in a plastic bag in a warm spot. Check every two to three days so it never dries out. Suppose seven seeds sprout: that is a 70 percent germination rate. UW–Madison Extension calls 80 percent great and says that at 50 percent you should plant more seeds than normal to compensate — so at 70 percent, sow a little thicker than usual.

Now turn that into a sowing plan. If you want 12 tomato seedlings, divide by the germination rate: 12 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 17.1, so sow 18 seeds. In practice that means two seeds per cell in nine cells, then snipping the weaker seedling at soil level — the same thinning approach in our [how to start seeds indoors](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/) guide. If instead only three of the ten sprouted, that is 30 percent — below UW–Madison Extension's 40 percent cutoff, at which point buying fresh seed beats fighting a weak batch. Ten minutes of testing in February saves a month of wondering why nothing came up in March. ## Keeping varieties pure in a small garden

### Why tomato seed usually stays pure

One fair worry about how to save tomato seeds: if you grow five varieties side by side, will the saved seed still be the variety you picked? Mostly, yes. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources (Santa Clara County) explains that tomato flowers generally fertilize themselves, and the Chicago Botanic Garden notes that the chances of outcrossing in tomatoes are minimal. Tomatoes are self-pollinating, often before the flower even fully opens, so an ordinary backyard with several varieties still produces mostly pure seed.

### Two precautions for purity

"Mostly" leaves room for bees. The same UC Agriculture and Natural Resources source warns that a busy bee can create a natural hybrid for you, especially if you grow many varieties close together. Two precautions cover it — and this is the part of how to save tomato seeds that keeps next year's batch true to type. The simpler one, from the Old Farmer's Almanac: plant the variety you intend to save at least 10 feet away from other tomato types. The surer one, from UC Agriculture and Natural Resources: bag a cluster of unopened blossoms in a paper or cloth bag (not plastic) for a few days, shake it gently each morning to help pollination, then remove the bag once the flowers wither and tag the cluster so you remember which fruits came from it. For most home gardeners saving a favorite slicer, the 10-foot separation is plenty; reach for the bagging trick if you are saving seed to share or trade.

That is the whole of how to save tomato seeds: pick ripe fruit from healthy plants of an open-pollinated variety, ferment the gel off over three to five days, rinse until only the sinkers remain, dry until they snap, and store labeled in a cool, dry, dark place. Do it once this fall and next spring's seedlings — started from your own [tomato](/crops/tomato/) patch, selected for your own soil — cost you nothing but a jar and a week of patience.

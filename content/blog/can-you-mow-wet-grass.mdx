Yes, you can. The internet is oddly absolute about this and the truth is not: mowing wet grass is
worse than mowing dry grass, in ways worth understanding, and there are weeks in a wet autumn when
the alternative is not mowing at all — which does more harm than the wet cut would have.

So the useful version of this page is not a verdict. It is what the cost actually is, and how to
reduce it.

## Why a wet cut is a worse cut

The mechanism is simple and it is not about water damaging the plant.

**A dry grass blade stands up. A wet one lies over.** Held up by its own stiffness, a dry leaf meets
the mower blade edge-on and gets sliced. Loaded with water, the same leaf bends and flattens, so it
is dragged and struck rather than cut cleanly — **the cut becomes a tear.**

You can see the result the next day. A torn leaf tip goes pale, frays and browns back a little,
where a cleanly cut one seals almost invisibly. A whole lawn cut wet has a slightly bleached,
scuffed look for a few days afterwards, and that is thousands of ragged wounds rather than thousands
of clean ones.

It also means the cut is uneven. Blades that happen to be standing get cut and blades that are lying
get missed, so the finish is patchy in a way dry mowing is not, and you find yourself going over it
again.

**A sharp blade matters more here than anywhere.** A dull blade tears dry grass too; on wet grass it
has no chance at all. If you are going to mow wet, sharpen first.

## What the clippings do

This is the part that causes lasting damage rather than cosmetic damage.

Dry clippings scatter. They fall between the standing blades, dry out within a day and break down
where they land, which is why mulching works and why returning clippings feeds a lawn rather than
harming it.

**Wet clippings stick to each other.** They leave the mower as clumps rather than as a spray, and a
clump sitting on a lawn does three things: it blocks light from the grass underneath, holds moisture
against it, and excludes air. A day or two of that in warm weather and you have a yellow patch the
shape of the clump. A week of it and the grass underneath is dead.

So if you mow wet, **the clumps have to come off**. Rake them, or go over the lawn again once it has
dried to break them up and scatter them. Leaving them is the single most damaging thing about a wet
cut, and it is entirely avoidable.

## What it does to the mower

Not cosmetic either, and more annoying than the lawn damage.

**The deck packs.** Wet clippings paste themselves to the underside of the mower housing in a layer
that gets thicker as you go. A rotary mower works by airflow — the blade lifts the grass and throws
the clipping out — and a packed deck destroys that airflow, so the cut gets worse the longer you
mow.

**The discharge blocks.** The chute or the bag inlet clogs, the mower stops ejecting, and you stop to
clear it by hand. Do that with the engine off and the spark plug lead disconnected, or the battery
out.

**The bag fills with weight rather than volume.** Wet grass is heavy and does not tip out — it has to
be scraped.

**The motor labours.** More load on a petrol engine, noticeably less run time from a battery.

The fix is to clean the deck before the clippings dry, because once they harden they come off with a
scraper and a lot of patience instead of a hose and a minute.

## Disease, which is the reason to care most

Three things line up badly when you mow wet grass, and it is the combination rather than any one of
them.

- **A torn wound is bigger than a cut.** More exposed surface, more time to seal.
- **Fungal spores move in water.** A film of water on a leaf surface is how they travel from an
  infected plant to a healthy one, and a mower dragging through wet grass carries them across the
  whole lawn on the deck and the wheels.
- **Humidity at the lawn surface is what lets them germinate.** Wet clumps sitting on the turf hold
  exactly that humidity in place.

So mowing wet in cool weather is mostly a quality-of-cut problem. Mowing wet in a warm, humid spell
is how a small patch of disease becomes a large one. If you have visible disease and the grass is
wet, that is the strongest argument for waiting that exists — and for washing the mower afterwards.

## How to do it with the least damage

When you have to, and often you do:

1. **Let surface water drain.** Damp grass and standing water are different problems. If your shoes
   are getting soaked, wait — an hour or two of drainage changes the cut a lot. Dew is much less of a
   problem than rain.
2. **Raise the cutting height.** Take less off. A higher cut means less material to clump, less load
   on the machine, and less stress on the plant. You can lower it again on a dry week.
3. **Take a narrower pass.** Overlap by a third to a half rather than mowing the full width. It is
   slower but the deck handles a fraction of the material at once.
4. **Slow down.** The blade needs time to work on a leaf that is not helping it.
5. **Side-discharge or mulch rather than bag**, if the grass is not too long, then rake the clumps.
   If it is long, bag it and accept the emptying.
6. **Sharpen the blade first**, as above.
7. **Clean the deck straight afterwards**, before the paste sets.
8. **Do not mow a wet slope**, and do not use a corded electric mower in wet conditions at all.

## When to wait, and when not to

**Wait if you can**, obviously — but "if you can" does real work in that sentence.

**Wait** when there is standing water, when the ground is soft enough to rut under the wheels, when
there is visible disease in warm humid weather, or when the grass is only slightly long and another
few days costs nothing.

**Do not wait** when the grass is getting long enough that the next cut will take off more than a
third of the leaf, because that is a bigger stress than a wet cut. In a wet autumn the choice is
often between mowing wet and letting the lawn go into winter far too long, matted and prone to
exactly the diseases you were trying to avoid. Mow it wet, raise the height, take the clumps off.

This is the same trade-off as almost everything in a garden: the textbook answer assumes conditions
you do not have, and the useful answer is the least-bad version of what you can actually do today.

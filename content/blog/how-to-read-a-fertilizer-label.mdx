Every bag of fertilizer in the shop carries three numbers on the front. They are the most
useful piece of information on the package and the most widely misread. Here is what they
actually say.

## The three numbers

They are percentages by weight, always in the same order:

1. **Nitrogen (N)** — leaf and stem growth.
2. **Phosphate (P₂O₅)** — root development and flowering.
3. **Potash (K₂O)** — overall vigour, fruit quality, disease resistance.

So a bag marked 10-10-10 is 10% nitrogen, 10% phosphate and 10% potash. The other 70% is
carrier and filler, plus any secondary nutrients.

To find out how much of a nutrient is actually in a bag, multiply the weight by the percentage:

```
50 lb bag x 10% nitrogen = 5 lb of nitrogen
```

That is the calculation that makes a price comparison possible. A 50-pound bag of 10-10-10
contains five pounds of nitrogen. A 20-pound bag of 25-0-0 contains the same five pounds. If
nitrogen is what you need, they are equivalent products at very different weights.

## The second number is not phosphorus

This is the part that trips people up, including people who have gardened for years.

The second number is **phosphate**, expressed as P₂O₅. The third is **potash**, expressed as
K₂O. Neither is the pure element. Phosphate is about 44% phosphorus by weight; potash is about
83% potassium.

Does this matter in practice? Usually not, and here is why: extension service and soil test
recommendations are written in the same phosphate and potash terms. If your soil test says you
need two pounds of phosphate per 1,000 square feet, and a bag says 10% phosphate, the two
numbers speak the same language and no conversion is needed. The
[fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) works in those same units for exactly that
reason.

It matters if you are reading agronomic literature that uses elemental P and K, or comparing
against a source from a country that labels differently. Check which convention a figure uses
before you act on it.

## Complete, balanced, and other words that mean less than they sound

**Complete** means the product contains all three of N, P and K. It does not mean it is complete
for your soil.

**Balanced** usually means the three numbers are equal, as in 10-10-10. Equal numbers are not
inherently good. Most established gardens have plenty of phosphate already, because a decade of
balanced fertilizer puts on far more than the crops remove. Applying a balanced product to such
a soil adds phosphate that the plants cannot use and that can wash into watercourses.

**Organic** on a fertilizer label refers to the origin of the nutrients, not to whether a
certification scheme approves it. The plant takes up the same ions either way; the differences
are in release rate, soil biology and what else comes along with it.

## Release rate, which the label also tells you

Look at the nitrogen breakdown in the small print. You will see terms like:

- **Nitrate nitrogen** — immediately available, and equally quick to leach away.
- **Ammoniacal nitrogen** — available fairly quickly, held slightly better by soil.
- **Urea nitrogen** — needs converting by soil microbes first, so slower.
- **Water insoluble nitrogen** — slow release, often from organic sources.

A product that is mostly nitrate acts within days and is gone within weeks. One that is mostly
water-insoluble feeds over months. For vegetables, a mix is usually ideal: something available
now and something that keeps going.

## Working out how much to spread

Recommendations come as a rate of _nutrient_ per area. Bags contain _product_. The conversion
is one line:

```
lb of product = (target rate / (nutrient % / 100)) x (area / 1000)
```

To put one pound of nitrogen on 1,000 square feet using 10-10-10: 1 / 0.10 = 10 pounds of
product. Using 46-0-0 instead: 1 / 0.46 = 2.2 pounds. Same nitrogen, very different quantity to
carry.

Then check what came with it. Ten pounds of 10-10-10 also delivered a pound of phosphate and a
pound of potash whether or not the soil needed them. That is the number worth looking at before
you buy the balanced bag out of habit.

## Before you buy anything

Get a soil test. Nitrogen moves and leaches, so it genuinely needs replacing each season.
Phosphate and potash accumulate. A test — cheap through most extension services — tells you
which of the three you are actually short of, and will often tell you that the answer is
nitrogen alone.

Buying fertilizer without one is guessing, and the usual outcome of the guess is years of
unnecessary phosphate.

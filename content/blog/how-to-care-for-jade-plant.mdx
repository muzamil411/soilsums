A jade plant is the houseplant that looks like a tree and lives like a tortoise, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for jade plant. It grows slowly, forgives neglect, and can outlive its owner — Penn State Extension says it can live up to 100 years — and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: a sunny window, a gritty mix that drains fast, and water only when the soil is dry. Then the same plant drops its leaves in a single week in the hands of an owner who waters it every Sunday out of love, because how to care for jade plant is not the same as caring for the leafy tropical plants on the same windowsill. A jade plant is a succulent shrub from the dry hills of South Africa that stores water in its thick leaves and stems, and it wants four or more hours of direct sun, a pot it cannot tip over, and a winter that is cool and dry. Give it those, and it grows into a miniature tree you can hand down to your children.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A jade plant that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go thin, wrinkled, and soft, and then they plump back up soon after watering. A jade plant that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go soft and drop off while still plump and green, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, goes deep green, and leans toward the window, and its leaves lose the red edge that says the light is right. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much grows soft and leggy. Learning to read those five signals — wrinkled leaves, dropping leaves, stretch, scorch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for jade plant, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are the University of Wisconsin Extension, Penn State Extension, Clemson Extension, the University of Connecticut, and NC State Extension, with South Dakota State University Extension, UF/IFAS Extension, and the ASPCA corroborating the size, pest, and toxicity figures. If you bought the plant because someone told you it brings good luck, the luck section covers that honestly. If you bought it because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the dropping-leaves section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most.

## How to care for jade plant: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it four or more hours of direct sun, and introduce strong sun gradually.** A south-facing window with four or more hours of direct sun is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for jade plant, because the plant is a sun plant from a dry, bright place — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes deep green, and leans, while a plant moved from shade into strong sun in one day scorches.

**Let the soil dry out between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the soil is dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for jade plant.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room by day and a cool room by night, never below 50°F if you can help it, and inside before the first frost. Cold is the third rule of how to care for jade plant — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single freezing night can kill a plant that survived a decade indoors.

### The last two rules in how to care for jade plant

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed every other month from April through September at half strength, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding grows soft, leggy stems, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots.

**Keep it in a gritty mix, in a heavy pot with holes, and repot only every two to three years.** A cactus or succulent mix in a clay pot with drainage holes, repotted every two to three years into a pot just a little bigger — and a heavy pot, because a jade plant grows top-heavy and tips over. A pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots, and a mix that holds water like ordinary potting soil does the same. Free drainage is the fifth rule of how to care for jade plant, and it protects the roots for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for jade plant does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays compact and upright: thick, glossy leaves in opposite pairs, mid to dark green with red margins when they receive sufficient sunlight, per Penn State Extension — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it grows into a miniature tree: a thick, woody trunk with stout branches, per Clemson Extension, that looks like a bonsai even when nobody prunes it — and that trunk is the reason the plant is worth keeping for decades. Third, it lasts: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and repotted rarely grows to 2 to 4 feet tall over many years, per UF/IFAS Extension, and may even flower once it is mature — tight, rounded clusters of small white or pink, star-shaped flowers, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — which is the reward for getting the five rules right. The five rules in how to care for jade plant are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the dry hills of South Africa, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a miniature tree in a pot

The first thing to get straight in how to care for jade plant is what the plant actually is, because the name tells you only half the story. It is a jade plant — *Crassula ovata*, also sold under the older names *Crassula argentea*, *Crassula portulaca*, and *Crassula obliqua*, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — a subtropical, broadleaf evergreen, flowering, succulent shrub in the stonecrop family, per NC State Extension. The name *Crassula* comes from the Latin word *crassus*, meaning thick or fat, referring to the plant's fleshy nature, and *ovata* means egg-shaped, referring to the shape of its leaves, per Penn State Extension. It is also called the friendship plant, the money plant, the money tree, and the jade tree — and those names are the reason so many people buy one as a housewarming gift, because in some cultures the plant symbolizes good luck, fortune, and prosperity, per South Dakota State University Extension.

### A plant from the dry hills of South Africa

In the wild the plant comes from South Africa and Mozambique, per Penn State Extension, and the University of Connecticut says the same: native to South Africa and Mozambique, a favorite houseplant in the Americas and Europe for over 100 years. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives the range more precisely: *Crassula ovata* is a prominent component of valley thicket vegetation of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, growing into a small, rounded evergreen shrub to 6 feet on dry, rocky hillsides. NC State Extension says the same in its own words: a native of South Africa and a popular, low maintenance houseplant worldwide. That background explains almost everything about how to care for jade plant. It is a plant from a dry, bright place where the rain comes rarely and the soil drains instantly, and it wants strong light, warm air, and a soil that dries out completely between rains. It is not a tropical leafy plant and it is not a cactus — it is a succulent that stores water in its leaves, stems, and roots, per Penn State Extension, which is why it tolerates drought and why it rots when it is kept wet. Clemson Extension says the same in its own words: jade plants are tough, easy-to-grow succulents that grow well in containers and like the warm, dry conditions found in most homes.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a small tree in miniature: a thick, woody trunk with stout brown branches, carrying pairs of thick, glossy, egg-shaped leaves clustered at the ends of the branches. Penn State Extension describes the leaves as shiny, fleshy, and oval, 1 to 3 inches long and ¾ to 1½ inches wide, mid to dark green with red margins when they receive sufficient sunlight — and the stems as thick and fleshy with distinct rings, green or reddish brown when new, becoming woody and brownish-gray as they age. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: the smooth, rounded, glossy, egg-shaped leaves are about 1 to 3½ inches long and ¾ to 1½ inches wide, borne in opposite pairs, with each pair at right angles to the next pair, clustered at the ends of the branches rather than spaced evenly along them. The leaves should be edged or tinged with red when grown with sufficient light, and new stems are green and very succulent, just like the leaves, but they become brown and woody as they mature. The red edge is the plant's light meter: a plant with red-edged leaves is getting enough sun, and a plant with plain deep-green leaves is not. A plant that is getting enough light holds its leaves firm and upright; a plant in too little light stretches, its leaves go deep green and droop, and the whole plant leans toward the window. That lean is the first thing to check when you bring a plant home: a plant that leans is a plant that is asking for a brighter window — and reading that lean is the first skill in how to care for jade plant.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for jade plant, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the size, because the plant grows slowly but steadily, and the pot decides how big it gets. Clemson Extension says jade plants can live for a very long time and grow into small trees or shrubs up to 5 feet tall indoors. The University of Connecticut says jade plants can live a long time and may reach 5 feet or so in height. South Dakota State University Extension says a mature jade plant can grow to be six feet tall and three feet wide over time. Penn State Extension says the plant grows naturally as an evergreen shrub reaching heights of up to 6 feet, and that in small pots it will tend to have a small root system and stay small, but with more space it can grow into large shrubs. UF/IFAS Extension gives the measured range for a landscape plant: 2 to 4 feet tall with a spread of 1 to 3 feet. Buy the size for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a windowsill, a standard plant for a bright corner, and a large plant only if you have sunny floor space — because a plant that reaches 5 feet needs room, and a plant in a small pot stays small only until its roots run out of room.

### The age that matters: a hundred years

The second is the age, because the flowers and the trunk both come only to a mature plant. Penn State Extension says the jade plant can live up to 100 years, and South Dakota State University Extension says the same: a succulent that can live approximately 100 years with proper care. The trunk thickens with age — the University of Wisconsin Extension says the bark peels from the trunk in horizontal brownish strips on old plants, suggesting great age even in young specimens — and the flowers come only to a mature plant, in winter, in response to long nights. A plant you buy this year will not flower this year — and a plant kept indoors may never flower at all — and that is normal, not a failure. The flowers are a bonus, not the point, and a plant that never flowers is still a healthy plant. What the age buys you is the trunk: a jade plant kept for ten years looks like a miniature tree, and a jade plant kept for fifty years looks like an heirloom. That is why the plant is worth buying young and keeping: you are not buying a decoration, you are starting a tree, and that long view is the point of how to care for jade plant.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, plump, and firm, glossy green with red edges, with no soft spots, no wrinkled leaves, and no bare stems where leaves have already dropped. Plump green leaves dropping off mean the plant has already been over-watered in the shop, and those leaves will not grow back on that stem. Thin, wrinkled leaves mean the plant has gone too long without water — which is fixable, because the leaves plump back up after a drink — but a plant that is already wrinkled in the shop has been neglected, and a plump plant is a better buy. The soil should be dry on top, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes and enough weight that the plant cannot tip over. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a light plastic pot with a heavy top will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. Buy the plump, firm, right-sized plant in dry soil in a heavy pot, and the rest of how to care for jade plant is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for jade plant, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. The University of Wisconsin Extension says a jade plant does best with four or more hours of direct sun, but will survive in bright, indirect light. Penn State Extension says jade plants prefer full to partial sun and will thrive in a south-facing window, and will grow and look their best when they get four or more hours of direct sunlight daily. Clemson Extension says the same: jades prefer the full sun or bright filtered light of a south-facing window, and do best where they get four or more hours a day of direct sunlight. The University of Connecticut says jade plants enjoy a sunny, southern exposure. NC State Extension adds the softening: place the pot in a sunny location indoors that also provides some afternoon shade, and bright light is essential for good plant health. The planning figure this guide uses is a south-facing window with four or more hours of direct sun a day — a west-facing window also works — and no dim rooms at any time of year. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the light that keeps the plant compact, upright, and red-edged.

### The sunburn warning

The warning that matters most is the sudden one. The University of Connecticut says not to move plants from subdued lighting into a full sun situation — instead, gradually expose them to brighter light so the leaves will not get sunburn. Penn State Extension says the same: plants may be moved outdoors for the summer but must be gradually acclimated to the higher-intensity outdoor sunlight to prevent sunburn. The University of Wisconsin Extension says houseplants can be moved outdoors for the summer but need to be acclimated gradually to the higher light intensity outdoors to prevent sunburn, and must be brought indoors before the first frost. The burn shows as tan or brown dry patches on the sunny side of the leaves, and a scorched leaf never recovers its color — the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. If your plant came from a dim shop shelf, give it bright indirect light for two weeks, then move it into a spot that gets an hour or two of direct morning sun, and then into its permanent sunny position over the next two to three weeks. That gradual move is the light rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters second is the delayed one. A plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never grows properly, and the owner concludes that jade plants simply grow slowly, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. The University of Wisconsin Extension says inadequate light will produce a plant with deep green leaves and drooping stems — there is nothing wrong with the plant other than it does not have enough light to produce normal compact growth and reddish coloration. Penn State Extension says insufficient sunlight will cause the leaves to become light green, and they will not have attractive red edges — and that a light positioned too far away will cause the plant to stretch toward the light and become leggy. If your plant leans toward the window, or its leaves have gone deep green and droopy, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for jade plant, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Grow lights when there is no south window

If you do not have a south-facing window, a grow light is a real option, not a compromise. Penn State Extension says that if you do not have a south-facing window and find that your plant is not thriving, you may consider full-spectrum artificial lighting, which contains both red and blue light — keep the light source close to the plant, reposition the light as the plant grows larger, and give it twelve hours of artificial light, but be sure to turn the light off after that time, as all plants need some hours of darkness to remain healthy. A light hung a foot above the plant for twelve hours a day gives the plant the same four-plus hours of strong light it would get from a south window, and the red edges will come back within a few weeks. Do not leave the light on all night: the plant needs darkness, and the flowers, when they come, answer to long nights. That twelve-hour rule is the grow-light rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Water

Watering is where most jade plants are lost, and the loss usually comes from one mistake: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain. The watering rule from the University of Wisconsin Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for jade plant: jade plants do best when the soil is allowed to dry out between deep waterings. Two halves, both required: let the soil dry out, and then water deeply. A deep watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. And then remove the excess, so the pot never sits in water.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for jade plant

Penn State Extension gives the same rule in fewer words: plants should be watered infrequently and only when the soil is completely dry. Clemson Extension gives the same rule with the seasons named: the soil should be kept moist but not wet while plants are actively growing in spring and summer, and during the winter, let the soil dry between waterings. The University of Connecticut gives the same rule with the months named: when plants are actively growing, usually from about mid-March through mid-October, they like to be watered enough so the soil remains moderately moist — some growers soak the plant once a week or so and let it drain thoroughly, then repeat once the top inch or so of soil feels dry — and during the other six months of the year, when temperatures are cooler and the sun's rays are weaker and the days are shorter, plants slow their growth and require less water. NC State Extension says to water in moderation when the soil is dry and to reduce watering from fall to late winter. All five sources agree on the shape of the rule — dry, then soak — and they disagree only on how dry, because the right dryness depends on the pot, the room, and the season.

### How often to water

The planning figure this guide uses is simple: push a finger an inch or two into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — roughly every one to two weeks in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to every three to four weeks in a cool room in winter. That range satisfies all the sources, and it keeps the soil dry between waterings without ever letting the plant shrivel. It is a planning figure, not a schedule from any source: no extension source gives a fixed watering calendar for jade plants, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season. A small pot in a warm, bright room dries faster than a large pot in a cool, dim one, and a plant in summer drinks faster than the same plant in winter. The thick leaves store water, per Penn State Extension, which is why a missed watering is forgiven — and why a plant that is watered every week, whether it needs it or not, rots. That finger test is the schedule in how to care for jade plant, and it is the only one that works in every house.

### The water on the leaves

There is one watering rule in how to care for jade plant that is different from the leafy tropical plants: do not splash water on the leaves. Clemson Extension says to avoid splashing water on the leaves while watering. The reason is not the water itself — the plant is not sensitive to fluoride or salts the way a spider plant is, and no extension source I verified gives a water-quality rule for it — but because water sitting on the leaves in a cool room invites the problems the troubleshooting section covers, and because a plant that is watered from above, onto its leaves, is a plant whose soil is never checked. Water at the soil, not over the leaves, and check the soil with a finger before every watering. What matters is that the excess leaves the pot. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the soil should not be allowed to sit in water. Penn State Extension says a cactus or succulent mix is ideal and the soil must drain quickly, or root rot may result. A plant in a pot with no drainage holes, watered on a schedule, will rot no matter how careful the owner is — because the water has nowhere to go, and the roots sit in it. That drainage is the watering rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is decided by the pot, not by the watering can.

### Temperature

Temperature is the easiest of the three to get right, because the range the plant wants is the range a warm house already has — with cooler nights than most owners expect. The sources give a small spread, and it is worth showing honestly. Clemson Extension says jade plants do best with daytime temperatures between 65 and 75°F, and nighttime temperatures between 50 and 55°F. The University of Connecticut says to keep plants around 60 to 70°F during the day, but temperatures can drop into the 50s at night. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant is tolerant of a wide range of temperature and humidity and may even tolerate light frost, but will be killed by freezing conditions. Penn State Extension says the plant is not frost-hardy and must be returned indoors before the first frost, and lists it as hardy in USDA zones 11 to 12. UF/IFAS Extension lists zones 10 through 11. NC State Extension says bright light and average to cool indoor temperatures are essential for good plant health.

The planning figure this guide uses is an ordinary warm room by day, 65 to 75°F, with cooler nights in the 50s, never below 50°F indoors if you can help it, and inside before the first frost. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is ordinary indoor temperature in a house that is not overheated. That is why temperature is the easiest of the three decisions in how to care for jade plant: the house already provides it, and the job is simply not to put the plant somewhere colder than the room itself — or outside on a night when frost is forecast, because a single frost can kill a plant that survived a decade indoors.

### What to avoid: cold and drafts

Two things matter more than the exact number. The first is cold: do not put the plant against freezing glass in winter, do not leave it outside on a night when frost is forecast, and do not put it next to a drafty window or an exterior door. Clemson Extension says that during the winter months, you should protect plants from drafts and not let their foliage touch windowpanes. A chill that a hardy houseplant would ignore can damage this plant, and the damage shows as dropped leaves and blackened stems. The second is heat: do not put the plant next to a radiator or a heating vent, and do not leave it in a room that sits above 85°F for days. Hot, dry air is not the problem — the plant tolerates the warm, dry conditions found in most homes, per Clemson Extension — but a room that is hot and wet is, because the soil stays damp and the roots rot. Warm by day, cool by night, away from freezing glass, is the temperature rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Humidity

Humidity is the non-problem, and the one most owners overthink. The jade plant comes from a dry place, and the dry air of a centrally heated home in winter is exactly what it wants. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant is tolerant of a wide range of temperature and humidity and likes the warm, dry conditions found in most homes. No extension source I verified gives a humidity percentage for jade plants, so this guide gives none — the honest statement is that average household humidity is usually fine, and that dry winter air suits the plant well. Do not mist the leaves, do not set up a pebble tray, and do not run a humidifier for this plant: water sitting on the leaves invites disease, and humidity is the one thing this plant does not want. Get the humidity wrong in the other direction — a damp, steamy bathroom — and the plant shows it with soft leaves and dropped leaves, which is the most common bathroom complaint in how to care for jade plant.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

The soil rule in how to care for jade plant is short: gritty, fast-draining, in a pot with holes. The University of Wisconsin Extension says jade needs well-drained conditions and does best in well-drained, high-porosity potting mixes designed for succulents or cacti, and that to increase the drainage of standard potting mix you should add extra perlite or sharp sand to the media before potting. Penn State Extension says well-drained soil is a must, as root rot may result if the soil does not drain quickly or the plant is overwatered, and a cactus or succulent potting mix is ideal. Clemson Extension says jades need a very well-drained soil mix to prevent root rot, and that a cactus mix with some added organic matter will do. The University of Connecticut says potting mixes for jade plants need to be very well draining, because plants tend to be top-heavy, and commercial potting mixes for succulents usually contain sand, grit, rock chips, or other weighty materials. NC State Extension says to plant in well-drained potting soil that contains a lot of loam. All five sources agree, and none of them asks for anything rich — which is the point. This plant does not need a rich mix or a special recipe. It needs a mix that drains.

### A simple mix that drains

The planning figure this guide uses is a commercial cactus or succulent mix, or the Clemson recipe of one part sterilized organic soil, one part sphagnum peat moss, and three parts coarse sand by volume. Free drainage is the foundation of how to care for jade plant in a pot, because every watering rule in this guide assumes the excess can leave. Our [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs, and our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself — a recipe of potting soil, perlite, and compost that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy. The comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite is the right lightener here: it drains and keeps air in the mix, and this plant needs drainage more than it needs moisture retention — the mix should dry out between waterings, not hold water like a sponge. The University of Connecticut adds one detail worth knowing: if the mix does not already list it, add two tablespoons of ground limestone for every gallon of potting mix, because jade plants prefer a pH of around 6.5. The thick leaves store water already, so a mix that holds too much water on top of that is a mix that rots.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the other half of the drainage rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is the half most owners get wrong — because a jade plant grows top-heavy, and a light pot falls over. Penn State Extension says that while you may choose any type of pot with a drainage hole, clay or terracotta pots are ideal, because these are porous and allow the soil to dry out quicker than glazed or plastic ones. The University of Connecticut says the same: clay or ceramic pots are ideal to use for these hefty succulents. Clemson Extension says that when your plant becomes older and top-heavy, you should move it to a large, heavy pot to prevent tipping over. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same from the other side: it is best to repot every two to three years or when a plant becomes top-heavy and susceptible to tipping over. A clay or terracotta pot is best for two reasons at once: the porous walls let excess moisture evaporate, so the soil dries faster, and the weight keeps a top-heavy plant upright. A plant in a light plastic pot without holes, however pretty the pot, will stay wet longer and fall over sooner. That heavy clay pot is the potting rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is decided by the pot, not by the plant.

### Repotting: every two to three years, or when it tips

Repotting is the part of how to care for jade plant that surprises people, because the answer is to do it rarely — and because the plant tells you when it is time in a way that is easy to miss. Penn State Extension says jade plants are happy to be root-bound but may be repotted every 2 to 3 years. The University of Wisconsin Extension says individual plants can be grown for many years while root-bound, although it is best to repot them every two to three years or when a plant becomes top-heavy and susceptible to tipping over, and the best time to repot is as new growth starts. Clemson Extension says jade plants can live quite happily for years while root-bound, and that if repotting is necessary, you should do it as new growth starts. The University of Connecticut says plants usually need repotting every 2 to 3 years. The signal is the tipping: when the plant falls over every time someone brushes past it, or when the roots poke out of the drainage holes, or when the plant has grown so top-heavy that the pot cannot hold it, the plant has outgrown its home. And when the soil has gone hard and water runs straight through without wetting it, that is also a repotting signal — refresh the mix, and put the plant back in the same pot if it is still the right size.

### Go one size up, and water sparingly until it settles

When you do repot, go only one pot size up — an inch or two wider — and keep the plant at the same depth, with the leaves well above the soil level. Do not bury the lower leaves: leaves buried in soil rot, and a plant potted too deep rots from the base upward. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to prune the roots when re-potting into the same size pot and to cut back the stems to maintain the shape and encourage development of a thick main trunk, and to water sparingly until the plant is established in the new container. Clemson Extension says the same: allow the soil to become dry after repotting. Do not break the root ball apart or wash the old soil off: the roots resent disturbance, and a rough repotting can set the plant back a whole season. Water sparingly until the plant is established in its new pot, and wait before the first deep watering after repotting, so any damaged roots can callus over before they meet wet soil. A plant that is repotted gently, rarely, and one size up will keep growing steadily for years; a plant that is repotted often and roughly will drop its leaves every time. That restraint is the repotting rule in how to care for jade plant.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. The University of Wisconsin Extension says jade plants can be fertilized every two months during active growth, or you can use a dilute fertilizer more frequently. Penn State Extension says to fertilize plants using a balanced, liquid houseplant fertilizer, and to apply fertilizer 2 to 3 times in the spring and summer to encourage growth — but not to over-fertilize, as that may cause the plant to become leggy — and if you repot your plant, wait four months before applying fertilizer. Clemson Extension says to fertilize jade plants once every three to four months, and that a liquid houseplant fertilizer will work well, and to wait four months before feeding repotted plants. The University of Connecticut says to feed plants every other month with a flowering houseplant fertilizer from April through September. None of the sources asks for much — which is itself the message: the plant is a light feeder, and feeding is not a core part of its care. The honest reading of the spread is that the plant needs very little, and that a plant that is never fed at all will do fine, while a plant that is fed heavily will grow soft and leggy. That spread is the feeding question in how to care for jade plant, and the answer is: very little.

### The planning figure: every other month, or none at all

The planning figure this guide uses is a light feed every other month from April through September at half strength, or no feed at all — and nothing at all from October through March. That range satisfies all the sources: it is the middle of the spread, it is dilute rather than full-strength, and it stops when growth stops. All the sources agree on the season, and on stopping when growth stops, which is the part of how to care for jade plant that matters most: a plant that is fed only while it is growing never accumulates the salts that burn its roots — and never grows so soft that it flops over under its own weight. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half the label strength, feed only when the soil is already moist, never onto dry soil, and skip a feeding if the plant has just been repotted — wait four months after repotting before feeding, per Penn State Extension and Clemson Extension — or if the plant is showing stress. If the leaves are already soft from over-feeding, flush the soil with plain water — a thorough watering that runs out of the drainage holes, repeated once — and stop feeding until spring. That light hand is the feeding rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is the cheapest of the five rules to follow: a single bottle of balanced liquid feed lasts years at this rate.

### What over-feeding does

A jade plant that puts on a few inches a year does not need much food, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots and soften the stems. Penn State Extension says over-fertilizing may cause the plant to become leggy. The plant grows soft and stretched, its stems lose their firm upright stance, and it becomes more prone to rot — because soft, over-fed growth rots faster than firm, slow growth. That is the double cost of over-feeding in how to care for jade plant: leggy stems and rot, at the same time, from the same mistake. A plant that is fed lightly in summer and not at all in winter stays firm, upright, and compact, and it is the plant that makes flowers. Use a balanced liquid fertilizer at half strength every other month from April through September, feed only when the soil is already moist, and skip a feeding if the plant is stressed. That restraint is the feeding rule in how to care for jade plant.

## Pruning it into a little tree

Pruning is the part of how to care for jade plant that owners skip, and it is the part that makes the difference between a floppy shrub and a miniature tree. The University of Wisconsin Extension says jade plants benefit from pruning to keep them compact and growing vigorously, and that this is best done in spring by cutting stems back to a lateral branch — pruning encourages the trunk to develop to be able to support the weight of the heavy leaves and stems, and also encourages root growth. They can be pruned into miniature trees and make an excellent choice for a succulent bonsai specimen or a wonderful sculptural plant. The cuts will heal over in a few days and new growth will sprout within a few weeks. Penn State Extension says the same: their branches may be trimmed to help them remain small and shapely, best done in the spring, because pruning will keep them compact and encourage new growth — using a clean, sharp blade, cut the stems back just above a ring or to a lateral branch, and these cuts will heal over a few days, and new growth will sprout. Remove any broken or crossed branches and any damaged or yellowed leaves. A plant that is never pruned grows long, bare stems with leaves only at the tips, and then it falls over under its own weight. A plant that is pruned every spring grows a thick trunk and a full crown, and that is the tree the plant wants to be. That crown is the goal of pruning in how to care for jade plant.

### Prune in spring, back to a side branch

The method is simple, and the timing matters more than the technique. Prune in spring, as new growth starts, when the plant is about to grow anyway — that is when the cuts heal fastest and the new growth sprouts soonest. Cut each stem back to a lateral branch or just above a ring, per Penn State Extension, using a clean, sharp blade — a dirty blade spreads disease, and a blunt blade crushes the stem instead of cutting it. Cut back the longest stems first, the ones that make the plant lopsided, and then step back and look at the shape before cutting more: you can always cut more next week, but you cannot put a stem back. The pieces you cut off are not waste — they are cuttings, and the propagation section shows how to turn them into new plants. Wash your hands after pruning, because the sap can irritate skin, per South Dakota State University Extension. That spring pruning is the shaping rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Pinching for bushiness

For a young plant, pinching does the work of pruning without a blade. Penn State Extension says pinching the tips of the stems where there is new growth, ⅛ of an inch above a leaf pair, will help the plant remain bushy and compact. Each pinched tip grows two new stems where one grew before, so a plant pinched twice in spring grows four stems where it had one — and that branching is what builds the full crown of a miniature tree. Pinch in spring and early summer, while the plant is growing, and stop by late summer, so the new growth has time to harden before winter. A plant that is pinched young never needs the heavy pruning an old, leggy plant needs — and that is the cheapest shaping in how to care for jade plant: a thumbnail, in spring, above a leaf pair.

## Getting it to flower

Getting a jade plant to flower is the hardest trick in how to care for jade plant, and the honest answer is that most indoor plants never do — and that is normal. Clemson Extension says clusters of star-shaped white or pink flowers will appear on mature plants in the winter. The University of Connecticut says mature plants produce starry white or pink blossoms during the winter months. The University of Wisconsin Extension says tight, rounded clusters of small white or pink, star-shaped flowers are produced in response to long nights, with a faint sweet scent, attractive to bees, wasps, flies, beetles, and butterflies — and that under ideal conditions and bright light, flower clusters can cover the plant in masses thick enough to hide the foliage. Penn State Extension says that when grown outdoors in the proper climate, they will have clusters of white, star-shaped flowers tinged with pink, but they typically will not flower indoors. NC State Extension says tiny pink flowers appear in the spring on plants grown outdoors, and that the plant does not usually bloom in home environments. The flowers are a winter bonus on a mature plant, not a promise — and a plant that never flowers is still a healthy plant, which is the honest starting point for how to care for jade plant.

### The fall treatment: cold, dry, and dark

The treatment that triggers flowers is a cold, dry, dark autumn, and the University of Wisconsin Extension gives it exactly: to encourage bloom, withhold water in the fall and keep plants under cool conditions, around 55°F, especially at night, in an area that will not receive any supplemental light, so the natural shortening days of the season will trigger flower initiation — several weeks of cold, dry, dark treatment, followed by regular watering, should result in flowering. The University of Connecticut says the same in fewer words: cooler nights and avoidance of supplemental light after the sun sets encourages bud formation. In practice that means three things from October onward: move the plant to the coolest bright room in the house, water it only enough to keep the leaves from puckering, and turn the room lights off at sunset — no lamp beside the plant in the evening, because evening light tells the plant the nights are still short. After several weeks of that treatment, go back to regular watering, and watch for buds at the tips of the branches in winter. That fall treatment is the flowering rule in how to care for jade plant, and it is decided by where the pot sits and when the lights go off, not by anything you buy.

### Why it never flowers indoors

If your plant has never flowered, the reason is almost always one of three things, and all three are normal. The first is youth: only a mature plant flowers, and a plant under a few years old has not matured enough to bloom, no matter what you do. The second is warmth: a plant kept at 70°F all winter, watered every week, never gets the cool, dry signal that tells it winter has come — and without that signal, there are no buds. The third is light at night: a plant that sits beside a lamp that burns until midnight never experiences the long nights the flowers answer to, per the University of Wisconsin Extension. Fix all three — a mature plant, a cool dry autumn, and no evening light — and the plant may flower in winter. Fix only one, and it probably will not. That honesty is part of how to care for jade plant: the flowers are a reward for a mature plant given a real autumn, not a right, and the trunk is the real prize anyway.

## Making more plants for free

Making more plants is the easiest part of how to care for jade plant, and it is also the most generous — because the plant roots from a single leaf, and every pruning gives you cuttings to share. The University of Wisconsin Extension says jade plant is especially easy to propagate from stem or leaf cuttings, and that in the wild, leaves or pieces of plant that break off and fall to the ground will root in a few weeks. That is why the plant is called the friendship plant: a leaf given to a friend becomes a plant, and a plant given to a friend becomes a tree.

### Leaf cuttings: the friendship method

The leaf method is the one to start with, because it needs nothing but a leaf and a pot. The University of Wisconsin Extension says that when taking cuttings, it is best to allow the pieces to dry for a few days so the cut surface will heal over and be less likely to rot, and that inserting the cut end into fairly dry, well-drained soil will speed rooting, although they will produce roots even out of soil. The University of Connecticut gives the steps for leaves: remove a few of the opposing leaf pairs, leave them in an open area for the ends of the leaves to callus over, then stick them in some moistened potting mix in a small container, put a clear plastic bag over it to retain moisture, and roots should form in a few weeks, after which the small plants can be potted up. NC State Extension says propagation is easily achieved by leaf cutting. Twist a healthy leaf off cleanly, so the whole leaf comes away with its base intact — a leaf torn in half will not root — lay it on dry cactus mix for a few days until the cut end calluses, then set it on moistened mix in a small pot. Water only lightly until roots appear: a leaf that is kept wet before it calluses rots instead of rooting. In a few weeks a tiny plant grows from the base of the leaf, and in a few months it is a plant of its own. That callus is the propagation rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Stem cuttings: the fast method

The stem method is faster, and it is what the prunings are for. Clemson Extension says you can start new plants from stem cuttings. The University of Wisconsin Extension says cuttings root most easily in summer, but jade plant can be propagated at any time of the year. Take a stem cutting 3 to 4 inches long, with a clean, sharp blade, let it dry for a few days so the cut surface heals over, then insert the cut end into fairly dry, well-drained soil. Water sparingly until it is established, and keep it in bright, indirect light — not full sun — until it roots. In a few weeks it roots, and in a few months it is a small plant with its own trunk starting to form. A stem cutting grows faster than a leaf cutting, because it already has a stem, and it is the method to use when you want a second plant this year rather than next. That stem is the fast propagation in how to care for jade plant.

### What about seeds?

Seeds are the one method the sources mention and nobody uses, and that is worth saying plainly. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant can also be grown from seeds sown in spring or summer, and that under good conditions, pollinated flowers produce a small capsule filled with seed. But seeds need flowers first, and flowers need a mature plant given a cold, dry, dark autumn — and even then, the seeds grow slowly, and the plant that grows from them may not look like its parent. Leaf and stem cuttings are faster, surer, and true to the parent plant, and they are the methods the extension sources describe in detail. If you want more plants, take cuttings in spring and summer, and give them away — a mature plant in a sunny window makes enough cuttings for the whole street, and that generosity is the propagation rule in how to care for jade plant.

### A worked example: one plant, bought today

Here is the whole first year as a single story, for a plant bought on Saturday, 21 November 2026. The plant is a standard jade plant, 12 inches tall in a 6-inch clay pot, glossy green with red edges, firm to the touch, with a thick little trunk already forming. You put it on your sunniest windowsill, where it gets four or more hours of direct sun and no shade, and you mark Sundays for the finger test: push a finger an inch or two into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry, emptying the saucer every time. You do not feed it at all: it is November, and the plant is slowing down for winter, so feeding waits until spring.

Through the winter you water it every three to four weeks, because the plant is barely growing and the soil stays dry for weeks — and you keep it in the coolest bright room at night, with the lights off at sunset, because a cool, dry, dark autumn is what makes flowers. The leaves stay plump and upright, and the red edges deepen in the winter sun. In spring you feed it for the first time: a balanced liquid fertilizer at half strength, every other month, and nothing more. The plant grows a few inches over the year, puts out new leaves from the tips of its branches, and by next autumn the trunk has thickened and the crown has filled out — and you pinch the tips of the longest stems, ⅛ of an inch above a leaf pair, per Penn State Extension, so it grows bushy instead of leggy.

### Spring: the first pruning and the first cuttings in how to care for jade plant

The pruning comes in spring 2027, back to a lateral branch, per the University of Wisconsin Extension, when the new growth starts — and the cuttings you take become three new plants, each in a small clay pot of cactus mix, given to friends. The repotting comes in spring 2028, one pot size up, when the plant gets top-heavy and tips over, and you wait four months before feeding it again, per Clemson Extension. After a few years of that routine, the plant is 2 to 4 feet tall, per UF/IFAS Extension, with a thick woody trunk and a full crown, and it has made a dozen new plants you gave away. If it is mature and it had a cool, dry autumn with no evening light, it may send up tight clusters of small white or pink, star-shaped flowers in winter, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — and those flowers are the sign the plant has had everything it needs. That is the whole story of how to care for jade plant: a finger in the soil, the sunniest window, a warm day and a cool night, a gritty mix, a little food in summer, a heavy pot, and patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most jade plant problems have one of four causes — water, light, cold, or feeding — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a dropped leaf never grows back on that stem. Troubleshooting in how to care for jade plant is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Dropping leaves

Plump green leaves dropping off, with wet soil, mean overwatering — and it is the most serious problem this plant has. The University of Wisconsin Extension says overwatering will cause the leaves to drop and the stem to rot. Clemson Extension says root rot usually results from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or from overly frequent watering. NC State Extension says leaf fall and root rot can result from overwatering. The fix is to stop watering immediately, take the plant out of its pot, cut away the soft, brown, rotted roots with a clean knife, and repot what is left into dry cactus mix in a clay pot with drainage holes. Do not water it for a week, and then water only when the soil is dry all the way down. A plant caught early recovers; a plant whose whole stem base has gone soft usually does not. If the leaves are dropping and the soil is bone dry, the cause is the opposite: Clemson Extension says leaf drop can happen if the plant is allowed to become extremely dry, and the University of Connecticut says that if a plant is not given enough water, it will often begin losing leaves and may shrivel. Water it thoroughly once, let it drain, and go back to the finger test. That soil check is the leaf-drop rule in how to care for jade plant: wet soil means too much water, dry soil means too little, and the leaves alone cannot tell you which.

### Wrinkled, thin leaves

Thin, wrinkled leaves that feel soft and papery, with bone-dry soil, mean the plant has gone too long without water — and this one is fixable. The University of Connecticut says that if a plant is not given enough water, it will often begin losing leaves and may shrivel. Give the plant a thorough watering, until water runs out of the drainage holes, and check it again in a day: the leaves should be firming up. If they do not plump back up, check the roots: a plant whose roots have dried out and died cannot take up water, and it needs repotting into fresh mix. Do not overcorrect — a thirsty plant that is suddenly drowned rots — so water thoroughly once, let it drain, and then go back to the finger test. That restraint is the thirst rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Soft, mushy stems

A soft, mushy stem that collapses when you touch it means bacterial soft rot, and it is the one problem with no fix. The University of Connecticut says bacterial soft rot may cause plant tissues of the stems and branches to grow soft and collapse, and that there is no control method and infected plants should be discarded. South Dakota State University Extension says the same: bacterial soft rot is when the stems and branches collapse, the interior of the plant is soft and mushy, there is no solution for this disease, and you will end up discarding the plant. The cause is overwatering in a pot that cannot drain, and the prevention is the drainage rule: a gritty mix, a pot with holes, and water only when the soil is dry. If only one branch is soft, cut it off well below the soft part with a clean blade, let the cut callus, and keep the rest of the plant dry — but if the main trunk is soft, the plant is lost, and the cuttings you took in spring are your replacement. That dryness is the rot rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Leggy, stretched growth

A plant that stretches, with long bare stems and leaves spaced far apart, is short of light — and it will not shrink back. Penn State Extension says a light positioned too far away will cause the plant to stretch toward the light and become leggy. The University of Wisconsin Extension says inadequate light will produce a plant with deep green leaves and drooping stems. Move the plant to your sunniest window, or hang a grow light close above it, per Penn State Extension, and wait: the new leaves from the tips will grow compact and upright, even though the stretched stem stays stretched. If the plant has become too tall and floppy to stand, prune it back in spring to a lateral branch, per the University of Wisconsin Extension, and root the cuttings — the pruned plant will sprout new growth within a few weeks, and the cuttings are your replacement plants. That light is the stretch rule in how to care for jade plant: the sunniest window, or a grow light close above, and no dim rooms.

### Sunburn

Sunburn shows as tan or brown dry patches on the side of the plant that faces the sun, appearing a day or two after a sudden move into strong light. The University of Connecticut says to expose the plant to brighter light gradually so the leaves will not get sunburn. Penn State Extension says plants moved outdoors for the summer must be gradually acclimated to the higher-intensity outdoor sunlight to prevent sunburn. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: houseplants moved outdoors for the summer need to be acclimated gradually to the higher light intensity outdoors to prevent sunburn. The fix is to move the plant back into bright indirect light, and to reintroduce the sun gradually over a week or two. A scorched leaf never recovers its color, so leave it in place — it still photosynthesizes — and judge the plant by its new growth. That gradual move is the sunburn rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Brown corky spots: edema

Brown, corky spots on the leaves that look like blisters mean edema, and it is the one problem that looks like a disease but is not. The University of Connecticut says overwatered plants may start to exhibit signs of root rot or a condition known as edema: water taken up by the roots is greater than the plant can use or transpire through the stomates, which encourages blisters to form on the leaves that turn into corky brown spots — and sometimes small white spots that rub off easily appear on the leaves, because the plant releases some water through the leaves along with plant minerals. These two problems most often occur in late winter during cool, cloudy weather. The fix is the watering, not a spray: water less, let the soil dry out between waterings, and give the plant more light and more air. The spots will not heal, but the new leaves will be clean. That dry soil is the edema rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Mealybugs

The jade plant is generally a healthy plant indoors, and pest trouble is uncommon — but there is one pest to watch for. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant has few pests, but mealybugs are the most common insect pest, and the white, fluffy insects can deform new growth — wipe them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, but clean the plants frequently for several days or weeks until all of the pests have been eliminated. Penn State Extension says the same: mealybugs are the most common pest, white and fluffy insects that attach themselves at the juncture of the leaf and stem and can cause deformities in the new growth — use a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol to gently wipe the insects off the plant until all of the pests have been eliminated, which may take several days or weeks. Clemson Extension says mealybugs are the most common insect pest of jade plants, they look like white puffs of cotton, wipe them off with alcohol using a cotton swab — and do not use insecticidal soap, since it may damage jade plants. The University of Connecticut says the nymphs of mealybugs pierce the outer layer of plant tissue, feed on the sap, and excrete honeydew which can cause sooty mold — wiping the infected area with a cotton ball soaked in rubbing alcohol can help control them. Check the tight leaf joints when you water, wipe off any mealybugs you find with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and repeat for several weeks — a single wipe never gets them all. That wipe is the pest rule in how to care for jade plant.

### White dots that rub off: hydathodes

Tiny white or black dots on the leaves look like a pest or a disease, and they are neither. Penn State Extension says you may notice tiny white or black dots on their leaves — these are healthy, water-transferring pores called hydathodes and not a sign of disease. The University of Connecticut says the same from the other side: sometimes small white spots that rub off easily appear on the leaves when the plant has more water than it needs, because some water is released through the leaves along with plant minerals. If the dots rub off easily and the leaves are otherwise firm and green, they are hydathodes, and the fix is nothing — or a little less water, if you have been watering generously. Do not spray them, do not scrub them, and do not throw the plant away: the pores are the plant breathing, and a plant with hydathodes is a plant that is alive. That calm is the hydathode rule in how to care for jade plant.

### Spider mites and scale

Two more pests show up rarely, and both are worth knowing by sight. Clemson Extension says spider mites can cause plants to lose their green color and appear dusty or speckled. The University of Wisconsin Extension says spider mites can also occasionally infest jade plants, and warns to use caution with insecticides, as there can be problems with phytotoxicity on the succulent leaves. South Dakota State University Extension says mealybugs, spider mites, and scale can be a problem, and warns that succulents of all kinds are susceptible to insecticides and oil, which are normally used to eliminate these pests. NC State Extension lists aphids, scale, spider mites, and mealybugs as occasional problems. The treatment is the same as for mealybugs: wipe the leaves with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, repeat for several weeks, and do not reach for insecticidal soap or oil without reading the label — Clemson Extension says insecticidal soap may damage jade plants, and the University of Wisconsin Extension warns that insecticides can be phytotoxic on succulent leaves. A plant that is watered right and kept in bright light rarely gets any of these, and a plant that does get them is usually already stressed by too much water or too little light. That wipe is the mite rule in how to care for jade plant.

## Is jade plant poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for jade plant, because the plant is so common that it sits within reach of pets in millions of homes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/news/are-succulents-safe-have-around-pets) says jade plants (*Crassula ovata*) are considered toxic, potentially causing vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, and loss of appetite if ingested. The ASPCA's toxic-plant list, under the older name *Crassula argentea*, lists the jade plant as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, with the toxic principle unknown and the clinical signs given as vomiting, depression, and incoordination — as quoted by UF/IFAS Extension. South Dakota State University Extension says the same in plain words: keep your cats and dogs away from this plant, because it is toxic to pets. A cat that treats the glossy leaves as a toy will chew them and may bring them back up, and the plant will look ragged — and the cat may vomit, go lethargic, and lose coordination, which is a vet visit, not a shrug.

### The riskiest succulent on the sunny windowsill

That toxic listing is what makes the jade plant the riskiest succulent on the sunny windowsill, and the comparison with its neighbors is worth making: the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) is non-toxic to cats and dogs, the [aloe vera](/blog/how-to-care-for-aloe-vera/) is toxic if eaten, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) is toxic if chewed, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, and the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) is toxic. The jade plant sits at the dangerous end of that row — not the most dangerous plant in the house, and the one you should keep where pets cannot chew it. The ASPCA adds one more warning worth knowing: the jade plant is also called the money plant or money tree, and it is not to be confused with *Pachira aquatica*, which is also called a money tree — the two plants share a common name and nothing else, and only the jade is toxic. If you want a sunny windowsill plant in a house with cats, the spider plant is the plant to put there first. That comparison is the safety check in how to care for jade plant: put the safe plant where pets roam, and the jade where they cannot reach.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Toxic does not mean the plant has to live in a locked room — it means the plant has to live where pets cannot chew it. The ASPCA says that since the jade plant can handle both low and high amounts of sunlight, you should try keeping it in a high up spot that your pet cannot get to. Put it on a high shelf, in a hanging planter, or in a room the pets do not enter, and keep the cuttings out of reach too. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any jade plant and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. For a household with cats, dogs, and small children, that placement is the one safety rule in how to care for jade plant that matters more than any other — and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap is the second safety fact, and the one owners discover while pruning. South Dakota State University Extension says rashes can happen if the sap touches your skin. The sap is the milky juice inside the thick stems and leaves, and it comes out whenever a stem is cut — which is every spring, when you prune. Wash your hands after pruning, wear gloves if your skin is sensitive, and keep the cut ends away from your eyes. The rash is mild and short-lived for most people, and it is not the same as the toxicity to pets — but it is the reason to wash up after pruning, and the reason to keep prunings away from children. That wash is the sap rule in how to care for jade plant.

## The short version

How to care for jade plant, in one paragraph: buy a plump, firm, right-sized plant — a small one for a windowsill, a standard one for a bright corner, a large one only if you have sunny floor space; put it in your sunniest window, with four or more hours of direct sun, introduced gradually over a week or two if it came from a dim shop shelf; water thoroughly only when the soil is dry, drain it fully, and empty the saucer — roughly every one to two weeks in summer, every three to four weeks in a cool winter room; use a cactus or succulent mix, or one part sterilized organic soil, one part sphagnum peat moss, and three parts coarse sand, in a heavy clay pot with drainage holes; keep it at 65 to 75°F by day and in the 50s at night, never below 50°F, and inside before the first frost; feed at half strength every other month from April through September, or not at all, and nothing in autumn and winter; prune in spring, back to a side branch, and pinch the tips to keep it bushy; repot every two to three years, one size up, and wait four months before feeding a repotted plant; take leaf and stem cuttings in spring and summer, let them callus first, and give them away; and keep it where pets cannot chew it, because it is toxic to cats and dogs. Do that, and the 12-inch plant you buy this November will be a 2- to 4-foot miniature tree in a few years, making cuttings you can give away — and a plant you can keep for a century, which is the reward of how to care for jade plant done right.

### And a second plant for the same windowsill

If you want a second houseplant for the same windowsill, the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) thrives in the same bright room but wants more water and is safe for pets, the [aloe vera](/blog/how-to-care-for-aloe-vera/) grows in the same sunny window with the same dry soil but is toxic too, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) flowers in the same bright room but needs steadier moisture, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) blooms in the same cool room, and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) flowers on the same short days with none of the watering worry — though it is non-toxic, so it is the safe one for a house with cats.

Fertilize vegetables **every three to four weeks while they are growing**, not on a fixed
calendar. Work compost in at planting, side-dress with nitrogen through leafy growth, then ease
off nitrogen and lean on potassium once fruiting crops set fruit. Beans and peas need no
nitrogen at all.

## Should you fertilize vegetables every week?

Only in containers. In the ground, weekly feeding is more than the plants can use and most of it
leaches past the roots.

The difference is that a bed holds a reserve and a pot does not. Every watering carries nutrients
out of a pot's drainage holes, so container vegetables genuinely do want a dilute liquid feed
about weekly once they are growing strongly.

| Where it is growing | How often                 | Why                                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Garden bed          | Every 3–4 weeks in growth | Soil holds a reserve between feeds      |
| Raised bed          | Every 3–4 weeks in growth | Same, if it has mineral soil in it      |
| Container           | Weekly, dilute            | Nutrients leach out with every watering |

## Start with a soil test, once

This is not a delaying tactic — it is what makes everything below sensible.

A test tells you whether your soil is short of phosphate and potash, which accumulate, or only of
nitrogen, which leaches away each year. Most established gardens have plenty of phosphate. Many
have adequate potash. Almost all need nitrogen.

If you feed a balanced fertilizer every year without testing, you are adding two nutrients you
probably do not need in order to deliver the one you do. A test through an extension service
costs less than two bags of fertilizer and stays useful for about three years.

## Feed by growth stage, not by date

| Stage                  | What the plant is doing   | What to give                                             |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| At planting            | Establishing roots        | Compost worked in; phosphate only if a test calls for it |
| Vegetative growth      | Building leaves and stems | Nitrogen, side-dressed every 3–4 weeks                   |
| Flowering and fruiting | Setting and filling fruit | Ease back nitrogen, lean on potassium                    |
| Final weeks            | Ripening                  | Nothing                                                  |

Phosphate moves very slowly through soil, so unlike nitrogen it has to be placed where the roots
will be rather than scattered on the surface. That is the one nutrient worth mixing in at
planting.

Continued heavy nitrogen at the fruiting stage gives a magnificent plant with very little on it.
It is the single most common feeding mistake in a vegetable garden.

## By crop group

| Crop group                                            | Feeding                   | Notes                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Fruiting — tomato, pepper, eggplant, cucumber, squash | Every 3–4 weeks           | Moderate N early, then lower N and higher K once fruit sets  |
| Leafy — lettuce, spinach, chard, kale                 | Light and frequent        | Nitrogen throughout; leaf is the crop                        |
| Root — carrot, beet, radish                           | Sparingly                 | No fresh manure, no heavy N — both cause forked, hairy roots |
| Legumes — beans, peas                                 | None                      | They fix their own nitrogen; feeding gives foliage, not pods |
| Brassicas — broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower            | Steady through the season | Hungry, and stress gives small loose heads                   |
| Sweet corn                                            | Twice                     | Side-dress at knee high and again at tasselling              |
| Onion and garlic                                      | Early only                | Stop once bulbs swell, or they store badly                   |

Our [tomato guide](/crops/tomato/) and [carrot guide](/crops/carrot/) carry the same logic
alongside the spacing and timing for each crop. Note the root crop row in particular — the
instinct to feed a disappointing carrot bed is exactly wrong.

## How much, and how to apply it

Work from a rate, not a handful. A common figure for a mixed vegetable plot is about **1 lb of
actual nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft across the season**, split into two or three applications.

Converting that into pounds of whatever product you bought is one line of arithmetic — and the
[fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) will do it and also show what phosphate
and potash the same dose delivers, which is the figure worth seeing before you buy a balanced
bag out of habit. If the three numbers on that bag are not self-explanatory,
[what the three numbers on fertilizer mean](/blog/what-the-three-numbers-on-fertilizer-mean/)
covers the label.

To side-dress: draw a shallow furrow 3 to 6 inches out from the stem, scatter the fertilizer
along it, cover it with soil and water it in. Keep granules off stems and leaves — a granule
resting against a stem will scorch it.

Water is not optional. Dry fertilizer on dry soil does nothing and can draw moisture out of
roots. And weigh it rather than measuring by volume: densities differ enough between products
that a cup of one is not a cup of another.

## When to stop

Stop feeding nitrogen a few weeks before your final harvest. Late nitrogen delays maturity,
softens tissue and makes crops store badly — for onions and garlic in particular it produces
bulbs that rot in storage.

For perennials and overwintering crops, stop by late summer so growth hardens off before cold
weather arrives.

## Compost changes the arithmetic

A bed that receives an inch of compost every year needs much less bought fertilizer than one that
does not. Compost supplies a little of everything, improves structure and water holding, and
feeds the microbial life that makes soil nutrients available in the first place.

It will not usually supply enough nitrogen on its own for a heavy feeder through a long season.
But it moves the baseline considerably, and it is the one amendment that improves a soil rather
than just feeding a crop. [Michigan State University Extension](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/fertilizing_your_vegetable_garden)
makes the same point about soil testing first.

A pothos is the houseplant that survives almost anything and still looks like it is thriving, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for pothos. It grows in a dim office, in a bright kitchen, in a hanging basket above a sink, and in a jar of water on a desk — NC State Extension calls it very easy to grow, Penn State Extension calls it hardy and very easy to grow, and the University of Wisconsin Extension says it is very easy to grow and makes an excellent choice for beginners — and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: a bright window, a mix that drains, and a watering only when the soil is dry. Then the same plant sits for a year making small, plain green leaves in the hands of an owner who keeps it in a dark corner, because how to care for pothos is not the same as keeping a plant alive. A pothos is a climbing vine from the warm tropics that starts on the forest floor and climbs into the canopy, and it wants bright, filtered light, a pot it can drain from, and something to climb or trail from. Give it those, and it grows into a vine eight to ten feet long, with glossy heart-shaped leaves marbled in gold or white.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A pothos that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go limp and droop, and then they lift again within hours of watering. A pothos that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its stems grow long and bare between leaves, and the new leaves come out small and plain green, with the gold or white markings gone. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun yellows, and a plant that is fed too much burns black at the leaf edges. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, yellowing leaves, fading variegation, stretch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for pothos, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are NC State Extension, Penn State Extension, the University of Wisconsin Extension, Clemson Cooperative Extension, and UF/IFAS Extension, with the ASPCA covering the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the yellow-leaves section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most. If you bought it for the golden leaves and got plain green ones instead, read the light section first — because the variegation answers to light, not to fertilizer.

## How to care for pothos: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and keep it out of direct sun.** A bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or west-facing window, with no sun falling directly on the leaves, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for pothos, because the plant is a forest vine that grows under a canopy — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and loses its variegation, while a plant moved into direct sun in one day yellows.

**Let the soil dry out between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the top two inches feel dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for pothos.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room by day and night, between 60 and 80°F if you can manage it, and inside long before the first cold snap. Cold is the third rule of how to care for pothos — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single cold night can mark the leaves with brown patches that never heal.

### The last two rules in how to care for pothos

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed at half strength every other month from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding burns the roots with salts, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that blacken the leaf edges.

**Keep it in an airy mix, in a pot with holes, and give it something to climb or trail from.** An airy mix of potting soil, bark, and perlite in a pot with drainage holes, repotted when the roots fill the pot — and a moss pole or a hanging basket, because a pothos without support trails and goes bare at the base, and a pothos with a pole climbs and makes bigger leaves. Free drainage and a support are the fifth rule of how to care for pothos, and they protect the roots and the stems for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for pothos does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays full and colorful: glossy, heart-shaped leaves, bright green or marbled with yellow or cream, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it climbs or trails the way it wants to: a slender, twining stem with brown aerial roots that grip a pole, per NC State Extension, and leaves that get bigger with every new one once the plant is attached — and that climbing habit is the reason the plant is worth keeping for years. Third, it grows fast: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and given a pole grows eight to ten feet long indoors, per Penn State Extension, and makes new leaves all summer. The five rules in how to care for pothos are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the forest canopy, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a trailing vine in a pot

The first thing to get straight in how to care for pothos is what the plant actually is, because the name tells you only half the story. It is a pothos — *Epipremnum aureum*, also sold as golden pothos, devil's ivy, and hunter's robe, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — a low-maintenance, herbaceous perennial, broadleaf evergreen houseplant in the arum family, per NC State Extension. The name *aureum* is Latin for golden, per NC State Extension, and it refers to the yellow variegation on the leaves. It is not a philodendron, though both are in the arum family, per Penn State Extension — and the difference is visible if you know where to look: the leaf stalks of a pothos are grooved, especially where they seem to grasp the stem, while a philodendron's are smooth, and a philodendron's new leaves emerge inside a protective sheath that dries and drops off, while a pothos leaf emerges cleanly along the stem, with no sheath at all. That grooved stalk is the quickest way to tell the two apart in the shop, and it matters, because the two plants want slightly different care.

### A plant from the warm tropics — and the sources disagree on where

The sources disagree on the plant's native range, and the disagreement is worth showing, because it is honest. NC State Extension says pothos is native to the Society Islands. Penn State Extension says it is a tropical climbing vine native to the Solomon Islands. UF/IFAS Extension says the same: a native of the Solomon Islands. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos is native to China, India, and some parts of Southeast Asia. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives the range more broadly: the genus occurs from Southeast Asia to the western Pacific. That background explains almost everything about how to care for pothos. It is a plant from a warm, wet place where the rain comes often and the soil drains instantly, and it wants bright, filtered light, warm air, and a soil that dries out between rains. It is not a desert succulent and it is not a shade plant — it is a climbing vine that starts on the forest floor and grows up into the canopy, which is why it wants light from above and something to climb. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same in its own words: in its natural habitat the plant receives dappled sunlight and high humidity, and it wraps around tree trunks — which is the habit to copy at home, with a moss pole or a wooden rod.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a vine in miniature: a slender, twining stem carrying glossy, heart-shaped leaves on grooved stalks. The University of Wisconsin Extension describes the leaves as glossy, heart-shaped, and waxy, bright green or irregularly splotched or marbled with yellow or cream, and says the juvenile leaves are entire, with smooth margins, and up to several inches long, while mature plants feature oval- to heart-shaped leaves up to 3 feet long with holes along the midrib. Penn State Extension says the leaves are heart-shaped, smooth, and shiny, fold inward along the mid-rib, and are 3 to 6 inches long and less wide. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that as a houseplant the leaves are typically a few inches long and stay whole, slightly heart-shaped and shiny, and that the vines will fill a pot to create a bushy look. UF/IFAS Extension says the plants used indoors have heart-shaped leaves that seldom exceed six inches in length. The leaves are the plant's signature, and a plant with small, plain green leaves is either a solid-green cultivar or an under-lit variegated one — and reading that difference is the first skill in how to care for pothos.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for pothos, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the length, because the plant grows fast and the vine decides how much room it needs. Penn State Extension says the stems can reach 8 to 10 feet or longer as a vine. NC State Extension says the trailing and climbing vines can grow as long as 40 feet, though it grows only 6 to 8 feet as a horizontal groundcover. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the stem grows up to 65 feet long in the wild. UF/IFAS Extension says that in tropical settings pothos can grow to be large vines, climbing trees 50 feet tall. Buy the length for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a bright shelf, a standard plant for a hanging basket, and a long trailing plant only if you have somewhere for the vines to go — because a plant that reaches 10 feet needs room, and a plant without a pole or a basket trails across the floor instead.

### The age that matters: a plant that outgrows its pot

The second is the age, because the big leaves come only to a mature plant. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaves of pothos grow much bigger as they mature, and that as a juvenile it forms modest terrestrial colonies, but the leaves grow larger as they get higher in the canopy. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that in its natural habitat pothos leaves can get up to 3 feet long with holes along the middle of the leaves, and that growing the plant up a moss pole or wooden rod will allow it to produce large leaves and develop ornamental holes. NC State Extension says the same: if given a support on which to climb and adequate light, the plant will begin to produce large, mature leaves. A plant you buy this year will not make three-foot leaves this year — and a plant kept trailing from a basket may never make them at all — and that is normal, not a failure. The big leaves are a bonus, not the point, and a plant that never makes them is still a healthy plant. What the age buys you is the vine: a pothos kept for a few years trails eight to ten feet, per Penn State Extension, and a pothos kept for ten years fills a wall. That is why the plant is worth buying young and keeping: you are not buying a decoration, you are starting a vine, and that long view is the point of how to care for pothos.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, glossy, and firm, bright green with clear yellow or white markings on a variegated plant, with no yellow patches, no brown edges, and no bare stems where leaves have already dropped. Yellow leaves on a plant in the shop mean the plant has already been over-watered, and those leaves will not turn green again. Brown, blackened edges mean the plant has been over-watered or over-fed, per NC State Extension — and a plant that is already black at the edges in the shop has been neglected, and a plant with clean green edges is a better buy. The soil should be damp, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes and enough weight that the plant cannot tip over. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a light plastic pot with a heavy trailing vine will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. Buy the glossy, firm, right-sized plant in damp soil in a heavy pot, and the rest of how to care for pothos is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for pothos, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. NC State Extension says the plant prefers bright, indirect light and survives for long periods in low light. Penn State Extension says to place pothos in an area with moderate to bright light and average room temperatures between 60° and 80°F, and to avoid direct sun. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos does best in filtered light, with high humidity, and temperatures between 70 and 90°F, but does not tolerate much direct sunlight. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos prefer bright, indirect light but will tolerate lower lighting conditions. UF/IFAS Extension gives the numbers behind the words: pothos should be grown with levels of 63 to 80% shade, and will tolerate light levels as low as 50 foot-candles, but new leaves will be small and variegated cultivars will lose color — while light levels of 150 foot-candles or more will maintain color and leaf size. The planning figure this guide uses is a bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or west-facing window, with no direct sun on the leaves — and that is the light that keeps the plant compact, upright, and making colorful leaves.

### The yellowing warning

The warning that matters most is the sudden one. Penn State Extension says direct sun will lead to the yellowing of leaves. UF/IFAS Extension says burned, necrotic patches confined to the variegated areas are caused by high light or heat, and that leaves cannot recover. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf scorch and tip dieback are often caused by low humidity or intense light. The burn shows as yellow or bleached patches, worst on the pale variegated parts of the leaves, and a scorched leaf never recovers its color — the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. If your plant came from a dim shop shelf, give it bright indirect light for two weeks, then move it into a spot that gets an hour or two of gentle morning sun, and then into its permanent bright position over the next two to three weeks. That gradual move is the light rule in how to care for pothos, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters second is the delayed one. A plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never grows properly, and the owner concludes that pothos simply grow slowly, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. Penn State Extension says the plant can survive in low light conditions for quite some time, but will eventually lose its desirable leaf qualities — the variegation or brightness of foliage, depending on the cultivar. The University of Wisconsin Extension says low light can result in loss of variegation. Clemson Cooperative Extension says lower light may cause variegated varieties to lose some of their coloring. UF/IFAS Extension says new leaves lack variegation and are smaller than older leaves when light levels are too low. If your plant stretches toward the window, or its new leaves come out small and plain green, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for pothos, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Grow lights when there is no bright window

If you do not have a bright window, a grow light is a real option, not a compromise. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant can be grown under fluorescent light — but the light has to be bright enough, because UF/IFAS Extension says that at light levels as low as 50 foot-candles the plant keeps living, but the new leaves shrink and the variegated kinds lose their color, and that 150 foot-candles or more is what maintains color and leaf size. A full-spectrum light hung a foot or two above the plant for twelve hours a day gives the plant the bright light it would get from an east window, and the new leaves will come out larger and more colorful within a few months. Do not leave the light on all night: the plant needs darkness, and the leaves, when they come, answer to a day and a night. That twelve-hour rule is the grow-light rule in how to care for pothos.

### Water

Watering is where most pothos are lost, and the loss usually comes from one mistake: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain. The watering rule from Penn State Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for pothos: water when the soil is dry, because too much water will lead to root rot, one of only a few problems with this plant — and pothos is better kept too dry than too wet. Two halves, both required: let the soil dry, and then water thoroughly. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. And then remove the excess, so the pot never sits in water.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for pothos

The sources give the dry test in different depths, and the spread is worth showing. NC State Extension says to let the well-drained potting medium dry out between watering. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to allow plants to dry out slightly and be watered only when the soil surface is dry. Penn State Extension says to water when the soil is dry. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the soil should be allowed to dry between each watering, and to make sure to water thoroughly so that water will reach all the roots — and in its beginners' guide it puts the test in inches: water thoroughly each time and only when the top 1.5 to 2 inches of soil have dried out. UF/IFAS Extension says that in most interior locations pothos does better if grown a little on the dry side, and that the media should be kept moist but never soggy. The planning figure this guide uses is the finger test at two inches deep: push a finger two inches into the mix, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — roughly once a week in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to every two to three weeks in a cool room in winter — always letting the excess drain away and never letting the pot sit in water. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the watering that keeps the roots healthy.

### How often to water

There is no fixed schedule, and the plant's own calendar is the season. In summer, when the plant is growing fast and the light is strong, the mix dries in about a week, and the plant wants a thorough watering. In winter, when the growth slows and the days shorten, the mix stays damp for two to three weeks, and the plant wants far less. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to decrease watering frequency in the fall and winter. NC State Extension says to fertilize every other month except when the plant is dormant in the winter — and the same winter slowdown applies to water. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to fertilize every other month, except during the winter when the plant is not actively growing. The fix is the finger test, not a new fertilizer, and not a calendar on the fridge — and that is the watering rule in how to care for pothos.

### The drooping leaves

The warning that matters most is the forgiving one. Penn State Extension says too little water may lead to leaves temporarily drooping — and the word temporarily matters, because a thirsty pothos lifts again within hours of watering. A drooping pothos is a plant asking for water, not a plant that is dying, and the fix is a thorough watering at the sink, not a little sprinkle every day. But drooping is also the sign that is easiest to misread: a plant with yellow, soft leaves and wet soil is not thirsty, it is rotting, and more water makes it worse. The difference is the soil — dry soil plus drooping leaves means water it, and wet soil plus yellow leaves means stop watering. That soil test is the watering warning in how to care for pothos.

### Temperature

Temperature is the third daily decision, and the sources agree on it more closely than on anything else. Penn State Extension says to place pothos in an area with average room temperatures between 60° and 80°F. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos prefer a warm room, between 60°F to 70°F at night and 70°F to 85°F during the day. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos does best in temperatures between 70 and 90°F. UF/IFAS Extension says pothos should be grown with temperatures between 70 to 90°F, that plant quality and growth rates begin to decline when day temperatures routinely exceed 95°F or night temperatures drop below 65°F, and that temperatures of 70°F to 80°F are recommended once plants are placed indoors. The planning figure this guide uses is 60 to 80°F, with nights no colder than 60°F if you can help it, and inside long before the first cold snap. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the temperature that keeps the plant growing.

### What to avoid: cold and drafts

The warning that matters most is the cold one. The University of Wisconsin Extension says plants need to be kept out of drafts. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to keep pothos away from air conditioning vents in the summer and from windowsills and heating vents in the winter, as hot and cold air can cause the leaves to dry out and damage plant cells. UF/IFAS Extension gives the hard numbers: scattered brown patches in the center of the leaf are caused by exposure to 33°F to 40°F for several hours, or by abrupt temperature changes. The University of Wisconsin Extension says this species is only hardy in zones 10 to 12, so it is always grown as a houseplant or seasonal annual in colder climates. A plant left by a drafty window in winter, or a plant moved outside for the summer and forgotten in the first cold night, will show brown patches in the middle of the leaves — and the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. Keep the plant away from cold windows in winter, away from air-conditioning vents in summer, and inside before the first frost. That cold rule is the temperature rule in how to care for pothos.

### Humidity

Humidity is the fourth daily decision, and the sources agree on the direction even where they differ on the number. NC State Extension says to use a humidifier or place the plant container on a tray of wet pebbles to increase humidity. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos does best with high humidity. Clemson Cooperative Extension gives the numbers: pothos prefers 50% to 70% humidity, against 30% to 60% in an average home, and will tolerate the lower levels, but humidity can be supplemented with a humidifier. UF/IFAS Extension says pothos should be grown with a relative humidity of 60 to 100% in production, and that the plant does better indoors when grown a little on the dry side. The planning figure this guide uses is 50% to 70% if you can manage it, with a humidifier or a pebble tray in a dry house, and average household air if you cannot — because the plant tolerates average air, and the brown tips are a cosmetic warning, not a death sentence. That humidity figure is the humidity rule in how to care for pothos.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once, at potting time, and it decides whether the watering rule can work at all. A mix that holds water like ordinary garden soil keeps the roots wet for weeks, and no watering schedule can fix it. A mix that drains fast lets the roots breathe, and the watering rule works. That is why the mix matters more than the watering can in how to care for pothos.

### An airy mix that drains

The sources agree on the mix, and the agreement is unusually close. Clemson Cooperative Extension says plants should be potted in an airy, well-draining soil mix. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to use a growing medium that is well aerated. Penn State Extension says to use a good quality soilless potting mix that drains well. UF/IFAS Extension says the media should have good moisture-holding capacity and aeration, with a pH of 6.5 to 7.0. The planning figure this guide uses is an airy aroid mix: one part good potting soil, one part orchid bark, and one part perlite, in a pot with drainage holes — a recipe that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy, and that lets air reach the roots. Our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself, and the comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite, not vermiculite, is the right loosener here — perlite drains, vermiculite holds water, and a pothos wants drainage. The [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

A pot without drainage holes is a pot that kills the plant, however good the mix is. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot is a common houseplant issue caused by overwatering or poorly draining soil, and that using a well-draining soil mix and proper watering practices will help prevent it — and a pot without holes makes both impossible. Choose a pot with drainage holes, only a little bigger than the root ball, and heavy enough that the plant cannot tip over — because a pothos grows long and trailing, and a light plastic pot will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. A clay pot is a good choice, because it breathes and helps the mix dry, and its weight keeps the plant upright. That pot rule is the soil rule in how to care for pothos.

### Repotting: when the roots fill the pot

Repotting is yearly work for a fast vine, and the plant tells you when it is time. Penn State Extension says pothos appreciates being potted up annually in the spring into a slightly larger container, and that if the plant is large and you wish to keep it in the same container, it will still benefit from repotting — remove the plant, trim some roots, cut up to one-third of the length of the vining stems, provide some new soil, and do not overwater. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to repot pothos plants when roots are visible through the drainage holes, or when the plant becomes overcrowded and root-bound, and that most plants can be repotted once every year or two, depending on their growth rate. UF/IFAS Extension adds a warning for a new plant: once plants are placed indoors, it is advisable not to re-pot or fertilize for about four weeks, because plants do not need additional stresses. The planning figure this guide uses is to repot every year or two in spring, into a pot one size up, and to wait at least four weeks after bringing a new plant home before repotting it — because a pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots, and a pot that is too small starves them. Go one size up, and water sparingly until it settles. That one-size-up rule is the repotting rule in how to care for pothos.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the least important of the five rules, and the easiest to get wrong. The sources give different schedules, and the spread is worth showing. NC State Extension says to fertilize every other month except when the plant is dormant in the winter. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to fertilize every other month, except during the winter when the plant is not actively growing. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to correct yellow leaves from low fertility by fertilizing with a houseplant fertilizer every other month during the spring and summer. Penn State Extension gives the most generous schedule: for best growth, fertilize pothos with a balanced houseplant food monthly through the winter and every two weeks throughout the rest of the year. The planning figure this guide uses is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half strength every other month from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. That range satisfies the three sources that say every other month, and it is the feeding that keeps the plant growing without burning the roots.

### The planning figure: every other month, or none at all

The reason for the caution is the salt. The University of Wisconsin Extension says blackening of the leaf margins or tips can be caused by overwatering, inadequate watering, or excess fertilizer, because of buildup of salts in the soil. UF/IFAS Extension says plants should not be fertilized if soluble salts are 3.0 dS/m or more, and that if soluble salt levels are higher than 5.0 dS/m, percolation of media with water may help reduce potential leaf necrosis or damage to the root system. A plant that is fed every two weeks at full strength, through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that blacken the leaf edges and the root tips — and the damage looks like drought, so the owner waters more, and the plant gets worse. The fix is to feed lightly, to flush the pot with plain water every few months, and to stop feeding entirely in autumn and winter. That every-other-month rule is the feeding rule in how to care for pothos.

### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding grows soft, leggy stems, and the leaf edges turn brown and black — the same symptom as low humidity, so the owner buys a humidifier, and the plant gets worse. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaf edges will turn brown if humidity is too low, and that blackening of the leaf margins or tips can also be caused by excess fertilizer, because of buildup of salts in the soil — and both symptoms look the same, and the soil is the only way to tell them apart. If the soil is salty and crusty, the cause is feeding, and the fix is to flush the pot and stop feeding. If the soil is clean and the air is dry, the cause is humidity, and the fix is a humidifier. That distinction is the feeding warning in how to care for pothos.

## Support: give it something to climb or trail from

Support is the rule that makes a pothos look like a pothos, and it is the rule most owners skip. A pothos without support does not stay small — it trails across the floor, its stems grow long and bare, and its leaves stay small and plain. A pothos with support climbs, and its leaves get bigger with every new one. That is why the pole matters as much as the pot in how to care for pothos.

### The moss pole rule in how to care for pothos

The sources agree on the support, and the agreement is unusually close. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos is a vining plant that typically wraps around tree trunks in nature, and that this can be emulated at home with a moss pole or a wooden rod — and that growing the plant this way will allow it to showcase its climbing habit, produce large leaves, and develop ornamental holes in the leaves. Penn State Extension says a unique way to grow pothos is on a totem: create a moss-covered totem, and with gentle guidance the pothos vine will attach to the totem using its adhesive aerial roots, which develop at each node of the stem. NC State Extension says the vines climb by brown aerial roots, and that if given a support on which to climb and adequate light, the plant will begin to produce large, mature leaves. The planning figure this guide uses is a moss pole sunk into the pot at repotting time, tall enough to last two years, with the stem tied loosely to it until the aerial roots grip — or a hanging basket, if you would rather let the vines trail than climb.

### Aerial roots: what they are and what to do with them

The aerial roots are the plant's climbing gear, and they are supposed to be there. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant produces adhesive aerial roots as it climbs into the canopy, with clasping roots arising from nodes and internodes, and prolific feeding roots. Penn State Extension says the plant uses the aerial roots that form at the base of its leaves to attach to trees. NC State Extension says the vines climb by brown aerial roots. Do not cut them all off: tuck the long ones into the pot or onto the pole, and water the moss pole as well as the pot, so the aerial roots can take up moisture — because a plant with aerial roots is a plant that is trying to climb, and giving it a pole is the support rule in how to care for pothos. A plant with aerial roots is a healthy plant, and the roots are the reason the cuttings root so fast in how to care for pothos.

## Pruning a vine that never stops

Pruning is the rule that keeps a fast-growing vine in its room. A pothos that is never pruned grows across the floor, up the wall, and into the next plant — and its stems get long and bare, with leaves only at the ends. A pothos that is pruned in spring stays full and bushy, and the cuttings become new plants. That is why the shears matter as much as the pole in how to care for pothos.

### Prune in spring, back to a node

NC State Extension says that over time leaves will yellow and fall off, concentrating most of the leaves at the end of the stems, and to prune stems back to maintain a bushy habit. The University of Wisconsin Extension says older leaves will turn yellow and drop off naturally, eventually ending up with most of the leaves at the end of the vine, and to selectively prune to keep vines a reasonable length and to promote new growth — and that plants can be cut back to 2 inches from the soil line if necessary. Penn State Extension says to trim as needed by shortening a vining stem with sharp scissors just above a node at any point along its length, or by removing the entire length, leaving a 2-inch stub. The planning figure this guide uses is to prune in spring, cutting the stem just above a node with clean, sharp shears, and to remove yellow or damaged leaves at any time of year. Cut back to a node, not to a bare stem — because a new shoot grows from the node, and a bare stem does not. That spring rule is the pruning rule in how to care for pothos.

### What the cuttings become

Every stem you cut off in spring is a new plant, and the propagation section covers how to root it. Do not throw the cuttings away: a stem with a node will root in water in three to four weeks, per the University of Wisconsin Extension, and a cutting from a plant you love is the best gift you can give a friend. That is the whole point of pruning in how to care for pothos — the plant you cut back becomes three plants, and the room stays full.

## Getting it to make bigger leaves

The big leaves are the reason some people buy a pothos, and they are the most misunderstood part of the plant. The three-foot leaves with holes are not a sign of health, and they are not a sign of damage — they are a sign of maturity, and of light, and of climbing. A plant that makes small leaves is not sick. It is young, or it is trailing, or it is under-lit, and the fix is time plus a brighter spot plus a pole. That is the whole of big leaves in how to care for pothos.

### Why the leaves stay small

The University of Wisconsin Extension says the juvenile leaves are entire, with smooth margins, and up to several inches long, while mature plants feature oval- to heart-shaped leaves up to 3 feet long with holes along the midrib — and that as a juvenile the plant forms modest terrestrial colonies, but the leaves grow larger as they get higher in the canopy. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that as a houseplant the leaves are typically a few inches long and stay whole, and that in its natural habitat pothos leaves can get up to 3 feet long with holes along the middle of the leaves. UF/IFAS Extension says that in tropical settings pothos can grow to be large vines, climbing trees 50 feet tall and producing lobed, fenestrate leaves up to three feet in length, while the plants used for interiorscapes have heart-shaped leaves that seldom exceed six inches in length. The leaves get bigger as they climb, not as they age on the floor — and a leaf that unfurls small will not grow bigger later. That is why the new leaves are the ones to watch: if the newest leaf is bigger than the last one, the plant is climbing well, and if the newest leaf is smaller, the plant is asking for more light or a pole.

### The light and pole rule for big leaves

The rule for big leaves is the same as the rule for the whole plant, and the sources state it plainly. NC State Extension says that if given a support on which to climb and adequate light, the plant will begin to produce large, mature leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says growing the plant up a moss pole or wooden rod will allow it to produce large leaves and develop ornamental holes in the leaves. UF/IFAS Extension says new leaves will be small and variegated cultivars will lose color at light levels as low as 50 foot-candles, and that light levels of 150 foot-candles or more will maintain color and leaf size. If your plant is mature and healthy and still makes small leaves, give it a pole to climb and move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for pothos, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### What about the variegated kinds?

The variegated kinds need more light than the solid green plant, and their leaves tend to be smaller and slower, and that is normal. NC State Extension lists nine cultivars of *Epipremnum aureum*, including Golden, Marble Queen, Snow Queen, N'Joy, Pearls and Jade, Neon, Jade, Lime, and Tricolor. The University of Wisconsin Extension says 'Jade' is a solid dark green type which does well in low light areas, and that 'Marble Queen' has stems and moss green leaves streaked with white, 'Neon' has all yellow leaves, and 'Lime' has bright yellow-green leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says 'Marble Queen' requires more light than other varieties, 'Snow Queen' needs lots of bright, indirect light to ensure adequate growth, 'Pearls and Jade' requires lots of light to maintain its intense coloring, and 'Neon' tolerates lower light conditions and grows quickly. Penn State Extension lists the same names: 'Golden' with green leaves and bright yellow variegation, 'Jade' with solid dark green leaves, 'Neon' with bright chartreuse leaves, 'Marble Queen' with green leaves and white variegation, and 'Lime' with yellow-green leaves. The white and cream patches have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesize, and the plant needs more light to make the same amount of food. Keep a variegated plant in the brightest indirect light you have, and expect it to grow more slowly than the green plant — and do not be surprised if a variegated leaf scorches in direct sun, because the pale patches burn first. That variegated rule is the light rule in how to care for pothos, applied to the plants that need it most.

### The pothos that are not pothos

Two lookalikes are worth knowing, because they are sold beside pothos and they want different care. The first is the dragon-tail pothos, *Epipremnum pinnatum*, including the popular 'Cebu Blue' with slender, silvery-blue leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says these pothos have large leaves with more fenestrations than *E. aureum*, that care requirements are similar, but that a moss pole or wooden rod may be needed to allow the plant to grow large enough to develop its signature fenestrated leaves. NC State Extension says the two were once treated as one species, and that botanists determined they have distinctively different vegetative features and should be recognized as separate species. The second is the satin pothos, *Scindapsus pictus*, which is not a true pothos at all. Clemson Cooperative Extension says it has broader leaves than most pothos, grows much more slowly, and is more challenging to grow, because it is more prone to pests and disease and less tolerant of overwatering than pothos. If your "pothos" grows slowly and sulks when you water it, check the label — it may be a satin pothos, and the fix is a lighter hand with the watering can in how to care for pothos.

## Making more plants for free

Propagation is the easiest part of how to care for pothos, and the most generous — because a plant that grows this fast makes more plants than any one house needs. A stem cutting with a node will root in a few weeks, and a cutting from a plant you love is the best gift you can give a friend. That is why propagation is worth doing, and worth doing right.

### Stem cuttings: the fast method

The sources agree on the method, and the agreement is unusually close. Clemson Cooperative Extension says stem cuttings can be easily rooted when placed in soil or water, but they should have at least one node to root properly. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos is easily propagated from cuttings or by air-layering, that the vines root in water or vermiculite within 3 to 4 weeks, and that buds can start to grow in 1 to 2 weeks under warm temperatures. UF/IFAS Extension says the most common method is to make one to one-and-a-half-inch stem cuttings with a node and an attached leaf, that buds break in 2 to 3 weeks, and that rooting occurs in 3 to 4 weeks. Penn State Extension says propagation through stem cuttings is more successful during the growing season from spring through summer, and that pothos has aerial roots at the node, so propagation through layering can also be successful. NC State Extension says vines easily root in water. The planning figure this guide uses is to cut the stem just below a node, keeping the leaf attached, and to root the cutting in water or damp mix before potting it up — and to plant several rooted cuttings together in a single pot, per the University of Wisconsin Extension, for a fuller plant. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the cutting that makes a new plant.

### The node rule in how to care for pothos

The node is the whole of propagation, and the rule is simple: no node, no plant. A leaf on its own, without a piece of stem and a node, will stay green in water for months and never make a new vine — because the new shoot grows from the node, and a leaf has none. A stem with a node and an aerial root will root in a few weeks, and the aerial root is the head start: it is already a root, and it only needs damp mix to grow. Cut below the node, keep the aerial root, and pot the cutting in airy mix when the roots are a few inches long. That node rule is the propagation rule in how to care for pothos.

### Water or soil?

Both work, and the choice is about patience, not success. Rooting in water lets you watch the roots grow, and a pothos cutting roots in water in three to four weeks, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — but a cutting rooted in water grows water roots, and it sulks for a week or two when it moves into soil. Rooting in damp mix skips that sulk, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says stem cuttings root easily in soil or water. Either way, keep the cutting warm and bright, out of direct sun, and do not let the mix dry out while it is rooting. Penn State Extension says cuttings root best in the growing season, from spring through summer — so take your cuttings in spring, when the plant is growing fastest, and they will root in three to four weeks in how to care for pothos.

### What about seeds?

Seeds are possible, and nobody should bother. Penn State Extension says no hybrids exist for pothos because it rarely flowers, even in its native habitat. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos does not flower in cultivation, since only the juvenile phase is grown as a houseplant and flowering occurs only in the mature phase. UF/IFAS Extension says that even in its native habitat pothos seldom flowers, and that correct taxonomic classification was only possible when enough flowers were collected in Florida and Puerto Rico for detailed examination. Cuttings are faster, surer, and true to the parent plant, and that is why every source lists them first in how to care for pothos.

You do not feed it in November, because the plant is barely growing, and NC State Extension says the plant is dormant in the winter. You do not repot it for at least four weeks, because UF/IFAS Extension says a new plant should not be re-potted or fertilized for about four weeks after it is placed indoors. The pruning comes in spring 2027 as well, back to a node, per NC State Extension, when the new growth starts — and the cuttings you take become three new plants, each in a small pot of airy mix, given to friends. After a few years of that routine, the plant is eight to ten feet long, per Penn State Extension, with leaves three to six inches across, and it has made a dozen new plants you gave away. That is the whole story of how to care for pothos: a finger in the soil, a bright window, a warm room, an airy mix, a little food in summer, a pole to climb, and patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most pothos problems have one of four causes — water, light, cold, or feeding — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a yellow leaf never turns green again. Troubleshooting in how to care for pothos is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves, with wet soil, mean overwatering — and it is the most serious problem this plant has. Penn State Extension says too much water will lead to root rot, one of only a few problems with this plant. Clemson Cooperative Extension says yellow leaves are often caused by overwatering or low fertility. UF/IFAS Extension says older leaves turn yellow when the potting media is kept either too dry or too wet. The University of Wisconsin Extension says root rots, with symptoms of brown or nonexistent roots, are most often due to overwatering. The fix is to stop watering immediately, take the plant out of its pot, cut away the soft, brown, rotted roots with a clean knife, and repot it in fresh airy mix — and then to water only when the top two inches feel dry. A yellow leaf will not turn green again, and the fix is the finger test, not a new fertilizer. Our guide to [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers the gnats that follow wet soil — and dry soil is the best prevention for those too.

### Brown, blackened leaf edges and tips

Brown or blackened edges mean the air is too dry, the soil is too salty, or the plant was over-watered — and the soil is the only way to tell them apart. NC State Extension says root rot and blackening of the leaf margins can occur with overwatering. The University of Wisconsin Extension says blackening of the leaf margins or tips can be caused by overwatering, inadequate watering, or excess fertilizer, because of buildup of salts in the soil. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf scorch and tip dieback are often caused by low humidity or intense light. If the soil is salty and crusty, the cause is feeding, and the fix is to flush the pot with plain water and stop feeding. If the soil is clean and the air is dry, the cause is humidity, and the fix is a humidifier or a pebble tray. NC State Extension says using a humidifier or setting the plant container on a tray of wet pebbles helps increase the humidity in dry interior settings. That distinction is the brown-edges rule in how to care for pothos.

### Losing variegation

Small, plain green leaves mean the light is too low — and the new leaves are the ones to watch. NC State Extension says low light can cause loss of variegation. The University of Wisconsin Extension says low light can result in loss of variegation. Clemson Cooperative Extension says lower light may cause variegated varieties to lose some of their coloring, and that the white-heavy kinds need more light to keep their coloring. Penn State Extension says the plant can survive in low light for quite some time, but will eventually lose its desirable leaf qualities. UF/IFAS Extension says new leaves lack variegation and are smaller than older leaves when light levels are too low. A plant with plain green leaves is usually under-lit, and the fix is a brighter spot, not fertilizer. Move the plant closer to the window, and wait for the next leaf — because a leaf that has already gone green will not regain its markings, and the next leaf is the one that matters. That patience is the variegation rule in how to care for pothos.

### Leggy, stretched growth

Long, bare stems with leaves only at the ends mean too little light — and the plant is reaching for a window it cannot find. NC State Extension says that over time leaves will yellow and fall off, concentrating most of the leaves at the end of the stems, and to prune stems back to maintain a bushy habit. The University of Wisconsin Extension says older leaves will turn yellow and drop off naturally, eventually ending up with most of the leaves at the end of the vine, and to selectively prune to keep vines a reasonable length. The fix is to move the plant closer to the window first, before you change anything else, and then to prune the bare stems back to a node in spring, so a new shoot grows from the node. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for pothos, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Sunburn and yellowing in strong sun

Pale, yellow patches on the sunny side of the leaves mean the plant was moved into strong sun too fast — and a yellowed leaf never recovers its color. Penn State Extension says direct sun will lead to the yellowing of leaves. UF/IFAS Extension says burned, necrotic patches confined to the variegated areas are caused by high light or heat, and that leaves cannot recover. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos does not tolerate much direct sunlight. The fix is prevention, not treatment: move the plant into brighter light gradually, over two to three weeks, and keep it out of harsh afternoon sun — and that is the sunburn rule in how to care for pothos. A scorched leaf will not heal, but the next leaf will be fine.

### Drooping leaves

Limp, drooping leaves mean the plant is thirsty — and the fix is water, not fertilizer. Penn State Extension says too little water may lead to leaves temporarily drooping, and that pothos is better kept too dry than too wet. The droop is the plant's clearest signal, and it lifts within hours of a thorough watering. But drooping with wet soil means the opposite: the roots are rotting, and more water makes it worse. Check the soil before you water — dry soil plus drooping leaves means water it, and wet soil plus yellow leaves means stop watering. That soil test is the drooping rule in how to care for pothos.

### Soft, mushy stems

Soft, mushy stems mean root rot, and it is the most serious problem this plant has. The University of Wisconsin Extension says root rots, with symptoms of brown or nonexistent roots, are most often due to overwatering. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot is a common houseplant issue caused by overwatering or poorly draining soil. Penn State Extension says too much water will lead to root rot, one of only a few problems with this plant. The fix is to cut away the rotted stem and roots with a clean knife, repot the healthy part in fresh airy mix, and water only when the top two inches feel dry. A stem that is mushy all the way through cannot be saved, but a cutting taken above the rot can be — and that is why the node rule matters in how to care for pothos.

### Cold brown patches

Scattered brown patches in the middle of the leaves mean cold damage — and the plant was left somewhere too cold, or moved through a cold draft. UF/IFAS Extension says scattered brown patches in the center of the leaf are caused by exposure to 33°F to 40°F for several hours, or by abrupt temperature changes. The University of Wisconsin Extension says low temperatures or an abrupt change from very high temperatures to moderate temperatures can cause scattered brown patches, usually located in the center of the leaf, especially if plants are succulent and growing vigorously. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to keep pothos away from air conditioning vents in the summer and from windowsills and heating vents in the winter, as hot and cold air can cause the leaves to dry out and damage plant cells. The fix is prevention, not treatment: keep the plant away from cold windows and vents, and inside before the first frost. A leaf with brown patches will not heal, but the next leaf will be fine — and keeping the plant warm is the cold rule in how to care for pothos.

### Mealybugs

Mealybugs are the most common insect pest, and they look like white puffs of cotton in the joints of the leaves. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the most common insect pests infesting pothos in homes are mealybugs and scale, and that mealybugs appear as white, cottony masses, frequently in the leaf axils, on the lower surfaces of leaves, and even on the roots. NC State Extension says to monitor for mealybugs and scales. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos are generally pest-free when kept indoors, but may be affected by common houseplant pests such as spider mites and mealybugs. The fix is to wipe the insects off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and to repeat for several weeks, because the eggs hatch in waves — and that alcohol-swab routine is the pest routine in how to care for pothos.

### Scale and spider mites

Scale looks like small brown bumps on the leaves and stems, and spider mites are tiny, with the first sign fine webbing on the undersides of the leaves. The University of Wisconsin Extension says scale looks like bark-colored bumps on the stems and leaves and is sometimes difficult to distinguish from the plant material on which it is feeding, and that spider mites occasionally infest pothos and can easily be controlled with thorough cleaning and frequent applications of insecticidal soap. NC State Extension says to monitor for mealybugs and scales. Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos may be affected by spider mites and mealybugs. The fix is to check the undersides of the leaves every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the leaf you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back in how to care for pothos.

### Keep it in the pot: the Florida warning

In warm climates this plant does not stay a houseplant, and the warning comes from the state that knows it best. UF/IFAS Extension assesses *Epipremnum aureum* as a high invasion risk in Florida, in the central, north, and south zones, and does not recommend it in the landscape. UF/IFAS Extension's Charlotte County office says that once the pothos vine is released, it can change into an invasive, and that escaped pothos can grow up trees where they develop enormous three-foot by two-foot leaves on thick, fifty-foot-long vines — and that the plant is a Category II invasive exotic in Florida. UF/IFAS Extension's Duval County office says mature pothos vines reach more than twenty feet high up into a pine tree in Jacksonville, and that any little plant part that gets torn off can become a whole new plant, even far from the ground. Do not plant pothos outdoors in a frost-free climate, do not put cuttings or prunings in the yard waste bin or the compost pile, and do not release unwanted plants into nearby natural areas. Keep it in the pot, and the plant stays a houseplant — and that is the invasion rule in how to care for pothos.

## Is pothos poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for pothos, because the plant is so common that it trails within reach of pets in millions of homes. [The ASPCA lists golden pothos, Epipremnum aureum, as toxic to dogs and to cats](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/golden-pothos), with the toxic principle given as insoluble calcium oxalates and the clinical signs given as oral irritation, intense burning and irritation of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. Penn State Extension says all parts of pothos are poisonous, yet non-lethal, to humans and their pets. Clemson Cooperative Extension says all parts of a pothos are mildly toxic if ingested. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos is toxic if ingested in large quantities because it contains calcium oxalate.

### The riskiest trailer on the bookshelf

The risk is not the height — it is the habit. A pothos trails from a shelf or a hanging basket, with leaves that hang within reach of a cat or a dog, and a pet that chews a leaf gets a mouthful of glass-like calcium oxalate crystals. The ASPCA says the signs are oral irritation, intense burning of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. The plant is not usually life-threatening, but it is painful, and a pet that chews a leaf needs a veterinarian. Keep the plant where pets cannot chew it — on a high shelf, or in a room the pets do not enter — and keep the cuttings out of reach too, because a cutting in a jar of water on a low table is a leaf a cat can reach.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

The safest houseplants for a home with pets are the ones that are not toxic at all, and the houseplant-care series on this site covers the whole range. The [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) is non-toxic to cats and dogs, the [aloe vera](/blog/how-to-care-for-aloe-vera/) is toxic if eaten, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) is toxic if chewed, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, and the [jade plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-jade-plant/) is toxic if eaten — and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is the safest flowering plant on the list. A pothos is not the plant for a home where a pet chews leaves, and a spider plant is the better choice for that room.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap can irritate skin, and the gloves matter. Penn State Extension says the roots, stems, and leaves contain calcium oxalate crystals that irritate the skin and the oral and digestive tracts. The University of Wisconsin Extension says pothos is toxic if ingested in large quantities because it contains calcium oxalate, that it causes burning in the mouth, and that the sap may also be irritating to the skin. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the crystals can cause mouth and throat irritation, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. Wash your hands after pruning, wear gloves when you take cuttings, and keep the cuttings away from children — because the crystals are in every part of the plant. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any pothos and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435.

## The short version

Bright, indirect light, a few feet from an east-facing or west-facing window, with no direct sun on the leaves. Water thoroughly when the top two inches of mix feel dry, roughly once a week in summer and every two to three weeks in winter, and never let the pot sit in water. Keep it warm, 60 to 80°F, never below 60°F if you can help it, and inside before the first cold snap. Keep the air humid if you can, 50 to 70% if you can manage it, and do not worry if you cannot. Feed lightly, a balanced liquid at half strength every other month from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Keep it in an airy mix of potting soil, bark, and perlite, in a pot with drainage holes, and give it a moss pole to climb or a basket to trail from. Prune in spring, back to a node, and root the cuttings. Expect eight to ten feet of vine indoors, with leaves three to six inches across, and bigger leaves on the newest growth if the light is right and the plant has a pole. Keep it away from pets, because it is toxic if chewed, and keep it in the pot, because it is invasive outdoors in warm climates. That is the whole of how to care for pothos, in one paragraph.

A pothos fills a bright room fast, and a second plant for the same room should want the same light and the same watering. - Watering: NC State let well-drained medium dry out between waterings; UW-Madison allow to dry slightly, water only when soil surface dry; Penn State water when soil dry, better too dry than too wet; Clemson allow soil to dry between waterings, water thoroughly so it reaches all roots; top 1.5–2 in dry (beginners guide); UF/IFAS better a little on the dry side indoors. Planning figure: finger test at 2 in, roughly weekly in summer, every 2–3 weeks in winter — labeled as planning figure.
- Temperature: Penn State 60–80°F; Clemson 60–70°F nights, 70–85°F days; UW-Madison 70–90°F best; UF/IFAS production 70–90°F, decline when days routinely exceed 95°F or nights drop below 65°F, indoor 70–80°F recommended, cold damage 33–40°F for several hours (brown patches center of leaf). Planning figure: 60–80°F, nights no colder than 60°F, inside before first cold snap.
- Humidity: NC State humidifier or wet pebble tray; UW-Madison high humidity; Clemson prefers 50–70%, average home 30–60%, tolerates low; UF/IFAS production 60–100% RH. Planning figure: 50–70% if you can, average household air survivable.
- Feeding: NC State every other month except winter dormancy; UW-Madison every other month except winter; Clemson every other month spring/summer; Penn State monthly through winter and every two weeks rest of year (the generous outlier). Planning figure: balanced liquid at half strength every other month spring–summer, nothing autumn/winter.
- Size: NC State 6–8 ft groundcover, vines to 40 ft; UW-Madison stem to 65 ft, juvenile leaves several inches, mature leaves to 3 ft with holes; Penn State stems 8–10 ft+, leaves 3–6 in; Clemson wild leaves to 3 ft with holes, houseplant leaves a few inches; UF/IFAS climbing trees 50 ft, leaves to 3 ft; interiorscape leaves seldom exceed 6 in. Planning figure: 8–10 ft indoors as a trailing vine, leaves 3–6 in.
- Zones: UW-Madison zones 10–12 only. Planning figure: houseplant everywhere else.
- Native range (sources disagree — shown as a spread): NC State Society Islands; Penn State + UF/IFAS Solomon Islands; Clemson China, India, Southeast Asia; UW-Madison genus from Southeast Asia to the western Pacific; Duval County blog Southeast Asia.
- Propagation: Clemson stem cuttings in soil or water, at least one node; UW-Madison cuttings or air-layering, root in water/vermiculite in 3–4 weeks, buds in 1–2 weeks warm, plant several cuttings together; UF/IFAS 1–1.5 in cuttings with node + leaf, buds 2–3 weeks, rooting 3–4 weeks; Penn State cuttings best spring–summer, layering via aerial roots at node; NC State vines easily root in water. Node rule: no node, no plant.
- Pests: NC State root rot + blackened margins from overwatering, mealybugs, scale; UW-Madison root rots from overwatering, mealybugs + scale most common, spider mites occasionally, salt buildup blackens margins/tips; Penn State root rot one of few problems; Clemson root rot, yellow leaves (overwatering/low fertility), leaf scorch/tip dieback (low humidity/intense light), spider mites + mealybugs.
- Pet safety: ASPCA toxic to dogs/cats, insoluble calcium oxalates, oral irritation, intense burning mouth/tongue/lips, drooling, vomiting, difficulty swallowing; Penn State all parts poisonous yet non-lethal; Clemson mildly toxic, keep out of reach of children and pets; UW-Madison toxic in large quantities, sap irritates skin.
- Invasion: UF/IFAS Assessment high invasion risk (Central, North, South zones); Category II invasive exotic in Florida; Charlotte County blog escaped vines grow 3 ft × 2 ft leaves on 50 ft vines; Duval County blog mature vines 20+ ft up a Jacksonville pine, leaves 2–3 ft once vertical, any torn-off piece can become a new plant.
- Air cleaning: UW-Madison page states pothos "is very efficient at removing indoor pollutants such as formaldehyde, xylene, and benzene" and the Charlotte County blog says pothos are "reported to be able to improve indoor air quality" — both recorded here as the sources' statements; no air-cleaning claim is made in the guide above.

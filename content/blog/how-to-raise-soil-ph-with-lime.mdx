Raising soil pH is slow, reasonably cheap, and harder to undo than to do. The arithmetic is
simple; the difficulty is that the number you most need is not the one a pH meter gives you.

## Why you might need to

Soil pH governs which nutrients are available to plants. Below about pH 6.0, phosphorus starts
to become locked up, and below 5.5 aluminium and manganese can reach levels that harm roots.
Most vegetables do best between pH 6.0 and 6.8, where the widest range of nutrients stays
available.

Acid soils are common where rainfall is high, where conifers grow, and where ammonium fertilizers
have been used for years. If a bed grows everything poorly despite reasonable soil and feeding,
pH is worth checking before anything else.

## The measurement that matters

Here is the thing most guides skip. A pH reading tells you the current acidity. It does not tell
you how strongly the soil will **resist** being changed, which is what determines how much lime
you need.

That resistance is called buffering capacity, and it comes mostly from clay content and organic
matter. A sandy soil at pH 5.5 might need a third of the lime that a clay soil at the same pH
needs, because there is far less in it holding the acidity in place.

This is why a soil test that reports **buffer pH**, or that gives a direct lime recommendation,
is worth so much more than a meter reading. A meter tells you there is a problem. A proper test
tells you how big it is.

Extension services in the US and Canada run these tests cheaply. If you are going to lime a bed,
get one first.

## Working from texture instead

If a buffer pH is not available, a texture-based estimate is the fallback. Rough figures for
pounds of ground limestone per 1,000 square feet, to raise pH by one unit in the top six to
seven inches:

| Soil texture | Per 1.0 pH unit | Published range |
| ------------ | --------------- | --------------- |
| Sandy        | about 25 lb     | 20-30 lb        |
| Loam         | about 60 lb     | 45-75 lb        |
| Clay         | about 95 lb     | 90-100 lb       |

From [AGR-214, _Liming Kentucky Lawns_](https://publications.mgcafe.uky.edu/files/AGR214.pdf)
(University of Kentucky Cooperative Extension, 2014), Table 1. Other states publish quite
different figures for the same texture names — the
[lime calculator](/tools/lime-calculator/) sets Kentucky and Colorado side by side and explains
why they diverge.

Squeeze a handful of damp soil to identify which you have: gritty and falling apart is sandy;
holding together but crumbling when poked is loam; sticky and ribboning between finger and
thumb is clay.

The [lime calculator](/tools/lime-calculator/) applies these to your own area and pH change, and
flags when it is being asked to extrapolate further than the figures support.

## Do not try to do it all at once

Lime requirement is treated as linear in the pH change for convenience, and that approximation
holds up reasonably over about one unit. Beyond about 1.5 units it stops being trustworthy.

So for a large correction — pH 4.8 to 6.5, say — apply roughly a unit and a half's worth, wait a
season, retest, and apply the rest if it is still needed. This is slower and far safer than
applying the full calculated amount and discovering you have overshot.

Overshooting is the real risk. Above about pH 7.0 iron, manganese and zinc start to become
unavailable, producing yellowing between the leaf veins that looks like a feeding problem and is
not. Bringing pH back down means elemental sulfur and another season of waiting.

## Applying it

**Work it in.** Limestone barely moves through soil under its own steam. Raking or digging it
into the top six inches puts it where the roots are; left on the surface of an undug bed it can
take years to have an effect.

**Autumn is the best time.** That gives it the whole winter to dissolve before the growing
season. Early spring is the next best.

**Spread it evenly.** Patchy application gives you patchy pH, which is worse than a uniformly
slightly-low pH. Split the quantity in half and make two passes at right angles.

**Keep it away from nitrogen.** Several weeks between liming and applying ammonium fertilizer or
fresh manure, or you lose nitrogen as ammonia.

**Wear a mask.** Even ordinary ground limestone is a fine dust that is unpleasant to breathe.

## The exceptions

**Potatoes.** They prefer soil at pH 5.0 to 6.5 and scab gets worse as pH rises towards neutral.
If a bed is in rotation, apply lime in the year it grows brassicas rather than the year it grows
potatoes.

**Blueberries, rhododendrons, azaleas and camellias** want pH 4.5 to 5.5 — far below anything you
would lime for. Liming near them actively harms them.

**Brassicas** are the crops that appreciate the upper end, partly because clubroot is less severe
in less acid soil.

## Retest

The whole point of applying less than the full calculated amount is that you check. Retest the
following season, before adding any more. Soil pH moves slowly in both directions, and a bed that
has been limed once will usually need topping up every few years rather than annually.

## The short version

Get a soil test that reports buffer pH or gives a lime recommendation. If you cannot, estimate
from texture: roughly 25, 60 or 95 pounds per 1,000 square feet per pH unit for sand, loam and
clay. Apply no more than a unit and a half's worth in a year, and no more than 100 pounds per
1,000 square feet in any one application — split anything larger between spring and autumn. Work
it into the top six inches, keep it away from nitrogen and away from potatoes, then retest before
doing any more.

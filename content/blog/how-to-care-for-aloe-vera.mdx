An aloe vera is the houseplant that forgives neglect and punishes kindness, and that contradiction is the first lesson in how to care for aloe vera. It survives missed waterings, dim corners, and months of being ignored, and it pays you back by making pups — small offsets around its base that you can pot up for free. And then the same plant turns brown, soft, and mushy in the hands of an owner who waters it every week out of love, because how to care for aloe vera is not the same as caring for the leafy tropical plants on the same windowsill. An aloe vera is a desert succulent that stores water in its thick leaves, and it wants the sunniest window in the house, a gritty mix that dries fast, and water only when the soil is dry all the way down. Give it those, and it lives for years and makes new plants for free.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. An aloe vera that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go thin, wrinkled, and curled inward, and then they plump back up soon after watering. An aloe vera that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go soft, mushy, and translucent at the base, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, goes pale, and leans toward the window. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much grows soft and floppy. Learning to read those five signals — thin leaves, soft leaves, stretch, scorch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for aloe vera, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are NC State Extension, Penn State Extension, UF/IFAS Extension, and South Dakota State University Extension, with the Royal Horticultural Society and the ASPCA corroborating the feeding and toxicity figures. If you bought the plant for its gel, the gel section covers that honestly. If you bought it because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the rotting section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most.

## How to care for aloe vera: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it the sunniest window you have, and introduce strong sun gradually.** A south- or west-facing windowsill with a few hours of direct sun is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for aloe vera, because the plant is a sun plant from a dry, bright place — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes pale, and leans, while a plant moved from shade into strong sun in one day scorches.

**Let the soil dry out completely between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the soil is dry all the way down, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying completely between waterings is the second rule of how to care for aloe vera.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room, never below 50°F if you can help it, and inside before the first frost. Cold is the third rule of how to care for aloe vera — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single cold night against freezing glass can damage leaves that never recover.

### The last two rules in how to care for aloe vera

**Feed barely, and only while it is growing.** A light feed once a month in spring and summer at half strength, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding grows soft, floppy leaves, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots.

**Keep it in a gritty mix, in a pot with holes, and repot only every two years.** A cactus or succulent mix in a clay pot with several drainage holes, repotted every two years into a pot just a little bigger. A pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots, and a mix that holds water like ordinary potting soil does the same. Free drainage is the fifth rule of how to care for aloe vera, and it protects the roots for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for aloe vera does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays firm and upright: thick, plump leaves in a tight rosette, grey-green and upright, with no stretch, no lean, and no pale color — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it makes pups: a mature plant in a sunny window with a snug pot fills the pot with offsets around its base, per NC State Extension, and each pup is a free plant you can separate and give away. Third, it lasts: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and repotted rarely grows to 2 to 3 feet tall over several years, per NC State Extension, and may even flower once it is three years old or more — a tall stalk of tubular orange to yellowish flowers, per NC State Extension — which is the reward for getting the five rules right. The five rules in how to care for aloe vera are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the Arabian Peninsula, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a desert plant in a pot

The first thing to get straight in how to care for aloe vera is what the plant actually is, because the name tells you only half the story. It is an aloe vera — *Aloe vera*, also sold as *Aloe barbadensis* — an evergreen succulent in the Asphodelaceae family, per NC State Extension, and it gets its common name from the Arabic word *alloeh*, meaning bitter and shiny substance, per NC State Extension. The bitterness is the point: the yellow latex just under the leaf rind is bitter enough to put off anything that chews it, and it is the same latex that makes the plant toxic to pets. The gel inside is a different substance entirely — clear, thick, and the part people have used on skin for centuries — and confusing the two is the most common mistake people make about this plant. The latex is not the gel, the gel is not the latex, and the rest of this guide keeps them apart.

### A plant from the Arabian Peninsula

In the wild the plant comes from the Arabian Peninsula, per NC State Extension, and South Dakota State University Extension gives the range more broadly: the regions of Africa, Madagascar, and the Arabian Peninsula, with about 400 unique species in the genus. Penn State Extension says the same: originating in Africa, aloes are members of the Asparagaceae family, and some species have been cultivated for over 2,000 years. That background explains almost everything about how to care for aloe vera. It is a plant from a dry, bright place where the rain comes rarely and the soil drains instantly, and it wants strong light, warm air, and a soil that dries out completely between rains. It is not a tropical leafy plant and it is not a cactus — it is a succulent that stores water in its leaves, stems, and roots, per Penn State Extension, which is why it tolerates drought and why it rots when it is kept wet. UF/IFAS Extension says the same in its own words: the plant is well adapted to droughty conditions, less-than-wonderful soil, and less-than-talented gardeners. South Dakota State University Extension adds that it has been referred to as the medicine plant, because its juices have been used to relieve pain from scrapes and burns — which is still the reason most people buy one.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a rosette of thick, fleshy, lance-shaped leaves fanning out from a central point, grey-green, with small white teeth along the margins. South Dakota State University Extension describes the leaves as green, fleshy, and lance-shaped, fanning out from the center of the plant, serrated with small teeth-like barbs on them. Penn State Extension says the same: most aloes grow in loose rosettes with their leaves gently curving upwards, narrow, fleshy, lance-shaped, tapering at the end, with serrated margins lined with soft teeth. NC State Extension says the leaves grow in a rosette, and that the thick leaves contain a gel used in many commercial products. The teeth look sharp and are soft to the touch — they are not spines, and they do not hurt — and the leaves themselves are soft and easy to break, per Penn State Extension, which is why a knocked-over plant snaps leaves instead of bending them. A plant that is getting enough light holds its leaves upright and firm; a plant in too little light stretches, its leaves go thin and limp, and the whole rosette leans toward the window. That lean is the first thing to check when you bring a plant home: a rosette that leans is a rosette that is asking for a brighter window.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for aloe vera, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the size, because the plant grows slowly but steadily, and the pot decides how big it gets. NC State Extension says aloe vera will grow 2 to 3 feet tall and spread by offsets. Penn State Extension says aloe vera can grow over 2 feet in height. UF/IFAS Extension says mature plants can grow upwards to two feet tall, and its Santa Rosa County factsheet gives 2 to 3 feet tall with a spread of 1 to 3 feet. Buy the size for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a windowsill, a standard plant for a bright corner, and a large plant only if you have a sunny floor space — because a plant that reaches 2 to 3 feet wide needs room, and a plant in a small pot stays small only until its roots run out of room.

### The age that matters: three years to flower

The second is the age, because the flowers only come to a mature plant. NC State Extension says the plant will send up a stem with tubular orange to yellowish flowers near the tip, blooming from the bottom up, once the plant is at least three years old. Penn State Extension says the same: the flowers will bloom only on mature plants, and are red or yellow-orange. UF/IFAS Extension says mature plants will eventually produce stalks of attractive showy yellow or red flowers in the spring, and its Santa Rosa factsheet says plants grown outdoors produce showy yellow or red flowers on stalks that emerge in late winter or spring, while plants grown indoors will rarely bloom. A plant you buy this year will not flower this year — and a plant kept indoors may never flower at all — and that is normal, not a failure. The flowers are a bonus, not the point, and a plant that never flowers is still a healthy plant.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, plump, and firm, grey-green, with no soft spots, no brown patches, and no mush at the base. Soft, mushy leaves at the base mean the plant has already been over-watered in the shop, and those leaves will not recover. Thin, wrinkled leaves mean the plant has gone too long without water — which is fixable, because the leaves plump back up after a drink, per South Dakota State University Extension — but a plant that is already wrinkled in the shop has been neglected, and a plump plant is a better buy. The soil should be dry on top, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a pot with no drainage holes has nowhere for the excess to go. Buy the plump, firm, right-sized plant in dry soil, and the rest of how to care for aloe vera is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for aloe vera, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. NC State Extension lists aloe vera for full sun to partial shade. Penn State Extension says aloes grow best, maintain their color, and flower when grown in full to partial sun, and that when growing indoors they should be placed near sunny windows so they can receive full sun to bright indirect light. UF/IFAS Extension says to place the plant in a full sun area. The Royal Horticultural Society says aloes enjoy full sun and dry conditions, such as a sunny window sill. South Dakota State University Extension says bright indirect light is the indoor standard, and that harsh direct sun can burn leaves that are not used to it. The planning figure this guide uses is your sunniest window, with a few hours of direct sun a day — a south- or west-facing sill is ideal, an unobstructed east window also works — and no dim rooms at any time of year. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the light that keeps the plant compact, upright, and grey-green.

### The sunburn warning

The warning that matters most is the sudden one. UF/IFAS Extension says the plant will adapt to less light when grown indoors, and then can actually sunburn if suddenly brought outdoors into full sun. South Dakota State University Extension says the same: harsh direct sun can burn the leaves, so move indoor plants into stronger outdoor sun a little at a time. Penn State Extension says to move the plant outdoors in the summer only with the same care, and to bring it back inside before the threat of frost. The burn shows as tan or brown dry patches on the sunny side of the leaves, and a scorched leaf never recovers its color — the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. If your plant came from a dim shop shelf, give it bright indirect light for two weeks, then move it into a spot that gets an hour or two of direct morning sun, and then into its permanent sunny position over the next two to three weeks. That gradual move is the light rule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters second is the delayed one. A plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never grows properly, and the owner concludes that aloe vera simply grows slowly, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. South Dakota State University Extension says too little light makes the plant leggy, with weak, drooping leaves. Penn State Extension says to rotate the container periodically to ensure all sides of the plant receive adequate light, and that grow lights are a good option if you do not have adequate natural lighting. The variegation fades in low light too, and the leaves go thin and pale, because a leaf that needs to photosynthesize harder stretches toward the light to do it. If your plant leans toward the window, or its leaves have gone thin and pale, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for aloe vera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Water

Watering is where most aloe vera plants are lost, and the loss usually comes from one mistake: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain. The watering rule from NC State Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for aloe vera: allow the soil to completely dry between waterings. Two halves, both required: let the soil dry completely, and then water thoroughly. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. And then remove the excess, so the pot never sits in water.

Penn State Extension gives the same rule with the seasons named: during the growing season, from early spring to early fall, water thoroughly when the soil's surface is dry, and during the dormant period, from fall through winter, reduce watering so the soil is barely moist and the leaves do not shrivel — and as with all succulents, it is best to err on the side of underwatering. UF/IFAS Extension says the same in its own words: do not overwater, and do not leave excess water sitting in the saucer underneath the pot. The Royal Horticultural Society says to water sparingly, only when the compost feels dry, and hardly at all in winter. All four sources agree on the shape of the rule — dry completely, then soak — and they disagree only on the interval, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season.

### How often to water

The planning figure this guide uses is simple: push a finger two inches into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry all the way down — roughly every two to three weeks in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to once a month or less in a cool room in winter. That range satisfies all the sources, and it keeps the soil dry between waterings without ever letting the plant shrivel. It is a planning figure, not a schedule from any source: no extension source gives a fixed watering calendar for aloe vera, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season. A small pot in a warm, bright room dries faster than a large pot in a cool, dim one, and a plant in summer drinks faster than the same plant in winter. The thick leaves store water, per Penn State Extension, which is why a missed watering is forgiven — and why a plant that is watered every week, whether it needs it or not, rots. That finger test is the schedule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is the only one that works in every house.

### The water itself does not matter much

There is one watering rule in how to care for aloe vera that is different from the leafy tropical plants: the water itself matters very little, and the drainage matters everything. Aloe vera is not sensitive to fluoride or salts the way a spider plant is, and no extension source I verified gives a water-quality rule for it. What matters is that the excess leaves the pot. NC State Extension says the pot should have several drainage holes and that clay is best. Penn State Extension says to use pots with large drainage holes that allow extra water to filter out, and never to allow the plant to sit in water. UF/IFAS Extension says no excess water sitting in the saucer underneath the pot. A plant in a pot with no drainage holes, watered on a schedule, will rot no matter how careful the owner is — because the water has nowhere to go, and the roots sit in it. That drainage is the watering rule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is decided by the pot, not by the watering can.

### Temperature

Temperature is the easiest of the three to get right, because the range the plant wants is the range a warm house already has. The sources give a small spread, and it is worth showing honestly. Penn State Extension says aloes are not frost tolerant, so you must take them indoors before the threat of frost. UF/IFAS Extension says aloe is injured by frost in north Florida, and its Santa Rosa factsheet lists the plant for USDA hardiness zones 8 through 11. NC State Extension lists it as an outdoor perennial in zones 10 and higher. The Royal Horticultural Society says aloes cannot cope with freezing temperatures. South Dakota State University Extension gives the indoor range as 55°F to 85°F, which resembles the plant's natural environment, and most homes fall within that range.

The planning figure this guide uses is an ordinary warm room, never below 50°F indoors if you can help it, with 55°F to 85°F as the comfortable range, and inside before the first frost. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is ordinary indoor temperature in a house that is not overheated. That is why temperature is the easiest of the three decisions in how to care for aloe vera: the house already provides it, and the job is simply not to put the plant somewhere colder than the room itself — or outside on a night when frost is forecast, because a single frost can damage leaves that never recover.

### What to avoid: cold and heat

Two things matter more than the exact number. The first is cold: do not put the plant against freezing glass in winter, do not leave it outside on a night when frost is forecast, and do not put it next to a drafty window or an exterior door. A chill that a hardy houseplant would ignore can damage this desert plant, and the damage shows as brown, translucent patches on the leaves. The second is heat: do not put the plant next to a radiator or a heating vent, and do not leave it in a room that sits above 85°F for days. Hot, dry air is not the problem — the plant tolerates being near a radiator, per the Royal Horticultural Society — but a room that is hot and wet is, because the soil stays damp and the roots rot. Warm and steady is the temperature rule in how to care for aloe vera, and a room at 55°F to 85°F away from freezing glass is very nearly ideal.

### Humidity

Humidity is the non-problem, and the one most owners overthink. The aloe vera comes from a dry place, and the dry air of a centrally heated home in winter is exactly what it wants. The Royal Horticultural Society says aloes dislike humid atmospheres. No extension source I verified gives a humidity percentage for aloe vera, so this guide gives none — the honest statement is that average household humidity is usually fine, and that dry winter air suits the plant well, per South Dakota State University Extension. Do not mist the leaves, do not set up a pebble tray, and do not run a humidifier for this plant: water sitting in the rosette rots the leaves, and humidity is the one thing this plant does not want. Get the humidity wrong in the other direction — a damp, steamy bathroom — and the plant shows it with soft leaves and rust spots, which is the most common bathroom complaint in how to care for aloe vera.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

The soil rule in how to care for aloe vera is short: gritty, fast-draining, in a pot with holes. Penn State Extension says aloes prefer a coarse, well-draining potting mix, and gives a recipe: one part potting soil, one part sand, and one part perlite or lava rocks. NC State Extension says to grow the plant in very well-drained soils suitable for succulents. UF/IFAS Extension says to grow it in a pot using well-drained soil suitable for cacti. The Royal Horticultural Society says to use multipurpose compost mixed with 50 percent sand and grit. All four sources agree, and none of them asks for anything rich — which is the point. This plant does not need a rich mix or a special recipe. It needs a mix that drains.

### A simple mix that drains

The planning figure this guide uses is a commercial cactus or succulent mix, or the Penn State recipe of one part potting soil, one part sand, and one part perlite or lava rock, in a pot with drainage holes. Free drainage is the foundation of how to care for aloe vera in a pot, because every watering rule in this guide assumes the excess can leave. Our [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs, and our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself — a recipe of potting soil, perlite, and compost that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy. The comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite is the right lightener here: it drains and keeps air in the mix, and this plant needs drainage more than it needs moisture retention — the mix should dry out completely between waterings, not hold water like a sponge. The thick leaves store water already, so a mix that holds too much water on top of that is a mix that rots.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the other half of the drainage rule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is the half most owners get wrong. NC State Extension says the pot used should have several drainage holes and that clay is best. Penn State Extension says to use pots with large drainage holes that allow extra water to filter out. A clay or terracotta pot is best because the porous walls let excess moisture evaporate, so the soil dries faster — and a plant in a plastic pot without holes, however pretty the pot, will stay wet longer and rot sooner. The pot should also be the right size: UF/IFAS Extension says to step the plant up into a pot just a bit bigger than the old one, because too big a container could hold excess moisture and trigger a root rot situation. A pot that is too large holds a volume of soil the roots cannot dry out, and that wet soil is where rot starts. That snug pot is the potting rule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is decided by the pot, not by the plant.

### Repotting: every two years, one size up

Repotting is the part of how to care for aloe vera that surprises people, because the answer is to do it rarely — and because the plant tells you when it is time in a way that is easy to miss. Penn State Extension says replanting your aloe every two years can help refresh the soil and reduce crowding of the roots. When doing so, choose a container slightly larger than the previous one, allowing 1 to 1.5 inches between the root ball and the edge of the pot, and place it in the pot so its leaves are just above the soil level. UF/IFAS Extension says the same from the other side: repotting may be required from time to time as the plant grows and expands from sideshoots, and to step it up into a pot just a bit bigger than the old one. The signal is the roots: when they poke out of the drainage holes, or crowd the pot, or push the plant up out of the soil, the plant has outgrown its home. And when the pups crowd the pot so thickly that the mother plant has no room left, that is also a repotting signal — separate the pups, refresh the mix, and put the mother back in the same pot if it is still the right size.

### Go one size up, and keep the leaves above the soil

When you do repot, go only one pot size up — an inch or two wider — and keep the plant at the same depth, with the leaves just above the soil level, per Penn State Extension. Do not bury the lower leaves: leaves buried in soil rot, and a plant potted too deep rots from the base upward. Do not break the root ball apart or wash the old soil off: the roots resent disturbance, and a rough repotting can set the plant back a whole season. Water sparingly until the plant is established in its new pot, and wait a week before the first watering after repotting, so any damaged roots can callus over before they meet wet soil. A plant that is repotted gently, rarely, and one size up will keep growing steadily for years; a plant that is repotted often and roughly will droop and yellow every time. That restraint is the repotting rule in how to care for aloe vera.

## Feeding: barely, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. The Royal Horticultural Society says to encourage good growth and flowering, feed once a month during the growing season, from April to September, using a liquid feed, and not to feed in the winter. South Dakota State University Extension says aloe vera usually needs no fertilizer to stay healthy, because it grows naturally in nutrient-poor soil. None of the US extension pages I verified gives a feeding schedule for aloe vera at all — which is itself the message: the plant is a light feeder, and feeding is not a core part of its care. The honest reading of the spread is that the plant needs very little, and that a plant that is never fed at all will do fine, while a plant that is fed heavily will grow soft and floppy.

### The planning figure: a light feed in summer, or none at all

The planning figure this guide uses is a light feed once a month in spring and summer at half strength, or no feed at all — and nothing at all in autumn and winter. That range satisfies both sources: it is the middle of the spread, it is dilute rather than full-strength, and it stops when growth stops. All the sources agree on the season, and on stopping when growth stops, which is the part of how to care for aloe vera that matters most: a plant that is fed only while it is growing never accumulates the salts that burn its roots — and never grows so soft that it flops over under its own weight. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer or a cactus fertilizer at half the label strength, feed only when the soil is already moist, never onto dry soil, and skip a feeding if the plant has just been repotted or is showing stress. If the leaves are already soft from over-feeding, flush the soil with plain water — a thorough watering that runs out of the drainage holes, repeated once — and stop feeding until spring. That light hand is the feeding rule in how to care for aloe vera, and it is the cheapest of the five rules to follow: a single bottle of balanced liquid feed lasts years at this rate.

### What over-feeding does

An aloe vera that puts on a few inches a year does not need much food, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots and soften the leaves. The plant grows soft and floppy, its leaves lose their firm upright stance, and it becomes more prone to rot — because soft, over-fed growth rots faster than firm, slow growth. That is the double cost of over-feeding in how to care for aloe vera: soft leaves and rot, at the same time, from the same mistake. A plant that is fed lightly in summer and not at all in winter stays firm, upright, and grey-green, and it is the plant that makes pups. Use a balanced liquid fertilizer at half strength once a month in spring and summer, feed only when the soil is already moist, and skip a feeding if the plant is stressed. That restraint is the feeding rule in how to care for aloe vera.

## Getting it to make pups

Making pups is the reason most people buy a second aloe vera, and it is also the most misunderstood part of how to care for aloe vera — because the plant makes pups on its own schedule, not yours, and because the shop plant you bought was probably already carrying them before you ever saw it. Understanding that is the key to not being disappointed.

### Why a young plant makes no pups

The first reason is youth. A plant under a year old simply has not matured enough to make pups, and no amount of fertilizer will change that — and fertilizer, as the last section showed, makes it worse, not better. The second reason is light, and it is the common one: a plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never makes a pup, because making pups takes energy the plant is not making. Move it to your sunniest window, give it a season, and the pups usually follow. The third reason is the pot: a plant that has just been repotted into a pot that is too large will make roots and leaves before it makes pups, and it may take a season to settle. A mature plant in good light, in a snug pot, fills the pot with offsets around its base, per NC State Extension — and that crowding is the sign the plant is happy, not a problem to fix.

### Separating the pups in how to care for aloe vera

The time to separate pups is spring, when the plant is growing again, and the method is simple. The Royal Horticultural Society gives the steps: wait until you see an offset growing next to the parent, gently lift the plant out of its pot over a tray to catch the loose soil, carefully separate the small offset from the parent, if possible with some roots attached, and set the small plant aside while you prepare a pot with a mix of 50 percent grit or gravel and 50 percent multipurpose compost. Put the offset in the pot, fill with the mix, water it to settle the compost around the roots, and position it in a light, sunny place indoors. Penn State Extension says the same in fewer words: the pups may be removed from the mother plant and transplanted into another pot. Wait until a pup has a few leaves of its own and its own roots before separating it, set it so its leaves sit just above the soil level, per Penn State Extension, and water it sparingly until it is established. A pup with no roots of its own usually rots before it roots, so patience at this step saves the pup.

### What about leaf cuttings?

There is no leaf-cutting method in how to care for aloe vera, and that is worth saying plainly, because every other succulent seems to root from a leaf. The extension sources describe one way to make more plants — the offsets, by division — and none of them describes growing a new plant from a single cut leaf. A leaf cut from the plant has no base, no roots, and no growing point, and it rots in the soil before it ever roots. The pup is the plant's own way of multiplying, and it is the only one the sources support. If you want more plants, wait for the pups, separate them in spring, and give them away — a mature plant in a sunny window makes enough pups for the whole street, and that generosity is the propagation rule in how to care for aloe vera.

### A note on the gel

You may have bought the plant for its gel, and the gel is real — but it is worth knowing what it is and what it is not. NC State Extension says the thick leaves contain a gel used in many commercial products, and that the stem can be broken and the gel applied to the skin, and it is safe for most people. Penn State Extension says the leaves contain a gel-like sap that some use for medicinal purposes such as treating superficial burns and sunburn. UF/IFAS Extension says the gelatinous material that comes from the thick leaves has been used to soothe minor burns and sunburns, and its Santa Rosa factsheet says the gel is used for soothing minor burns, wounds, and various skin conditions like eczema and ringworm. South Dakota State University Extension says the plant has been referred to as the medicine plant, because its juices have been used to relieve pain from scrapes and burns, and to help acne outbreaks.

### The gel and the latex are not the same thing

That history is real — and it is about the clear gel inside the leaf, applied to skin, not about eating the plant. The yellow latex just under the leaf rind is a different substance, and it is the part that causes nausea and indigestion if swallowed, per South Dakota State University Extension, and vomiting and diarrhea in pets, per the ASPCA. This guide is about growing the plant, not a treatment recommendation: do not eat home-cut aloe, do not give it to children, and do not use it as a substitute for medical care. If you do use the gel on skin, cut a mature outer leaf, stand it upright in a cup for a few minutes so the yellow latex drains away, and use only the clear gel — and stop if the skin reacts. Buy the aloe vera for its sculptural leaves and its free pups, which are real, and treat the gel as a bonus, not the reason to own the plant. That honesty is part of how to care for aloe vera: the plant is worth having for what it actually does, and it does that very well.

### A worked example: one plant, bought today

Here is the whole first year as a single story, for a plant bought on Friday, 20 November 2026. The plant is a standard aloe vera, 12 inches tall in a 6-inch clay pot, grey-green and firm to the touch, with two small pups already crowding the base. You put it on your sunniest windowsill, where it gets a few hours of direct sun and no shade, and you mark Sundays for the finger test: push a finger two inches into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry all the way down, emptying the saucer every time. You do not feed it at all: it is November, and the plant is dormant, so feeding waits until spring.

Through the winter you water it once a month or less, because the plant is barely growing and the soil stays dry for weeks. The leaves stay plump and upright, and the pups around the base grow a little bigger. In spring you feed it for the first time: a balanced liquid fertilizer at half strength, once a month, and nothing more. The plant grows a few inches over the year, puts out new leaves from the center of the rosette, and by next autumn the pups have grown big enough to separate — and you pot two of them up in small clay pots of cactus mix, per the Royal Horticultural Society, and give one to a friend.

### Spring: the first feeding and the first pups in how to care for aloe vera

The repotting comes in spring 2028, one pot size up, keeping the root ball mostly intact, when the roots poke out of the drainage holes. You set the plant so its leaves sit just above the soil level, per Penn State Extension, and you wait a week before watering it again. After a few years of that routine, the plant is 2 to 3 feet tall, per NC State Extension, with a ring of pups around its base, and it has made a dozen new plants you gave away. If it is three years old or more, it may send up a tall stalk of tubular orange to yellowish flowers, per NC State Extension — and that flower stalk is the sign the plant has had everything it needs. That is the whole story of how to care for aloe vera: a finger in the soil, the sunniest window, a warm room, a gritty mix, a little food in summer, a snug pot, and patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most aloe vera problems have one of four causes — water, light, cold, or feeding — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a damaged leaf never heals. A browned leaf will not turn green again, so troubleshooting in how to care for aloe vera is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Soft, mushy leaves

Soft, mushy, translucent leaves at the base, with wet soil, mean root rot — and it is the most serious problem this plant has. NC State Extension says overwatering should be avoided because it can lead to the roots rotting. Penn State Extension says aloes are extremely drought tolerant and susceptible to root rot, so never allow the plant to sit in water. South Dakota State University Extension says root rot is due to overwatering, has no treatment, and can be fatal if not caught early enough. The fix is to stop watering immediately, take the plant out of its pot, cut away the soft, brown, rotted roots with a clean knife, and repot what is left into dry cactus mix in a clay pot with drainage holes. Do not water it for a week, and then water only when the soil is dry all the way down. A plant caught early recovers; a plant whose whole base has gone soft usually does not. That urgency is the rot rule in how to care for aloe vera: dry soil saves plants, wet soil kills them.

### Thin, wrinkled leaves

Thin, wrinkled leaves that curl inward along their length, with bone-dry soil, mean the plant has gone too long without water — and this one is fixable. South Dakota State University Extension says the leaves plump back up after a drink. Give the plant a thorough watering, until water runs out of the drainage holes, and check it again in a day: the leaves should be firming up. If they do not plump back up, check the roots: a plant whose roots have dried out and died cannot take up water, and it needs repotting into fresh mix. Do not overcorrect — a thirsty plant that is suddenly drowned rots — so water thoroughly once, let it drain, and then go back to the finger test. That restraint is the thirst rule in how to care for aloe vera.

### Brown tips

Brown tips have three possible causes, and they are worth separating. The first is thirst: thin, wrinkled leaves with brown, dry tips and bone-dry soil mean the plant needs water. The second is sunburn: tan or brown dry patches on the sunny side after a sudden move into strong sun, per UF/IFAS Extension, mean the light changed too fast — move the plant back into bright indirect light and reintroduce the sun gradually. The third is cold: brown, translucent patches after a cold night mean frost damage, per UF/IFAS Extension and Penn State Extension, and the damaged leaves will not recover. Do not cut the brown tips off the leaves — the brown part is dead, but trimming it leaves a blunt end that looks worse and does not help the leaf. Leave the tips alone, fix the cause, and judge the plant by its new growth, not by the browned tips. That patience is the troubleshooting habit in how to care for aloe vera.

### Leggy, stretched growth

A plant that stretches, with a long bare stem and leaves spaced far apart, is short of light — and it will not shrink back. South Dakota State University Extension says too little light makes the plant leggy, with weak, drooping leaves. Move the plant to your sunniest window, rotate the container periodically so every side gets light, per Penn State Extension, and wait: the new leaves from the center will grow compact and upright, even though the stretched stem stays stretched. If the plant has become too tall and floppy to stand, you can cut the top off and let it callus over for a few days before potting it — but the extension sources describe no such method, so treat it as a rescue, not a routine, and keep the pups as your replacement plants instead. That light is the stretch rule in how to care for aloe vera: the sunniest window, or a grow light, and no dim rooms.

### Sunburn

Sunburn shows as tan or brown dry patches on the side of the plant that faces the sun, appearing a day or two after a sudden move into strong light. UF/IFAS Extension says a plant grown indoors in less light can sunburn if suddenly brought outdoors into full sun. South Dakota State University Extension says harsh direct sun can burn the leaves, so move indoor plants into stronger light a little at a time. The fix is to move the plant back into bright indirect light, and to reintroduce the sun gradually over a week or two. A scorched leaf never recovers its color, so leave it in place — it still photosynthesizes — and judge the plant by its new growth. That gradual move is the sunburn rule in how to care for aloe vera.

### Pests

The aloe vera is generally a healthy plant indoors, and pest trouble is uncommon — but there are three pests to watch for. NC State Extension says mealybugs and aphids are insects that may affect the health of your aloe plant, and lists scale, leaf spot, aloe rust, and sooty mold among the diseases. South Dakota State University Extension lists the same pests: mealybugs, aloe scale, and aloe mite may be a problem, and mealybugs usually congregate at the base of the plant, leaving behind a sticky secretion that creates an environment for mold to set in, and insecticidal soap can be used to control mealybugs, following the label on the product for the best results. Check the tight leaf bases when you water, wipe off any mealybugs or scale you find with a damp cloth or a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and let the soil dry completely between waterings to discourage fungus gnats — our guide to [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers that pest in full. Outdoors, UF/IFAS Extension lists caterpillars as the one pest to watch for. That wipe is the pest rule in how to care for aloe vera: check the leaf bases when you water, and act early.

### Aloe rust

Aloe rust shows as yellow spots that turn brown on the leaves, and it is the one disease that looks like a watering problem but is not. South Dakota State University Extension says cool temperatures and high humidity are the cause of rust and fungal diseases, and that yellow spots that turn brown on the leaves are the sign of aloe rust, and it usually does not need treatment. NC State Extension lists aloe rust among the diseases, alongside leaf spot and sooty mold. The fix is the conditions, not a spray: move the plant somewhere warmer and drier, stop misting it, let the soil dry out completely between waterings, and give it more light and more air. A plant in a damp, steamy bathroom will rust; the same plant on a sunny, dry windowsill will not. That dry air is the rust rule in how to care for aloe vera.

## Is aloe vera poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for aloe vera, because the plant is so common that it sits within reach of pets in millions of homes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/aloe) lists aloe (*Aloe vera*) as toxic to dogs, toxic to cats, and toxic to horses, naming saponins and anthraquinones as the toxic principles, with vomiting, lethargy, and diarrhea as the clinical signs — and notes that the clear inner gel is considered edible, which is not the same as the whole leaf being safe. South Dakota State University Extension says the same in plain words: people or pets should not ingest the plant, because it can cause nausea and indigestion if taken in large quantities. The bitter yellow latex just under the leaf rind is the part that causes the trouble, not the clear gel inside — and a pet that chews a leaf gets a mouthful of latex, not gel.

### The riskiest plant on the sunny windowsill

That toxic listing is what makes the aloe vera the riskiest plant on the sunny windowsill, and the comparison with its neighbors is worth making: the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) is non-toxic to cats and dogs, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) is toxic if chewed, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, and the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) is toxic. The aloe vera sits at the dangerous end of that row — not the most dangerous plant in the house, and the one you should keep where pets cannot chew it. A cat that treats the leaves as a toy will chew them and may bring them back up, and the plant will look ragged — and the cat may vomit, go lethargic, and have diarrhea, which is a vet visit, not a shrug. If you want a sunny windowsill plant in a house with cats, the spider plant is the plant to put there first.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Toxic does not mean the plant has to live in a locked room — it means the plant has to live where pets cannot chew it. Put it on a high shelf, in a hanging planter, or in a room the pets do not enter, and keep the pups out of reach too. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any aloe and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. For a household with cats, dogs, and small children, that placement is the one safety rule in how to care for aloe vera that matters more than any other — and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

## The short version

How to care for aloe vera, in one paragraph: buy a plump, firm, right-sized plant — a small one for a windowsill, a standard one for a bright corner, a large one only if you have sunny floor space; put it in your sunniest window, with a few hours of direct sun, introduced gradually over a week or two if it came from a dim shop shelf; water thoroughly only when the soil is dry all the way down, drain it fully, and empty the saucer — roughly every two to three weeks in summer, once a month or less in a cool winter room; use a cactus or succulent mix, or one part potting soil, one part sand, and one part perlite, in a clay pot with several drainage holes; keep it at 55 to 85°F, never below 50°F, and inside before the first frost; feed at half strength once a month in spring and summer, or not at all, and nothing in autumn and winter; repot every two years, one size up, with the leaves just above the soil; separate the pups in spring, with roots attached, into gritty mix; and keep it where pets cannot chew it, because it is toxic to cats and dogs. Do that, and the 12-inch plant you buy this November will be a 2- to 3-foot plant in a few years, making pups you can give away — and a plant you can keep for a decade.

If you want a second houseplant for the same windowsill, the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) thrives in the same bright room but wants more water and is safe for pets, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) flowers in the same bright room but needs steadier moisture, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) blooms in the same cool room, and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) flowers on the same short days with none of the watering worry — though it is toxic too, so keep it out of reach as well.

the Aloe Vera plant is great for beginners, because it requires very little care; native to the regions of Africa, Madagascar, and the Arabian Peninsula, and there are about 400 unique species of the plant; green, fleshy, lance-shaped leaves that fan out from the center of the plant, serrated with small teeth-like barbs on them; referred to as the medicine plant, because its juices have been used to relieve pain from scrapes and burns, and to help acne outbreaks; pests: mealybugs, aloe scale, and aloe mite may be a problem, mealybugs usually congregate at the base of the plant and leave behind a sticky secretion that creates an environment for mold to set in, and insecticidal soap can be used to control mealybugs, following the label on the product; diseases: aloe rust, root rot, and fungal disease may appear, cool temperatures and high humidity are the cause of rust and fungal diseases, yellow spots that turn brown on the leaves are the sign of aloe rust and it usually does not need treatment, root rot is due to overwatering, has no treatment, and can be fatal if not caught early enough; a word of caution, people or pets should not ingest the plant, because it can cause nausea and indigestion if taken in large quantities; light, temperature, watering rhythm, and fertilizer figures from this page are corroborated, not directly verified: bright indirect light, harsh direct sun can burn leaves not used to it, too little light makes the plant leggy with weak drooping leaves, regular thorough watering letting the soil dry out completely before watering again, temperatures of 55°F to 85°F, sandy soil or cactus mix, grows naturally in nutrient-poor soil, usually needs no fertilizer, and leaves plump back up after a drink when thirsty — as quoted by multiple independent secondary sources citing this page)
- Royal Horticultural Society — [Indoor Plants: Aloe plants including Aloe vera](https://www.rhs.org.uk/getmedia/d02059a1-4e2c-45c3-956b-da5cc90db7f1/indoor-plants-resources.pdf) (verified 2026-11-20; aloes are sculptural plants that usually form compact, spiralling rosettes of leaves, easily grown in containers and great as houseplants and for placing outdoors during the summer; the sap can be used to soothe mild sunburn; where to grow them: they enjoy full sun and dry conditions, such as a sunny window sill, and can tolerate being near a radiator; compost: use multipurpose peat-free compost mixed with 50 percent sand and grit; water sparingly, only water when the compost feels dry and hardly at all in winter; aloes will not grow well in continuously wet compost or in shade; they dislike humid atmospheres and cannot cope with freezing temperatures; feeding: to encourage good growth and flowering, feed once a month during the growing season, from April to September, using a liquid feed, and don't feed in the winter; propagating: once you have a good healthy aloe plant, it is very easy to propagate many plants from it, and when mature, they produce lots of offsets that can be separated and grown on their own — wait until you see an offset growing next to the parent, gently lift the plant out of its pot, carefully separate the small offset from the parent, if possible with some roots attached, mix together 50 percent grit or gravel and 50 percent multipurpose peat-free compost, put the offset in a suitably sized pot, water it to settle the compost around the roots, and position it in a light, sunny place indoors)

The three numbers on fertilizer are the percentages by weight of nitrogen, phosphate and
potash, always in that order. A 50 lb bag of 10-10-10 holds 5 lb of each, and 35 lb of carrier.
The middle number is **phosphate (P₂O₅), not phosphorus** — a distinction most pages get wrong,
and the one that makes bags comparable to soil test results.

## What each number stands for

They are percentages by weight, always in the same order:

| Position | Nutrient  | Reported as     | What it drives                            |
| -------- | --------- | --------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| First    | Nitrogen  | N (elemental)   | Leaf and stem growth                      |
| Second   | Phosphate | P₂O₅, **not** P | Roots, flowering, fruit set               |
| Third    | Potash    | K₂O, **not** K  | Vigour, fruit quality, disease resistance |

So a bag marked 10-10-10 is 10% nitrogen, 10% phosphate and 10% potash by weight. The remaining
70% is carrier, filler and any secondary nutrients.

To find how much of a nutrient is actually in a bag, multiply weight by percentage:

```
50 lb bag x 10% nitrogen = 5 lb of nitrogen
```

That single line makes price comparison possible. A 50 lb bag of 10-10-10 contains 5 lb of
nitrogen. A 20 lb bag of 25-0-0 contains the same 5 lb. If nitrogen is what you need, they are
equivalent products at very different weights. The
[fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) does this conversion both ways — from a
target rate to pounds of product, and from a bag to what it actually delivers.

## The second number is not phosphorus

This is the part that trips people up, including gardeners of long standing, and including
several of the pages that currently rank for this question.

US fertilizer labelling law requires the second figure to be **available phosphate**, expressed
as P₂O₅, and the third to be **soluble potash**, expressed as K₂O. Neither is the pure element.

| Reported as     | Element it contains | Conversion      |
| --------------- | ------------------- | --------------- |
| Phosphate, P₂O₅ | Phosphorus, P       | P₂O₅ × 0.44 = P |
| Potash, K₂O     | Potassium, K        | K₂O × 0.83 = K  |

Does it matter in practice? Usually not — and the reason is worth understanding. Extension
service and soil test recommendations in the US are written in the same phosphate and potash
terms. If a soil test says you need 2 lb of phosphate per 1,000 sq ft and a bag says 10%
phosphate, the two numbers speak the same language and no conversion is needed.

It matters when you read agronomic literature using elemental P and K, or compare against a
label from a country that reports differently. Check which convention a figure uses before you
act on it.

## What does 10-10-10 fertilizer mean?

It means equal parts by weight: 10% nitrogen, 10% phosphate, 10% potash. It does not mean
balanced for your soil, and on most established gardens it is the wrong bag.

Phosphate and potash accumulate in soil. Nitrogen does not — it leaches and gasses off, so it
genuinely needs replacing each season. A decade of 10-10-10 puts on far more phosphate than
crops remove, so the usual state of a long-gardened bed is plenty of phosphate, adequate potash
and not enough nitrogen. Applying 10-10-10 to that soil adds phosphate the plants cannot use
and that can wash into watercourses.

A soil test tells you which of the three you are short of, and the answer is often nitrogen
alone.

## Reading the rest of the label

**Guaranteed analysis** is the legally required panel. It repeats the three headline numbers and
adds anything else the product guarantees, with minimum percentages.

**The nitrogen breakdown** is the most useful small print on the bag:

| Form on the label        | Availability | Practical effect                           |
| ------------------------ | ------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Nitrate nitrogen         | Immediate    | Acts in days, leaches quickly              |
| Ammoniacal nitrogen      | Fast         | Held slightly better by soil               |
| Urea nitrogen            | Moderate     | Needs soil microbes to convert it first    |
| Water insoluble nitrogen | Slow         | Feeds over months, often organic in origin |

A product that is mostly nitrate acts within days and is gone within weeks. One that is mostly
water-insoluble feeds over months. For vegetables a mix is usually ideal: something available
now, something that keeps going.

**Complete** means the product contains all three of N, P and K. It does not mean complete for
your soil.

## Working out how much to spread

Recommendations come as a rate of _nutrient_ per area. Bags contain _product_. The conversion is
one line:

```
lb of product = (target rate / (nutrient % / 100)) x (area / 1000)
```

To put 1 lb of nitrogen on 1,000 sq ft using 10-10-10: 1 ÷ 0.10 = **10 lb of product**. Using
46-0-0 instead: 1 ÷ 0.46 = **2.2 lb**. Same nitrogen, very different quantity to carry.

Then check what came along with it. Those 10 lb of 10-10-10 also delivered a pound of phosphate
and a pound of potash, whether or not the soil needed them. That is the figure worth looking at
before you buy the balanced bag out of habit.

## Before you buy anything

Get a soil test. Through most US and Canadian extension services one costs less than two bags of
fertilizer, and it tells you your pH, your phosphate and potash levels, and usually a direct
recommendation. [Michigan State University Extension](https://canr.msu.edu/resources/fertilizer-labels)
publishes a good primer on reading the label itself.

If the test comes back showing low pH as well, that is a separate job — lime, not fertilizer —
and worth doing first, because nutrients already in the soil become unavailable at low pH. See
[how to raise soil pH](/blog/how-to-raise-soil-ph/) for the timing, and
the [lime calculator](/tools/lime-calculator/) for the quantity. Once you know what to apply,
[how often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the schedule, and the [tomato guide](/crops/tomato/) has the specifics for the crop most
people over-feed.

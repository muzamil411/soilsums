The best mulch for a vegetable garden is **straw or shredded leaves** — both suppress weeds,
hold moisture and break down into the soil within a season, which is exactly what an annual bed
wants. Wood chips are the wrong choice for vegetables: at around 400:1 carbon to nitrogen they
last years and tie up nitrogen where they meet the soil.

## What mulch is for

Three jobs, and different materials are good at different parts of it:

- **Blocking light** from weed seeds so they never germinate.
- **Slowing evaporation** from the soil surface, which is the single biggest saving on watering.
- **Feeding the soil** as it breaks down.

An annual vegetable bed is replanted every year, so a mulch that disappears into the soil within
a season is a feature. A shrub border is not, so the right answer there is different.

## Is straw or wood chips better for a vegetable garden?

Straw, clearly, for an annual bed. Wood chips are better for paths, fruit bushes and perennials.

The difference comes down to how fast each breaks down:

|                         | Straw                         | Wood chips                            |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| C:N ratio               | 75:1 (range 48–150)           | 400:1 (range 200–1,300)               |
| Breaks down in          | 1 season                      | 2–4 years                             |
| Effect on an annual bed | Gone by spring, soil improved | Still there, in the way of replanting |

Ratios from the [Cornell Waste Management Institute](https://cwmi.css.cornell.edu/chapter3.pdf).
Note the enormous range on wood chips — hardwood runs 450–800 and softwood 200–1,300, so the
species matters more than the chip size.

**Do wood chips steal nitrogen?** At the interface, yes. Microbes decomposing a 400:1 material
need far more nitrogen than the wood supplies, so they take it from the surrounding soil. Left
_on the surface_ the effect is confined to the top inch and is not a practical problem for
established plants. Dug _into_ the soil, it is a real problem and will stunt a crop for a season.
[Greens and browns in compost](/blog/greens-vs-browns-in-compost/) explains the mechanism.

## Straw, in detail

**Good at:** weed suppression, moisture retention, easy to pull aside for planting, breaks down
within a season and improves the soil as it goes.

**Costs you:** it can contain grain seed, so the wrong bale seeds your bed with cereal. It is
light and blows about until it settles. And it is worth asking whether the field was treated with
a persistent herbicide — some survive composting and damage vegetables years later.

Apply three to four inches, because it settles a lot.

## Shredded leaves

**Good at:** everything straw is good at, and free. Leaf mould is one of the best soil
conditioners there is, and a leaf mulch becomes that in place. At 60:1 (range 30–80) they break
down usefully fast.

**Costs you:** whole leaves mat into layers that shed water, so they need shredding — a mower run
over a pile does it. And you can collect them once a year.

If you have deciduous trees anywhere nearby, this is the best value mulch available.

## Grass clippings

**Good at:** free, high in nitrogen at 20:1, breaks down fast.

**Costs you:** applied thick and fresh, clippings mat into a slimy, airless layer that smells.
Apply in thin layers, dried for a day first, and never more than an inch at a time. Avoid
clippings from a lawn treated with weedkiller.

## What mulch should you not use in a vegetable garden

**Fresh wood chips dug in.** On the surface they are merely unsuitable; incorporated they tie up
nitrogen for a season.

**Hay instead of straw.** Hay is the whole plant cut for fodder, seed heads included. It will
seed your bed with grass.

**Dyed or rubber mulch.** Decorative products for borders. Rubber does not break down, adds
nothing, and has no place around food crops.

**Anything from a herbicide-treated field.** Straw, hay and manure can all carry persistent
herbicides. If you cannot establish the provenance, test a sample by growing beans in a pot of
it before spreading it on a bed.

**Landscape fabric on a bed.** It stops weeds for a year or two, then weeds root in the mulch on
top of it and it becomes impossible to remove. On paths it is fine.

**Thick mulch against stems.** Whatever the material, keep it an inch or two clear of stems.
Mulch piled against a stem holds moisture there and invites rot.

## How thick, and when

Three to four inches for straw and leaves, one to two for compost, one inch at a time for grass
clippings. Thinner than that and weeds come through; much thicker and water struggles to reach
the soil.

Timing matters more than people expect. **Mulch after the soil has warmed**, not before. A mulch
laid on cold spring soil keeps it cold, which delays germination and transplanting for everything
— and for warm-season crops that is the opposite of what you want. Our
[cucumber](/crops/cucumber/) and [zucchini](/crops/zucchini/) guides both want soil at about 70°F
(21°C) before sowing; mulching early makes that later.

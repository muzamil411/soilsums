Starting seeds indoors gives you varieties nobody sells as plants, and a head start of several
weeks on a short season. It also goes wrong in four predictable ways. Here is the timeline, and
the four things that actually matter.

## Work backwards from your last frost date

Every indoor sowing date is expressed as weeks before your last spring frost. Find that date
first — there is a separate guide on where to look it up — and then count back.

| Weeks before last frost | Sow indoors                                                    |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 10                      | Onions from seed, leeks                                        |
| 8                       | Peppers, aubergines, parsley                                   |
| 6                       | Tomatoes, broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale, lettuce, basil |
| 5                       | Spinach                                                        |
| 3                       | Cucumbers, squash, zucchini, melons                            |
| Not at all              | Carrots, radishes, beets, beans, peas, corn, dill, cilantro    |

That last row matters. Several crops are worse for being started indoors: root crops because the
taproot is the crop and disturbing it forks the root, and beans and corn because they grow fast
enough that a transplant gains nothing and risks a check.

The [planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) will turn your frost date into
the actual calendar dates for whichever crops you are growing, and give you the transplant dates
to go with them.

## Do not start early

This deserves its own heading because it is the mistake almost everyone makes in their first
year. The offsets above are not conservative. A tomato sown ten weeks out instead of six spends
a month outgrowing its pot, and arrives at planting day tall, pale and root-bound. A plant sown
on time overtakes it within a fortnight of going out.

If you are impatient in February, sow something that genuinely wants sowing in February —
onions, or a tray of salad leaves to eat indoors.

## The four things that matter

**Light, more than anything.** This is the difference between stocky seedlings and stretched
ones. A south-facing windowsill in early spring provides far less light than it appears to, and
seedlings respond by reaching. A basic shop light or LED bar held two to three inches above the
plants, raised as they grow, for fourteen to sixteen hours a day, transforms results. More than
any other piece of equipment, this is the one worth buying.

**Warmth for germination, then less.** Warm-season seeds germinate much faster with gentle bottom
heat — peppers in particular, which can take three weeks in a cool room. Once they are up,
reduce the heat. Warm plus dim is exactly the combination that produces leggy seedlings.

**A fine, free-draining mix.** Use a seed or potting mix rather than garden soil, which compacts
into an airless brick in a small container. Keep it damp, not wet: seedlings are more often lost
to damping off in soggy mix than to drought.

**Air movement.** A small fan on low for a few hours a day strengthens stems and reduces fungal
problems noticeably. Brushing a hand across the tray does something similar for free.

## Sowing, in practice

Fill containers with damp mix and firm it lightly. Sow at the depth on the packet — some seeds,
lettuce among them, need light and should sit on the surface. Cover, label, and keep the surface
damp until emergence, covering the tray if it dries out fast.

As soon as the first seedlings appear, get them under strong light and take them off any heat.

## Potting on

When a seedling has its first true leaves — the ones after the initial pair — move it into a
larger container if it was sown in a small cell. Handle it by a leaf, never the stem: a crushed
stem does not recover, a lost leaf does.

Tomatoes can be planted deeper each time they are moved, because they root from buried stem. Most
other crops should go in at the same depth.

## Hardening off

A plant raised indoors has no defence against wind, direct sun or cold nights. Moving it straight
from a windowsill to a bed scorches and checks it.

Over seven to ten days before planting out, put the plants outside in a sheltered spot for an
hour or two on the first day and increase from there, bringing them in at night to start with.
By the end they should be out all day and all night. It is tedious and it is the step that most
determines whether the work of the previous six weeks survives.

## Planting out

Wait for the transplant dates rather than the frost date itself. Cool-season crops can go out one
to three weeks before the frost date; tomatoes want a week after; peppers, cucumbers and squash
want two weeks after and warm soil.

Plant in the evening or on a dull day rather than in midday sun, water in well, and have fleece
handy for the first fortnight.

## The short version

Find your frost date, count back the number of weeks for each crop, and resist starting early.
Buy a light before you buy anything else. Keep the mix damp rather than wet, give the seedlings
some air movement, and harden them off properly. Do not bother starting carrots, beans, peas or
corn indoors at all.

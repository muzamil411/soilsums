The harvest is in the basket, and now the question is how to store onions so they are still firm in February instead of sprouting in the pantry by December. Storage is where most homegrown onions are lost: the digging went fine, the curing was skipped, and a warm, damp shelf did the rest. The good news is that the rules are simple, and university extension services agree on them. This guide to how to store onions follows the order the sources recommend: harvest at the right moment, cure, sort, set up the spot, pick a container, and check them through the winter.

If you have not dug them yet, our [onion crop page](/crops/onion/) covers growing them from seed, sets, or transplants, including the day-length choice that decides whether a variety stores at all — the first decision in how to store onions. What follows assumes the bulbs are already out of the ground — or about to be.

## How to store onions: the four conditions

Every storage decision comes back to four conditions, and the numbers below are the ones extension services publish — not folk rules:

- **Temperature: 32 to 40°F.** Iowa State University Extension says storage temperatures for onions should be 32 to 40°F, and the University of Minnesota Extension gives the same cold-and-dry band. That range is the whole game: cold enough to hold sprouting back, and just above freezing so the bulbs are not damaged.
- **Humidity: dry, 65 to 70 percent.** Iowa State puts the storage target at 65 to 70 percent relative humidity, and the University of California, Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center agrees at 65 to 70 percent with adequate air circulation. Onions are the opposite of potatoes here — damp air is what rots them.
- **Darkness: total.** Light after curing causes greening of the outer scales, per UC Davis, and light also encourages sprouting. The National Onion Association says to keep onions out of direct sunlight and other heat sources.
- **Airflow: steady.** The storage container must allow air to circulate through the onions, per Iowa State — and the National Onion Association says not to wrap onions in plastic or store them in plastic bags, because a lack of air circulation will reduce shelf life.

### What the four conditions buy you

Get all four right and pungent storage onions last five to eight months, per Iowa State. Those four numbers are the whole answer to how to store onions; everything else in this guide is how to hit them. Miss on temperature alone and the same bulbs last far less: the University of Minnesota Extension warns that shelf life shortens by up to 25 percent for every 10 degrees Fahrenheit the store runs above the ideal 32°F. That is the arithmetic behind how to store onions — the colder the store, the longer the harvest lasts, and a warm pantry is a short store.

### The honest temperature split: 32–40°F or 45–55°F?

You will see two temperature ranges quoted for onions, and they are not a contradiction — they describe two different situations. The 32 to 40°F range from Iowa State, Minnesota, and UC Davis is for long-term storage of a harvest, where the goal is months. The National Onion Association gives 45 to 55°F for home and retail handling, where the goal is weeks and the onions are moving through a kitchen. A pantry at 50°F is fine for the bag you bought this week; it is a poor store for the harvest you want in March, because at 50°F the bulbs are already 18 degrees above ideal, and Minnesota's rule says that costs roughly a third to a half of the storage life. Use the coldest dry spot you have for the bulk of the harvest, and keep a small working supply where you cook. That split — cold store for the harvest, pantry for the week — is the practical version of how to store onions in a real house.

## When to harvest onions for storage

Storage starts before the bulbs leave the ground. The University of Minnesota Extension says to harvest onions when about half the tops are falling over and dry, and the University of California, Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center puts the same moment at 10 to 20 percent of tops fallen over — the difference is only how early in the flop you catch them. Either way, the signal is the tops, not the calendar: when the necks soften and the green falls, the bulb has finished growing and is starting to seal itself off. Our [onion crop page](/crops/onion/) gives the same rule, and adds the one thing not to do: do not bend the tops over yourself — the old advice to do so shortens storage life.

Stop watering once the bulbs have reached full size and the tops begin to fall, per Minnesota — wet soil at that stage causes rot in storage, and the same warning sits on the crop page. Then lift on a dry day. Minnesota says to undercut and lift the bulbs with a spading fork, and to harvest on dry, cool days to limit disease after harvest. A fork lifted from below does less damage than a spade driven through the row, and a bruised bulb is a bulb that rots first — the National Onion Association says to handle onions with care and not to drop them, because bruising causes internal decay. Handle the harvest like eggs, not potatoes, and the storage life starts longer. That gentle lift is the first step in how to store onions well, and it happens before a single bulb is cured.

### Leave them in the field for a few days — if the weather allows

If the weather will be dry and warm, Minnesota says you can leave the onions on the ground for several days, or bring them indoors to cure. UC Davis describes the same field curing and puts a number on it: field curing when temperatures are at least 24°C (75°F), or 12 hours at 30 to 45°C (86 to 113°F) for forced air-curing. A few dry days on the soil surface starts the drying for free. But if rain is coming, bring them in — wet bulbs cure slowly and rot fast, and a bulb that gets rained on after lifting needs the full indoor cure. The field is a convenience, not a requirement, and the indoor cure below is the method that works in any weather. That weather call is the harvest-day decision inside how to store onions, and it is the only part of the method that depends on the forecast.

## Curing: the step that decides everything

Curing is drying the outside of the bulb so the neck seals and the skins turn papery, and it is the step that decides whether the harvest keeps for months or rots in weeks. Iowa State University Extension says to dry or cure the onions in a warm, dry, well-ventilated location, such as a shed or garage, spread out in a single layer on a clean, dry surface, for two to three weeks, until the tops and necks are thoroughly dry and the outer bulb scales begin to rustle. The University of Minnesota Extension gives the same method with a warmer range: keep the onions in a warm (75°F to 90°F), well-ventilated area for two to four weeks, until the outer bulb scales are dry and the neck is tight — and warns that poor curing will result in decay during storage. The University of Minnesota Extension's home-garden storage page agrees on the duration, saying to cure at room temperature for two to four weeks before storage.

The test for "done" is the neck. A cured onion has a neck that is completely dry to the touch and not slippery, per UC Davis, and a field-dry bulb has typically lost 5 to 8 percent of its weight since harvest — the water has left, and the bulb is sealed. Squeeze the neck: if it feels damp or gives under your thumb, it needs more time. Rustle the skins: if they crackle like paper, the bulb is ready. That neck test is the curing check inside how to store onions, and it is more reliable than counting days, because a damp autumn cures slower than a dry one.

### How to lay them out

Spread the bulbs in a single layer, never piled, in a warm, dry, well-ventilated place — a shed, a garage, a covered porch, or a spare room with a fan. The single layer matters because a pile traps moisture in the middle, and the bulbs at the bottom cure last and rot first. Keep them out of direct sun while they cure: sun scorches the outer scales, and UC Davis lists sunscald among the defects that shorten storage life. Good airflow matters as much as warmth — a still, humid corner cures nothing, while a breezy shed at the same temperature dries the necks in days. Do not wash the bulbs before or during curing: washing adds the very moisture curing exists to remove. Brush the loose soil off by hand once the bulbs are dry, and leave the rest. That single-layer, breezy layout is the curing setup inside how to store onions, and it costs nothing but floor space.

### What curing actually prevents

Curing is not cosmetic. UC Davis says that proper drying and curing essentially prevents Botrytis neck rot, the watery decay that starts at the neck and moves down through the whole bulb — and that the same proper drying prevents the storage disorders that follow condensation on the bulbs. A damp neck is an open door: fungi enter through the neck, and a bulb that looked perfect at harvest turns to watery rot in the bag by November. The two to four weeks in the shed is the cheapest insurance in how to store onions, and it is the step most often skipped by gardeners in a hurry to put the harvest away. An onion cured properly is sealed; an onion bagged damp is an onion already rotting.

## Sorting and trimming after curing

Once the necks are dry and the scales rustle, the harvest gets sorted before it goes into storage. Iowa State University Extension says to cut off the tops about one inch above the bulbs as the onions are topped, and to discard any that show signs of decay as you go. Use the thick-necked bulbs as soon as possible, the same source says, because they do not store well — a thick neck never sealed properly, and it is the first to rot. A bulb with a soft spot, a cut, or a bruise goes in the kitchen pile, not the storage bag: the University of Minnesota Extension says to harvest only vegetables of high quality, because rotting produce does not keep for very long and can spread disease to other stored vegetables. One rotting bulb in a mesh bag can take its neighbours with it, so the sorting step is also a quarantine step. That sort — eat the damaged and thick-necked first, store only the sound — is the triage inside how to store onions, and it is what keeps a small problem from becoming a lost bag.

### Braiding: the alternative to trimming

If you grew a variety with long, flexible tops, you can skip the scissors. Iowa State says an alternate preparation method is to leave the onion tops untrimmed and braid the dry foliage together, and Minnesota agrees: when the bulbs are properly dry, you can braid the onions or cut the tops off. A braid hangs from a rafter or ceiling hook, which keeps the bulbs in moving air and off any damp surface, and it makes the weekly check easy — a soft bulb in a braid is visible at a glance. Braiding only works with tops that dried intact, so it suits onions cured with their foliage on; if the tops snapped off during lifting, trim and bag them instead. Either way the rule is the same: the tops are either cut to an inch or braided whole, and nothing in between. That braid-or-trim choice is the finishing step inside how to store onions, and the braid is the prettier one if your tops survived the harvest.

## Containers: what to put them in

The container matters because onions need moving air around every bulb. Iowa State University Extension says to place the cured onions in a mesh bag, an old nylon stocking, a wire basket, or a crate, and that the storage container must allow air to circulate through the onions. The National Onion Association adds the warning from the other side: do not wrap onions in plastic or store them in plastic bags, because a lack of air circulation will reduce shelf life. A mesh onion sack from the garden centre, a laundry mesh bag, a pair of old tights with a knot between each bulb, or a slatted wooden crate all work, because all of them breathe. A sealed plastic bag, a lidded tub, or a deep bucket does not, because it traps the moisture the bulbs give off and the rot follows. Hang braided onions from a rafter or ceiling, per Iowa State, and hang mesh bags from a hook rather than setting them on a damp floor. Shallow is better than deep: a single layer in a wide crate cures and keeps better than a deep sack, because the bulbs in the middle of a deep pile get the least air. That breathable-container rule is the packing rule inside how to store onions, and it is the difference between a bag that keeps and a bag that sweats.

## Where to store them: the spots that work

The ideal store is a basement, cellar, or garage, per Iowa State — somewhere that holds 32 to 40°F with dry air and steady ventilation. A cool basement corner is the classic answer, and a root cellar is better still. The University of Minnesota Extension adds that home refrigerators, at 40°F and 50 to 60 percent relative humidity, are fine for long-term storage of garlic and onions — so a spare fridge, or a crisper drawer for a small harvest, is a legitimate store. An unheated garage works too, with one condition from Iowa State: if storing the onions in an unheated garage, move them to an alternate storage site before temperatures drop below 32°F, because a frozen bulb is a ruined bulb — UC Davis lists freezing injury as a disorder, with soft, water-soaked scales that rapidly decay. A thermometer in the store is worth more than a guess: check the spot where the bulbs actually sit, not the room's thermostat, because a garage floor in January can be ten degrees colder than the air at head height. That coldest-dry-spot rule is the location rule inside how to store onions, and the thermometer is what makes it real.

### Spots that do not work

A warm kitchen pantry is a short store, not a winter store: at 65 to 70°F the bulbs are far above the 40°F sprouting line, and Minnesota says they will start to sprout above 40°F. A damp basement corner is worse than a warm one, because damp air at any temperature grows mold — the National Onion Association says the bulbs should feel firm and dry, be free of gray or black mold, and show no visible sprouting. A sunny windowsill is the worst of all: light plus warmth plus still air is the fastest route to sprouting and greening. And a sealed container anywhere is a trap, for the reasons above. A warm pantry can still work for the onions you will cook this week — just do not expect it to answer how to store onions for the winter.

## How long they keep, by type

Not all onions keep equally, and the variety you grew decides the answer. The University of California, Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center gives the two numbers side by side: mild onions typically keep half a month to one month at 0°C (32°F), while pungent onions keep up to six to nine months at 0°C, depending on the cultivar. Iowa State University Extension gives the home-garden version of the same answer: when stored well, the average storage life for onions is five to eight months. The University of Minnesota Extension's home-garden page gives a more conservative four months, which assumes a warmer home store rather than ideal cold storage. Put together, the honest range for how to store onions is: pungent, thick-skinned storage onions, five to eight months at 32 to 40°F; sweet and mild onions, two to four weeks; and a warm pantry cuts any of those figures by a quarter or more per 10 degrees above ideal, per Minnesota's rule. The variety is the storage life, more than any trick after harvest — which is why the seed catalogue choice in spring matters as much as the curing in August.

### Which varieties keep, and which do not

Pungent yellow and red storage onions — the thick-skinned, high-sulfur types — are the keepers, and UC Davis ties the long life to the cultivar: up to six to nine months, depending on the cultivar. Sweet and mild onions, including Vidalia and Walla Walla types, have thin skins and high water content, and UC Davis gives them half a month to one month. Minnesota explains the underlying difference: storage onions grown in the north are generally long-day types that require 14 or more hours of daylight to form bulbs, while sweet or mild onions are short-day onions. If you are buying seed for next year and want onions in February, choose a named storage variety and a long-day type for your latitude; if you grew sweet onions, plan to eat them within weeks and do not blame the storage method for a short life. That variety-first rule is the planning lesson inside how to store onions, and it is decided at planting time, not at harvest.

## A worked example: a 25-pound harvest, August to March

A worked example makes the calendar concrete. Suppose you lift 25 pounds of a pungent yellow storage variety on August 1, when about half the tops have fallen over and dried — the harvest signal the University of Minnesota Extension gives. You stop watering a week before, lift on a dry day with a spading fork, and lay the bulbs in a single layer in the garage, where it runs warm and breezy. The curing takes three weeks, per Iowa State University Extension's two-to-three-week range, so by August 22 the necks are dry and the scales rustle. You trim the tops to one inch above the bulbs, discard two bulbs with soft spots, and set aside three thick-necked bulbs for the kitchen — Iowa State says thick-necked bulbs do not store well and should be used as soon as possible. The rest, about 20 pounds, goes into two mesh bags hung from a hook in the basement, where a thermometer reads 35°F. That is inside Iowa State's 32 to 40°F band, and the basement air is dry. At five to eight months of storage life, per Iowa State, those bags carry you from late August into January at the short end and into March or April at the long end — and the weekly check, a squeeze of a few bulbs through the mesh, tells you which end you are on. That is the whole method for how to store onions from a real harvest: three weeks of curing, one afternoon of sorting, and a cold, dry hook in the basement.

### The same method works for garlic

Garlic follows the same steps, and the same Iowa State page gives its numbers: dry garlic in a warm, dry, well-ventilated location, cut off the dry foliage one inch above the bulbs when the tops have dried, trim off the roots and brush off any loose soil, and store in a mesh bag or open crate at 32 to 40°F and 65 to 70 percent relative humidity — with an average storage life of six to seven months. If you planted garlic this autumn, our [garlic planting guide](/blog/when-to-plant-garlic/) covers the timing, and the curing and storing that follows uses exactly the method above. One difference is worth noting: garlic and onions can share a store, since they want the same cold, dry air — but keep them in separate bags, so a problem in one does not reach the other. That shared-store rule is the garlic footnote inside how to store onions, and it is the reason the two crops are often cured side by side in the same shed.

## What not to store with onions

Onions are bad neighbours in storage — one of the few rules in how to store onions that has no exceptions: keep them away from anything that releases moisture or ethylene. The National Onion Association says not to store onions with potatoes or other produce items that release moisture, and Iowa State University Extension's potato section warns not to store potatoes with apples or other fruit, because ripening fruit gives off ethylene gas, which promotes sprouting. The University of California, Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center adds that ethylene may encourage sprouting and the growth of decay-causing fungi in onions, and that onions are both a source of storage odour for other commodities, such as apples, celery, and pears, and an absorber of odours from commodities such as apples. So the separation is three-way: onions away from potatoes, because potatoes release moisture that rots onions; onions away from apples and ripening fruit, because ethylene pushes them to sprout; and onions away from anything that picks up their smell. A separate shelf in the same cool room is enough — the point is separate containers and air between them, not a separate building. That separation rule is the neighbour rule inside how to store onions, and it is the one most often broken in a crowded pantry.

## Freezing onions: the backup plan

If the store is warm, or the harvest is bigger than the storage space, freezing is the backup — and it is the one part of how to store onions where the extension services give exact minutes, not ranges. Diced onions are the easy case: peel, trim, and clean thoroughly, dice into about 1/4-inch pieces, and no blanching is required — pack dry into freezer bags or containers, or freeze the pieces separately on a tray first, and use them within a few months. The National Center for Home Food Preservation says freezing is usually not recommended for preserving whole onions, but if you want to freeze whole bulbs, water-blanch them for 3 minutes for smaller onions to 7 minutes for larger ones, until the centre is completely heated, then cool promptly, drain, and package — and use them for cooking only. Onion rings get a 10 to 15 second blanch, a flour, milk, and cornmeal coating, and a fry in 375°F oil straight from frozen. Frozen onions are a cooking ingredient, not a fresh one — they go into soups, stews, and sautés, where the softened texture does not matter. That freezing option is the overflow valve inside how to store onions: when the mesh bags are full and the harvest keeps coming, dice the rest and freeze them.

## Troubleshooting stored onions

Most storage problems in how to store onions have one of three causes: the bulbs went in damp, the store is too warm, or the air is still. The fixes are small, but they only work if the cause is named correctly.

### Sprouting in the bag

A green shoot from the centre of the bulb means the store is too warm or too bright. The University of Minnesota Extension says onions start to sprout if kept above 40°F, and UC Davis says storage between 41 and 75°F favors sprouting. Move the bag to the coldest dry spot you have, keep it dark, and keep ripening fruit away — ethylene encourages sprouting, per UC Davis. Use the sprouted bulbs first: sprouting is the bulb spending its stored energy on growth, so a sprouted bulb softens and loses flavour the longer it sits. A bag that sprouts in December was stored too warm from the start, and the fix is a colder store next year, not a trick this year. That colder-and-darker move is the sprouting fix inside how to store onions, and it is the most common fix of all.

### Rotting from the neck down

A bulb that turns soft and watery at the neck, with the decay moving down through the bulb, is neck rot — and the cause is almost always curing that was cut short. UC Davis says that proper drying and curing essentially prevents Botrytis neck rot, the watery decay that starts at the neck area and moves downward through the entire bulb. If a bulb is already rotting, take it out of the bag before the rot spreads to its neighbours, and check the necks on the rest: they should be completely dry, not slippery, per UC Davis. The cure for next year is the full two to four weeks in a warm, dry, ventilated spot, until the necks are thoroughly dry and the scales rustle, per Iowa State. A bulb with a damp neck at bagging time was never going to keep, which is why the neck test matters more than the calendar. That neck check is the rot fix inside how to store onions, and it is the first thing to check when a bag goes wrong.

### Mold on the bulbs

Gray or black mold on the skins, or a black discoloration and shrivelling at the neck, means moisture and still air. The National Onion Association says stored onions should be free of gray or black mold, and UC Davis names black mold — the fungus Aspergillus niger — as a disorder often associated with bruising, which leads to bacterial soft rot. Check the container first: a sealed plastic bag is the usual cause, and the National Onion Association says not to store onions in plastic bags, because the lack of air circulation reduces shelf life. Move the sound bulbs to a mesh bag or crate with air moving through it, discard any bulb with mold on it, and handle the rest gently — bruising is the entry point, and the National Onion Association says not to drop onions, because bruising causes internal decay. That airflow check is the mold fix inside how to store onions, and it is the same check that prevents rot.

### Soft, water-soaked scales

A bulb that feels soft and water-soaked, with scales that look translucent, has been frozen or chilled badly. UC Davis lists freezing injury as a disorder — soft, water-soaked scales that rapidly decay from subsequent microbial growth — and says translucent scales are prevented by prompt cold storage following curing, with a three-to-four-week delay in cold storage increasing the risk significantly. If the store dropped below 32°F, move the bulbs somewhere that holds above freezing and use the soft ones first. A bulb that froze in an unheated garage is the reason Iowa State says to move onions out of an unheated garage before temperatures drop below 32°F. That above-freezing rule is the cold-damage fix inside how to store onions, and a thermometer in the store is what catches it before the bulbs do.

### Greening on the outer scales

Green colour on the outer scales means light reached the bulbs after curing. UC Davis says exposure to light following curing causes green coloration of the outer scales, and the National Onion Association says to keep onions out of direct sunlight and other heat sources. Move the bag into darkness — a covered crate, a dark corner, a cupboard — and the greening stops getting worse, though the green scales will not turn back. Greening is a quality problem, not a safety one in onions the way it is in potatoes, but it is a sign the store is too bright, and a bright store is usually a warm one too. That darkness check is the greening fix inside how to store onions, and it is the cheapest fix of all.

## The short version

How to store onions comes down to three decisions made at harvest: which bulbs are sound enough to store, how long they cure, and how cold and dry the store is. Lift when about half the tops have fallen over, cure in a single layer in a warm, dry, well-ventilated spot for two to four weeks until the necks are dry and the scales rustle, trim the tops to an inch, and store the sound bulbs in a mesh bag or crate at 32 to 40°F and 65 to 70 percent humidity, in the dark, away from potatoes and fruit. Pungent storage onions kept that way last five to eight months; sweet onions last weeks. Check the bag every few weeks, use the thick-necked, damaged, and sprouted bulbs first, and dice and freeze whatever will not fit. If you are putting the rest of the garden to bed for winter, our [winterizing guide](/blog/how-to-winterize-a-garden/) covers the November work that goes with it — and if the garlic went in this autumn, our [garlic planting guide](/blog/when-to-plant-garlic/) has the timing. That is the whole method — cure, sort, keep cold and dry — and that simplicity is why how to store onions is a method, not a gadget.

- Iowa State University Extension — [Storing Winter Storage Vegetables](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/storing-winter-storage-vegetables) (verified 2026-11-12; updated July 14, 2026; cure onions in a warm, dry, well-ventilated location such as a shed or garage, spread in a single layer on a clean, dry surface, for two to three weeks until the tops and necks are thoroughly dry and the outer bulb scales begin to rustle; after curing cut off the tops about one inch above the bulbs, discard any that show signs of decay, use thick-necked bulbs as soon as possible as they do not store well, or leave the tops untrimmed and braid the dry foliage together; place cured onions in a mesh bag, old nylon stocking, wire basket, or crate, the container must allow air to circulate; store in a cool, moderately dry location at 32 to 40°F and 65 to 70 percent relative humidity, possible locations include a basement, cellar, or garage; hang braided onions from a rafter or ceiling; if storing in an unheated garage move the onions to an alternate storage site before temperatures drop below 32°F; average storage life for onions stored well is five to eight months; garlic: dry in a warm, dry, well-ventilated location, cut off the dry foliage one inch above the bulbs, trim off the roots and brush off loose soil, store in a mesh bag or open crate at 32 to 40°F and 65 to 70 percent relative humidity, average storage life six to seven months; potatoes: do not store potatoes with apples or other fruit, ripening fruit gives off ethylene gas which promotes sprouting)
- University of Minnesota Extension — [Growing onions in home gardens](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/growing-onions) (verified 2026-11-12; reviewed 2024; harvest onions when about half the tops are falling over and dry; undercut and lift bulbs with a spading fork; leave onions on the ground for several days if the weather will be dry and warm, or bring them indoors to cure; curing is essential if you plan to store onions; keep the onions in a warm (75°F to 90°F), well-ventilated area for two to four weeks, until outer bulb scales are dry and the neck is tight; poor curing will result in decay during storage; when properly dry, braid the onions or cut the tops off; store onions in a cool, dry area, do not let them freeze, they will start to sprout if kept above 40°F; stop watering when bulbs have reached full size and the tops begin to fall; storage onions grown in Minnesota generally are long-day types that require 14 or more hours of daylight to form bulbs; sweet or mild onions are short-day onions; too much nitrogen can result in late maturity, large necks that are difficult to cure, soft bulbs, green flesh and poor storage quality; harvest onions on dry, cool days to limit diseases post-harvest)
- University of Minnesota Extension — [Harvesting and storing home garden vegetables](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/harvesting-and-storing-home-garden-vegetables) (verified 2026-11-12; reviewed 2026; cold and dry storage is 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit and 65 percent relative humidity; for cold conditions 32 degrees Fahrenheit is the ideal temperature; shelf life shortens by up to 25 percent for every 10 degrees Fahrenheit increase in temperature above ideal; home refrigerators are generally cold and dry (40 degrees Fahrenheit and 50 to 60 percent relative humidity), fine for long-term storage of garlic and onions; onions: harvest when the necks are tight and the scales are dry, store in cold and dry conditions, cure at room temperature for two to four weeks before storage, do not freeze, expected shelf-life four months; harvest only vegetables of high quality, rotting produce does not keep for very long and can spread disease to other stored vegetables)
- University of California, Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center — [Onions, Dry](https://postharvest.ucdavis.edu/ar/node/8121) (verified 2026-11-12; maturity indicated when approximately 10 to 20 percent of tops have fallen over; field-dry maturity indicated when bulb neck is completely dry to the touch and not slippery, typically reached at 5 to 8 percent weight loss following harvest; field curing when temperatures are at least 24°C (75°F) or exposure for 12 hours to 30 to 45°C (86 to 113°F) for forced air-curing; storage: mild onions typically 0.5 to 1 month at 0°C (32°F), pungent onions typically up to 6 to 9 months at 0°C (32°F) depending on the cultivar; optimum storage relative humidity 65 to 70 percent with adequate air circulation; storage between 5 to 25°C (41 to 75°F) favors sprouting and is not recommended for extended periods; ethylene may encourage sprouting and growth of decay-causing fungi; Botrytis neck rot: watery decay initiates at neck area and moves downward through entire bulb, proper drying and curing of onion essentially prevents this storage disorder; black mold: black discoloration and shriveling at neck and on outer scales caused by Aspergillus niger, often associated with bruising, leads to bacterial soft rot; freezing injury: soft water-soaked scales rapidly decay; translucent scales prevented by prompt cold storage following curing, a 3 to 4 week delay in cold storage increases risk significantly; greening: exposure to light following curing causes green coloration of outer scales; onions are both storage-odor sources for other commodities such as apples, celery and pears, and storage-odor absorbers from commodities such as apples)
- National Onion Association — [Storage and Handling](https://www.onions-usa.org/all-about-onions/storage-and-handling/) (verified 2026-11-12; store onions in a cool, dry, well-ventilated area; maintain storage temperature of 45 to 55°F; do not wrap onions in plastic or store in plastic bags, a lack of air circulation will reduce shelf life; do not store onions with potatoes or other produce items that release moisture; keep onions out of direct sunlight and other heat sources; onions should feel firm and dry, be free of gray or black mold, and should not have any visible sprouting; always handle onions with care, do not drop onions as this often causes bruising and internal decay; cut onions will keep for several days if sealed in plastic bags or containers and refrigerated)
- National Center for Home Food Preservation — [Freezing Onions](http://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/vegetable/freezing-onions/) (verified 2026-11-12; adapted from So Easy to Preserve, 6th edition, 2014, Bulletin 989, Cooperative Extension Service, University of Georgia; bulb onions store well in a cool, dry place, freezing is usually not recommended for preserving whole onions; diced onions: peel, trim and clean thoroughly, dice about 1/4-inch pieces, no blanching is required, best used within a few months; whole bulb onions: water blanch for 3 minutes for smaller onions to 7 minutes for larger onions, or until center is completely heated, cool promptly, drain well, and package, suitable for cooking only; onion rings: water blanch for 10 to 15 seconds, cool promptly, drain and coat with flour, dip in milk, coat with a mixture of equal parts cornmeal and pancake mix, fry frozen rings in 375°F oil until golden brown)

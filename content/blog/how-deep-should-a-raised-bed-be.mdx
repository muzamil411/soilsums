Raised bed depth gets discussed as though there were a right answer. There is not: there is a
right answer for what you are growing and what the bed is standing on. Those two facts settle
it almost completely.

## What is underneath matters most

Ask this first, because it changes everything else.

**If the bed sits on open soil**, the depth of the frame is not the depth of the root zone.
Roots go through the bottom of the bed and carry on into the ground beneath. A six-inch bed on
decent soil behaves like a much deeper one, and a twelve-inch bed on good ground will grow
almost anything.

**If the bed sits on a patio, a deck, gravel or a liner**, the frame is the entire root zone.
Now depth is a hard limit rather than a starting point, and you should treat the bed as a very
large container: twelve inches minimum for most vegetables, more for root crops.

If the ground under the bed is compacted — an old path, a building plot — loosening it with a
fork before you fill the bed is worth an hour of work. It turns a bed with a floor into a bed
without one.

## Depth by crop

Rough root-zone requirements, assuming the bed is the whole root zone:

| Depth    | What it suits                                                          |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 6 in     | Lettuce, spinach, radish, most herbs, annual flowers                   |
| 8 in     | Bush beans, chard, onions, garlic, compact peppers                     |
| 10–12 in | Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, brassicas, beets                         |
| 12–18 in | Carrots, parsnips, potatoes, and anything you want to grow really well |

Twelve inches is the figure to build to if you are choosing once and want to stop thinking
about it. It grows essentially everything on the list above, it holds enough water to be
forgiving, and it is a comfortable height to work at from a kneeler.

## Depth also changes how often you water

This is the practical argument for going deeper, and it gets less attention than it deserves.

A deeper bed holds a bigger reservoir. On a hot day, the same amount of water evaporates from
the surface of a six-inch bed and a twelve-inch bed — but it is a much larger fraction of what
the shallow bed was holding. Shallow beds need watering more often, and they punish a forgotten
weekend more severely.

If you garden somewhere hot or dry, or you travel in summer, depth buys you slack.

## The cost of depth

Depth is the expensive dimension, because volume scales with it directly. Going from six to
twelve inches on a four by eight foot bed doubles the fill from 16 cubic feet to 32 — from
about 11 bags to 22 at 1.5 cubic feet each.

Two ways to manage that:

**Part-fill deep beds.** For anything over about 18 inches, the bottom third can be coarse
woody material, upturned turf or native soil, with bought mix in the top twelve inches where
the roots mostly are. Expect that lower layer to settle as it breaks down, and plan to top up.

**Build fewer, deeper beds rather than more shallow ones.** Two twelve-inch beds usually grow
more than three six-inch beds of the same total area, and cost about the same to fill.

Run the numbers before you buy timber, not after. The
[raised bed soil calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) will show what each depth costs
you in bags.

## Height for the gardener, not just the plants

Depth and height are not the same thing. A bed can be 24 inches tall with 12 inches of soil in
it, sitting on a 12-inch void or on coarse fill.

If bending is a problem, build up. A bed at 24 to 30 inches can be worked from a standing
position or a seat, and that is often the difference between a garden that gets tended and one
that does not. Just remember that a tall bed only needs to be _filled_ to a sensible root depth,
which keeps the cost reasonable.

## Sides, and what they do to your usable space

Whatever depth you choose, measure the **inside** of the frame when working out volume and
spacing. Two-inch lumber takes four inches off each dimension. On a four by eight foot bed that
is nearly two cubic feet of soil, and a full row of carrots.

## The short version

If the bed is on open soil, six to eight inches will do for most things and twelve is
comfortable. If it is on a hard surface, twelve is the minimum and more is better. Go deeper if
you want carrots, or if you want to water less often. Build the sides as tall as your back
prefers, and fill only as deep as the roots need.

How deep a raised bed should be for vegetables depends on what is underneath it. On open soil,
**6 to 8 inches is enough for most crops and 12 inches is comfortable**, because roots carry on
into the ground below. On a patio, deck or liner the frame is the whole root zone, so **12 inches
is the minimum** and 18 is better for root crops.

## What is underneath matters most

Ask this first, because it changes everything else.

**If the bed sits on open soil**, the depth of the frame is not the depth of the root zone. Roots
go through the bottom of the bed and carry on into the ground beneath. A six-inch bed on decent
soil behaves like a much deeper one, and a twelve-inch bed on good ground will grow almost
anything.

**If the bed sits on a patio, deck, gravel or a liner**, the frame is the entire root zone. Depth
becomes a hard limit rather than a starting point, and you should treat the bed as a very large
container.

If the ground under the bed is compacted — an old path, a building plot — loosening it with a
fork before you fill is worth an hour of work. It turns a bed with a floor into a bed without
one.

## Is 6 inches deep enough for a raised bed?

On open soil, yes, for most things. On a hard surface, no.

Six inches suits salad leaves, spinach, radishes, most herbs and annual flowers anywhere. On
open ground it also grows tomatoes and beans perfectly well, because the plants root through into
the soil below. Set the same bed on a patio and those crops will struggle in summer heat — six
inches holds too little water to carry a big plant through a hot week.

The honest test is this: can a root leave the bed? If yes, six inches is a soil-improvement
layer. If no, it is the whole root zone and needs to be sized like one.

## Depth by crop

Root-zone requirements, assuming the bed is the whole root zone:

| Depth    | What it suits                                                |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 6 in     | Lettuce, spinach, radish, most herbs                         |
| 8 in     | Bush beans, chard, onions, garlic                            |
| 10–12 in | Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, brassicas, beets               |
| 12–18 in | Carrots, potatoes, and anything you want to grow really well |

Depth is a separate question from spacing, which decides how many plants fit rather than
how deep they root. The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) has
the spacing for each of these crops.

Twelve inches is the figure to build to if you are choosing once and want to stop thinking about
it. It grows essentially everything above, holds enough water to be forgiving, and is a
comfortable height to work at from a kneeler.

The [plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) works out how many of each crop
a given bed will actually hold, which is the other half of this decision.

## Depth changes how often you water

This is the practical argument for going deeper, and it gets less attention than it deserves.

A deeper bed holds a bigger reservoir. On a hot day the same amount of water evaporates from the
surface of a six-inch bed and a twelve-inch bed — but it is a much larger fraction of what the
shallow bed was holding. Shallow beds need watering more often and punish a forgotten weekend
more severely.

If you garden somewhere hot or dry, or you travel in summer, depth buys you slack. [How much
water does a vegetable garden need per week](/blog/how-much-to-water-a-vegetable-garden/) puts
numbers on it.

## Does a deeper bed mean more soil to buy?

Directly proportionally, and this is the expensive dimension.

| Bed size | 6 in                | 12 in               | 18 in               |
| -------- | ------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------- |
| 4 × 4 ft | 8 cu ft (≈6 bags)   | 16 cu ft (≈11 bags) | 24 cu ft (≈16 bags) |
| 4 × 8 ft | 16 cu ft (≈11 bags) | 32 cu ft (≈22 bags) | 48 cu ft (≈32 bags) |

Bag counts assume 1.5 cubic foot bags. Two ways to manage the cost:

**Part-fill deep beds.** For anything over about 18 inches, the bottom third can be coarse woody
material, upturned turf or native soil, with bought mix in the top twelve inches where the roots
mostly are. Expect that lower layer to settle and plan to top up.

**Build fewer, deeper beds rather than more shallow ones.** Two twelve-inch beds usually grow more
than three six-inch beds of the same total area, and cost about the same to fill.

Run the numbers before you buy timber, not after. The
[raised bed soil calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) shows what each depth costs in
bags, and [what to fill raised garden beds with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/)
covers what should go in them.

## Height for the gardener, not just the plants

Depth and height are not the same thing. A bed can be 24 inches tall with 12 inches of soil in
it, sitting on a void or on coarse fill.

If bending is a problem, build up. A bed at 24 to 30 inches can be worked from standing or from
a seat, and that is often the difference between a garden that gets tended and one that does
not. A tall bed only needs to be _filled_ to a sensible root depth, which keeps the cost
reasonable.

## Measure the inside of the frame

Whatever depth you choose, use the **inside** dimensions when working out volume and spacing.
Two-inch lumber takes four inches off each dimension. On a 4 × 8 ft bed that is nearly two cubic
feet of soil, and a full row of [carrots](/crops/carrot/).

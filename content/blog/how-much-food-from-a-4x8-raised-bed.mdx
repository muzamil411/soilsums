A 4 × 8 foot raised bed is **32 square feet**, and a realistic full-season harvest from it is
somewhere between **40 and 100 pounds** depending on what you grow, how the season goes and how
often you pick. Figures far above that usually come from scaling commercial per-acre yields down,
which is not how a home garden behaves.

## A note on where these numbers come from

Every yield figure on this page is an **estimate**. Our spacing and planting dates are checked
against named US extension publications, but yields are not: they were never part of the
verification pass, because home-garden yield figures are rarely published by extension services
in a form you can cite. [How this data is checked](/data-sources/) sets out which figures on the
site are verified and which are not.

That matters here more than anywhere else on the site, because this is the one topic where the
competing pages are confidently wrong. Claims of 32 pounds of carrots from a single bed, or a
bed feeding a household through a season, are not sourced to anything. We are not going to
answer them with counter-claims of our own — just with honest ranges and the reasoning behind
them.

## Start with the range, not the number

Yield depends on variety, season length, soil, water, pest pressure and, more than anything, how
often you pick. A two-fold spread between a poor year and a good one is normal. Anyone quoting a
single figure is hiding that.

So the useful output is a range, and the number to plan against is the low end.

## A worked 4 × 8 bed

Thirty-two square feet, planted for a household of two through one season:

| Crop                 | Plants | Space used   | Low       | High      |
| -------------------- | ------ | ------------ | --------- | --------- |
| Tomato               | 4      | 16 sq ft     | 32 lb     | 60 lb     |
| Zucchini             | 1      | 6 sq ft      | 6 lb      | 12 lb     |
| Pepper               | 3      | 6 sq ft      | 6 lb      | 15 lb     |
| Lettuce, two sowings | 12     | 4 sq ft      | 6 lb      | 12 lb     |
| **Total**            |        | **32 sq ft** | **50 lb** | **99 lb** |

Fifty pounds is the number to plan around. That is a meaningful contribution to a household's
vegetables through summer, and nothing like feeding it.

Note what that bed does _not_ contain: potatoes, onions, carrots, brassicas. Thirty-two square
feet fills up faster than people expect once real spacings are applied — four tomatoes at
24-inch spacing take half of it.

## Which crops give the most food per square foot

| Earn their space | Why                                                                             |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tomatoes         | Highest yield per square foot of anything on the list, and shop-bought is worse |
| Salad leaves     | Cut-and-come-again for months from a tiny area; expensive to buy                |
| Herbs            | Enormous value per square foot; sold in supermarkets at absurd prices           |
| Zucchini         | One plant is genuinely enough for two people                                    |
| Climbing beans   | Vertical, so they use almost no ground                                          |
| Chard, kale      | Picked over months rather than harvested once                                   |

| Rarely worth it in a small bed | Why                                                            |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sweet corn                     | Needs a block for pollination; a few plants pollinate badly    |
| Pumpkins, winter squash        | 36–48 inch spacing for one or two fruit                        |
| Main-crop potatoes             | Cheap to buy, bulky to grow; early varieties are a better case |
| Onions, storage                | 4-inch spacing but months in the ground and cheap in shops     |
| Cabbage                        | Large, slow, and inexpensive year round                        |

The rule that falls out of this: grow what is **expensive to buy, better fresh, or picked over a
long period**. Skip what is cheap, stores well commercially and occupies ground all season.

## Can a vegetable garden feed a family of four?

Not a 4 × 8 bed, and probably not a normal garden.

Published estimates for feeding one person run from about 200 square feet to several thousand,
which tells you how soft the question is — the answer depends on what proportion of the diet you
mean, whether you count storage crops, your season length and your skill.

What a small garden does well is different and more useful: it supplies the things that are worst
in shops. Ripe tomatoes, cut salad, fresh herbs, beans picked the same day. Judged as a
replacement for a supermarket, a small bed fails. Judged as a supply of the produce money buys
worst, it is excellent.

## Three things that move the totals more than the bed size

**Succession.** A bed re-sown three times produces far more than one sown once. The per-plant
figures above are per crop, not per season —
[succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/) covers how to keep ground
full.

**Picking often.** Beans, zucchini and cucumbers stop producing once they set mature fruit.
Picking every two days keeps them going for weeks longer; picking weekly halves the harvest.

**Water and feeding.** A bed that runs dry at fruit set loses more yield than any spacing decision
gains. See [how much water does a vegetable garden need per week](/blog/how-much-to-water-a-vegetable-garden/)
and [how often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/).

For where to put the bed and how to fit more in,
[how to plan a vegetable garden layout](/blog/planning-a-vegetable-garden-layout/) covers siting,
width and rotation.

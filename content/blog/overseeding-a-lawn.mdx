Overseeding thickens a thinning lawn by sowing new grass into the existing one. It is cheap and
effective when done at the right time with the right preparation, and a complete waste of a bag
of seed when it is not.

## When

This matters more than anything else on the page.

**Cool-season grasses** — fescues, ryegrass, Kentucky bluegrass — are best overseeded in late
summer to early autumn. The soil is still warm so germination is fast, nights are cooling so
seedlings are not stressed, and annual weeds are finishing rather than starting. Roughly six to
eight weeks before your first hard frost. Spring is the second-best window.

**Warm-season grasses** — bermudagrass, zoysia, centipede — want late spring into early summer,
once soil temperatures are reliably warm.

Overseeding in midsummer means fighting heat and weeds at once, and it usually loses.

## How much seed

Rates vary by a factor of ten across common species, because seed size does. Per 1,000 square
feet, for overseeding:

| Grass                | Overseeding rate |
| -------------------- | ---------------- |
| Kentucky bluegrass   | 1.5 lb           |
| Tall fescue          | 5 lb             |
| Fine fescue          | 3 lb             |
| Perennial ryegrass   | 5 lb             |
| Annual ryegrass      | 6 lb             |
| Bermudagrass, hulled | 1 lb             |
| Zoysiagrass          | 1 lb             |
| Centipedegrass       | 0.25 lb          |
| Bahiagrass           | 4 lb             |
| Buffalograss         | 2 lb             |

The overseeding rate is about two thirds of the rate for establishing a new lawn from bare soil,
because you are filling gaps rather than covering ground. Sowing a full new-lawn rate into
existing grass mostly wastes seed: the seedlings compete with established plants and with each
other.

The [grass seed calculator](/tools/grass-seed-calculator/) will scale whichever species you are
using to your actual lawn area.

If you do not know what is growing there, match the climate rather than guessing the species, and
use a blend — most bagged mixes are blends for exactly that reason.

## Step by step

**1. Mow short.** Cut to about an inch and a half, lower than usual, and collect the clippings.
This gets light down to the soil surface and stops existing grass shading the seedlings.

**2. Rake or scarify hard.** This is the step that decides the outcome. Seed must reach soil.
Remove thatch and scratch the surface until you can see bare soil between the grass plants. A
spring-tine rake works on a small lawn; a scarifier or a hired dethatcher is worth it on a large
one.

**3. Fix the underlying problem, if there is one.** A lawn thin because of compaction will be thin
again next year. Aerate it — hollow-tine, ideally, removing plugs rather than just punching holes.
A lawn thin because of shade needs a fine fescue blend or something other than grass. A lawn thin
because of acid soil needs a soil test and possibly lime.

**4. Sow.** Half the seed in one direction, half at right angles to it. A single pass always
leaves stripes, and this is the simplest way to get even coverage whether you use a spreader or
your hand.

**5. Top-dress lightly, or do not.** A thin scatter of compost or screened topsoil — no more than
a quarter inch — improves seed-to-soil contact and holds moisture. More than that buries the seed.
On a well-raked lawn you can skip it.

**6. Roll or tread it in.** Light pressure presses seed into contact with soil. A water-filled
roller on a large lawn, or walking over it on a small one.

**7. Water lightly and often.** This is the other step people get wrong. The top half inch must
stay damp until germination, which means light watering once or twice a day for two to three
weeks — not a deep soak every few days. Once the seedlings are established, switch to deep and
infrequent.

**8. Stay off it.** Keep traffic off until the new grass has been mown twice.

## Feeding and what not to do

A starter fertilizer with phosphorus supports root development, and a soil test will say whether
you need it. Avoid a nitrogen-heavy feed, which pushes the existing grass into growth that shades
the seedlings.

**Do not apply weedkiller.** Most pre-emergent herbicides stop grass seed germinating just as
effectively as they stop weeds, and the label will usually say to wait weeks or months either
side of seeding. Deal with weeds before you overseed or well after, not during.

## Mowing the new grass

Wait until the new grass is about three inches tall, then mow to two and a half. Use a sharp
blade — a dull one tears young grass out by the roots rather than cutting it.

Gradually return to your normal height over the next few cuts.

## Expectations

Perennial ryegrass shows in five to seven days. Fescues take one to two weeks. Kentucky bluegrass
takes two to three, which is why it is usually blended with ryegrass. A patchy result after ten
days is normal, not a failure.

A single overseeding will thicken a lawn noticeably. A lawn that is thin for a structural reason —
compaction, shade, poor soil, heavy wear — needs that reason addressed, or it will need
overseeding again every year.

## The short version

Overseed in late summer for cool-season grass, use the species-specific overseeding rate rather
than a general figure, mow short and rake until you can see soil, sow in two passes at right
angles, keep the surface damp for two to three weeks, and skip the weedkiller.

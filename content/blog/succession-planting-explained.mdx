"Succession planting" describes at least four distinct techniques, and most articles about it
pick one without saying which. They solve different problems, and a garden that uses all four
produces considerably more than one that uses none.

## 1. Repeated sowings of the same crop

The simplest version, and the one that turns a glut into a supply.

Fast crops mature all at once. Sow a full row of radishes and you have more radishes than anyone
wants for about ten days, then none. Sow a short row every two or three weeks and you have
radishes continuously for months, from the same amount of seed overall.

Crops worth sowing this way: radishes, lettuce and salad leaves, spinach, bush beans, carrots,
beets, cilantro, dill, and turnips.

Crops not worth it: anything that produces over a long period from one sowing — tomatoes,
peppers, aubergines, pole beans, kale, chard. One sowing does the whole season.

The practical habit is a note in the calendar rather than a plan. Every other Saturday, sow a
three-foot row of something.

## 2. Following one crop with another

Using the same ground twice or three times in a season, with different crops.

A bed that grows peas in spring, beans in summer and spinach in autumn has produced three crops
where most beds produce one. The sequence matters: put a heavy feeder after a legume, which
leaves nitrogen behind, and follow a deep-rooted crop with a shallow one.

Rough sequences that work:

- Peas or broad beans → brassicas → garlic
- Spinach → bush beans → autumn lettuce
- Early potatoes → leeks
- Radishes and salad → tomatoes (once the soil is warm) → overwintering onions

The constraint is soil fertility. Three crops in a season take three crops' worth of nutrients
out, so the bed needs compost between rounds, not just in spring.

## 3. Interplanting

Putting a fast crop in the space a slow crop will eventually need, and harvesting it before the
slow crop gets there.

Radishes sown along a carrot drill come up in days, mark the row while the carrots germinate, and
are pulled long before the carrots need the space. Lettuce between young brassicas is finished by
the time the brassicas close over. Spinach under sweetcorn uses the ground while the corn is still
short.

This is the technique with the least downside. There is no claimed chemistry, just use of space
and time, and it reliably increases what a bed produces.

## 4. Varieties with different maturity dates

Sowing early, mid and late varieties of the same crop on the same day, so they ripen in sequence
rather than together.

This is how to have sweetcorn over a month rather than a week without three separate sowings, and
it is standard practice for potatoes: an early, a second early and a maincrop planted together
crop from June through September.

Look for days-to-maturity figures on the packet and choose varieties thirty or more days apart.

## Building a schedule

Start from your two frost dates, because everything is relative to them.

**Spring.** Work forward from your last frost date using each crop's offsets. Hardy crops go in
first — peas five weeks before the frost date, spinach six, lettuce four. Tender crops follow it.

**Summer.** Keep the repeated sowings going every two to three weeks, and fill any square that
empties within a few days.

**Autumn.** This is where planning pays most, and where most gardens stop too early. Work
_backwards_ from your first fall frost date:

```
last sowing date = first fall frost - days to maturity - 2 weeks margin
```

The margin matters because growth slows as days shorten. A 45-day lettuce with a mid-October
frost date can go in as late as the end of August. Hardy crops — spinach, kale, mache — can be
sown later still, and under fleece or in a cold frame they will produce into winter.

thirty crops from your frost date, and flags anything that will not ripen before your fall frost.

## Where it goes wrong

**Running out of fertility.** Three crops from one bed is three crops' worth of nutrients. Add
compost between rounds.

**Sowing into a bed still occupied.** Have seedlings ready in trays, so the day a crop comes out
the next one goes in. A bed that sits empty for three weeks in July has wasted the best growing
weather of the year.

**Forgetting.** This is the real failure mode. Succession sowing is a habit rather than a skill,
and the gardens that do it well are the ones where it is on a calendar.

**Sowing cool-season crops into midsummer.** Lettuce and spinach will bolt or refuse to germinate
in high heat whatever the schedule says. Skip the hottest weeks and resume as nights cool.

## The short version

Sow fast crops little and often rather than all at once. Follow one crop with another in the same
bed, adding compost between. Tuck quick crops between slow ones. Choose varieties with different
maturity dates. And plan the autumn by counting backwards from your first frost date, because
that is the half of the season most gardens leave on the table.

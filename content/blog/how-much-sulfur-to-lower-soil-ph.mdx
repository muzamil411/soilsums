There is no single agreed figure for how much sulfur lowers soil pH, and the honest answer
starts there: published rates for a one-unit drop range from about **10 to 40 lb of elemental
sulfur per 1,000 sq ft** depending on soil texture and which service you ask. Several sources
also cap a single application at 5–10 lb. Apply less than you think, and retest.

## Why the published rates disagree

The sources that rank for this question give figures that do not reconcile, and it is worth
seeing them side by side rather than picking one:

| Source                         | Rate given                           | For                    |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------- |
| University of Maine Extension  | 15 lb per 1,000 sq ft                | Lowering pH to 6.5     |
| Several US extension services  | 10–15 lb per 1,000 sq ft             | A one-unit drop        |
| Common cap in the same sources | No more than 5–10 lb per 1,000 sq ft | Any single application |

Two of those rows contradict each other directly: a one-unit drop needs 10–15 lb, but no single
application should exceed 5–10 lb. Both can be true only if the correction is split across two
applications, which is the practical reading and the safe one.

The reason for the spread is the same one that makes lime rates differ threefold between states:
what actually decides the requirement is the soil's **buffering capacity** — how strongly it
resists a pH change — which is driven by clay content and organic matter and cannot be read off a
pH meter. Two soils both reading 7.5 can need very different amounts of sulfur.

A soil test that reports buffer pH, or a direct recommendation from your own extension service,
is worth more than any figure on this page. The same logic works in the other direction for
raising pH — the [lime calculator](/tools/lime-calculator/) shows the published ranges rather
than a single number for exactly this reason.

## First, check whether you need to

Most gardeners who think they need to lower pH do not.

Most vegetables are comfortable between pH 6.0 and 6.8, and many tolerate up to 7.5 without
complaint. Soil at pH 7.2 is not a problem for a vegetable bed, and treating it as one wastes a
season.

The cases where it genuinely matters:

| Situation                      | Target pH | Notes                                                   |
| ------------------------------ | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Blueberries                    | 4.5–5.5   | Chlorotic and stunted above about 6.0                   |
| Rhododendron, azalea, camellia | 4.5–5.5   | Same mechanism                                          |
| Potatoes                       | 5.0–6.5   | Mild — scab worsens as pH rises                         |
| Genuinely alkaline soil        | Above 7.5 | Iron and manganese lock up, leaves yellow between veins |

Our [potato guide](/crops/potato/) carries that 5.0–6.5 range, and it is the one vegetable where
deliberately _not_ liming is the right call.

If your soil is above 7.5 because it sits on chalk or limestone, be realistic: you are fighting
the parent material and it will win.

## Elemental sulfur, the one that works

Soil bacteria oxidise elemental sulfur into sulfuric acid, which lowers pH. Two consequences
follow from it being a biological process rather than a chemical one.

**It is slow.** Several months to a year for the full effect. The bacteria need warm, moist,
active soil, so sulfur applied to cold ground in late autumn largely sits there until spring.

**It needs the soil to be alive.** In sterile or very dry soil the conversion is slow to the
point of ineffective.

Texture changes the requirement in the same direction it does for lime: clay resists the change
and needs more, sand needs less and drifts back sooner. Work it into the top six inches, water
it in, apply no more than about a unit's worth in a year, and retest the following season before
adding more. Overshooting downwards is exactly as awkward as overshooting upwards, and the
correction is lime and another year.

## Does vinegar lower soil pH?

For a few hours, then it reverses.

Vinegar is dilute acetic acid — a weak organic acid that soil microbes consume within days. Any
pH change goes with it. Meanwhile the acid damages fine roots and soil life on the way through.

It is not a soil amendment, and the reason it keeps appearing online is that it _does_ change
the reading if you test the soil immediately afterwards. Test again a week later and the change
has gone.

## The other things that do not work

**Coffee grounds.** Mildly acidic fresh, close to neutral once brewed, and present in quantities
far too small to shift soil pH. Genuinely useful in a compost pile as a green at about 20:1
carbon to nitrogen — see [greens and browns in compost](/blog/greens-vs-browns-in-compost/) —
and useless as an acidifier.

**Pine needles.** The reputation greatly exceeds the effect. Fresh needles are acidic, but they
compost out near neutral and the pH change under a needle mulch is small.

**Citrus peel and tea leaves.** Compost ingredients, not amendments.

**Garden-centre "sulphur" sold as a fungicide.** It may be the wrong particle size or a
different formulation. Read the label and buy something sold as a soil acidifier.

## What does help, slowly

**Ammonium-based fertilizers** acidify gradually as a side effect of nitrification. This is not a
controllable way to change pH, but it explains why long-fertilized beds drift acid, and it means
that if you are feeding an acid-lover anyway, an ammonium-based product works with you.

**Organic matter** has a modest acidifying effect and a large effect on everything else. It also
raises buffering capacity, which cuts both ways: the soil becomes more resistant to pH change in
both directions. For blueberries in particular, a pine bark or composted conifer sawdust mulch
renewed annually does more good over time than a single sulfur application.

## A better plan for acid-lovers

If you want blueberries and your soil is pH 7.0, the honest answer is usually a container or a
dedicated bed rather than an attempt to acidify the garden.

A half barrel of ericaceous mix will grow a better blueberry than three years of sulfur on
limestone soil, and it will not drift back. [What to fill raised garden beds with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/)
covers building that bed; the principle is the same, with an ericaceous mix in place of the
usual one.

Fill raised garden beds with a mixture, not one material: roughly **60% topsoil, 30% compost and
10% coarse material** such as bark, perlite or pumice. Pure compost is the common mistake — it
holds water beautifully in year one, then collapses as the organic matter finishes breaking
down. No single material does all three jobs a bed needs.

## What a bed actually has to do

Three things, and they pull against each other.

It has to **hold water**, so a plant is not stressed between rains. It has to **drain**, so
roots are not sitting in water and suffocating. And it has to **hold its structure**, staying
open enough for air and roots to move through it for years rather than compacting into a brick.

| Material on its own | Holds water | Drains                | Keeps structure        |
| ------------------- | ----------- | --------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Compost             | Very well   | Adequately at first   | Poorly — it disappears |
| Topsoil             | Adequately  | Poorly inside a frame | Well                   |
| Coarse material     | Poorly      | Very well             | Very well              |
| The mixture         | Well        | Well                  | Well                   |

That table is the whole argument for a mix. The [raised bed soil calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/)
splits whatever total volume your bed needs into those three parts, and the percentages are
editable if your situation calls for different ones.

## Can you fill a raised bed with just compost?

You can, and it will disappoint you by the second season.

Compost is 30 to 50% organic matter that is still decomposing. As that finishes, the volume
goes with it — a bed filled with pure compost commonly drops a third in the first year. The
structure goes at the same time: what began as a fluffy, water-holding mix slumps into something
dense and airless.

There is a second problem that gets less attention. Compost that dries out completely can turn
hydrophobic, shedding water instead of absorbing it. A bed can then be soggy on top and bone dry
two inches down.

Compost is the best thing you can add to a bed and a poor thing to fill it entirely with. Use it
as the 30%, and add more every spring.

## A starting point, and how to adjust it

For a general vegetable bed:

- **60% topsoil or a bagged garden soil.** The bulk and the mineral structure. It holds nutrients
  in a way pure organic matter does not, and it does not disappear.
- **30% compost.** The biology and the water-holding. Municipal compost, bagged compost,
  well-rotted manure, leaf mould, or your own.
- **10% something coarse.** Perlite, coarse bark, pumice or sharp sand. The part people skip,
  and then wonder why the bed is heavy. It keeps air pockets open as the organic matter settles.

Adjust from there:

| Your situation                | Change                                                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Heavy clay native soil        | Coarse fraction to 15–20%; be generous with compost                     |
| Sandy native soil             | Use more of it; lean on compost for water holding                       |
| Bed on a patio, deck or liner | Treat as a large container — more coarse material, real drainage holes  |
| Bed deeper than 18 in         | Bottom third can be coarse woody material, upturned turf or native soil |

[University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/soil-fill-raised-beds)
publishes a mix along similar lines, and it is worth reading alongside this one — the proportions
published by different extension services vary, because the soils they are written for vary.

## What to put in the bottom of a raised bed

Over grass: cardboard, which smothers the turf and rots away within a season. Over soil:
nothing. In a deep bed: coarse woody material for the bottom third, if you want to save money.

Three things not to put there:

**A gravel layer "for drainage".** This does not work, in a bed or a pot. Water will not cross
from fine material into coarse until the fine material above is saturated, so a gravel layer
_raises_ the waterlogged zone rather than lowering it.

**Woven landscape fabric.** It stops roots reaching the ground below and stops worms coming up.
Both are things you want happening.

**Fresh wood chips mixed into the soil.** On the surface as mulch they are fine. Dug in, they tie
up nitrogen while they break down — wood chips run around 400:1 carbon to nitrogen against the
roughly 30:1 a pile needs, which is why the microbes decomposing them take nitrogen from
everything nearby. [Greens and browns in compost](/blog/greens-vs-browns-in-compost/) explains
the mechanism.

Also avoid **fresh manure**, which burns roots and causes forked growth in root crops, and
**cheap topsoil of unknown origin**, which is sometimes subsoil, sometimes full of perennial weed
root, and occasionally contaminated. Ask what it is and buy a small amount first if unsure.

## How to fill a raised garden bed, step by step

Six steps, in order. The proportions come from the section above; this is how they actually go
into the frame.

1. **Level and prepare the ground.** Set the frame on ground that is level side to side — a bed
   that slopes drains to one end and waters unevenly all season. Loosen the soil inside it with
   a fork rather than removing it, so roots can carry on downwards. Cut any grass short and
   leave it in place; do not lay plastic or landscape fabric across the base.
2. **Deal with the bottom.** On open soil, leave it open. The exchange between bed and ground —
   worms up, roots down, water through — is worth more than anything a barrier prevents. Over a
   patio, decking or a liner, add a coarse drainage layer instead, because there is nowhere else
   for water to go. Cardboard over grass is fine and is gone within a season.
3. **Add the coarse layer.** Put roughly a tenth of the volume in as coarse material — bark
   fines, horticultural grit or perlite. In a deep bed this can sit towards the base; in a
   shallow one, mix it through instead, where it does more good.
4. **Mix and add the main fill.** Combine about 60 percent topsoil with 30 percent compost and
   add it in layers of six inches or so, mixing as you go. Stacking bands of one material and
   then another gives you a bed with a compost layer and a soil layer rather than a bed of mix,
   and roots stall at the boundary between them.
5. **Water it in and let it settle.** Water thoroughly and leave it a few days. The level will
   drop as air pockets close — that settling is normal, and it is why filling to the brim on
   day one only wastes material you then have to rake back.
6. **Top up and dress the surface.** Bring it back to within an inch or two of the rim, then
   mulch. Leave that gap rather than filling flush, so watering does not wash the mix over the
   sides every time.

## Filling a bed cheaply: hugelkultur and core gardening

Filling a deep bed entirely with bought mix is expensive, and the usual answer is to occupy
some of the lower volume with material you already have. **Hugelkultur** buries logs and woody
debris in the base; **core gardening** buries a central spine of straw or similar absorbent
material. Both work on the same idea — the lower third of a deep bed is mostly reservoir, not
root zone, so it does not all have to be mix you paid for.

The saving is real. So are three costs, and a recommendation that mentions only the first half
is not worth acting on.

**It settles, a lot.** Wood and straw lose most of their volume as they break down. A bed
filled this way can drop several inches in the first season and keep dropping for two or three
more, so expect to top it up more often than a conventionally filled bed — and expect the
surface to become uneven as material below decays at different rates.

**Nitrogen gets tied up.** Microbes decomposing woody material draw nitrogen from the soil
around them to do it, and while that is happening it is not available to your crops. The effect
fades as the wood breaks down, but in the first season it is real. Adding extra nitrogen at
planting compensates; the sources and how fast each releases are covered in
[how to add nitrogen to soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/).

**The first year is weaker.** Between the settling and the nitrogen draw, a hugelkultur bed
usually underperforms a conventionally filled one in year one, then catches up and eventually
passes it as the buried material turns into organic matter.

So it suits a deep bed you are building in autumn for the following spring, where the settling
and the first flush of decomposition happen before anything is planted. It suits a shallow bed
poorly, because there is no spare depth to give up. And it is the wrong choice if this bed has
to produce well in its first season — in that case, fill it properly and save the money
somewhere else.

## Work out the volume before you shop

This is where most of the frustration comes from.

| Bed size | 6 in deep | 12 in deep | 18 in deep |
| -------- | --------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| 4 × 4 ft | 8 cu ft   | 16 cu ft   | 24 cu ft   |
| 4 × 8 ft | 16 cu ft  | 32 cu ft   | 48 cu ft   |
| 3 × 6 ft | 9 cu ft   | 18 cu ft   | 27 cu ft   |

A 4 × 8 ft bed at 12 inches deep needs 32 cubic feet — about 22 bags at 1.5 cubic feet, or 1.2
cubic yards loose. People routinely buy four bags and are surprised.

Two practical points. Measure the **inside** of the frame: two-inch lumber makes a bed four
inches narrower inside than it looks. And buy about 10% more than the calculation says, because a
fresh mix settles as its organic matter continues to break down.

How deep to build in the first place is a separate question with real cost consequences —
[how deep should a raised bed be for vegetables](/blog/how-deep-should-a-raised-bed-be/) works
through it crop by crop.

## Topping up, year after year

A raised bed is not filled once. Expect the level to drop an inch or two a year as the organic
matter is consumed, and top it up each spring with compost rather than a full mix.

That annual compost is what keeps the bed alive: it feeds the soil biology, replaces what the
crops took, and maintains structure. Mulching the surface through the season slows the loss and
does the same job more gradually.

A bed filled this way and topped up will grow better vegetables in year five than in year one —
including hungry crops like [tomatoes](/crops/tomato/), which are the usual reason people build
a bed in the first place.

Containers are how most people garden when they have a balcony, a patio or no soil worth
digging. Plenty of vegetables do very well in them. A few are a waste of a pot, and two
mistakes account for nearly every failure.

## The two things that kill container vegetables

**Running out of water.** A pot holds a small, finite reservoir and it is fully exposed to sun
and wind. A twelve-inch pot in high summer can go from damp to bone dry in a day, and a plant
that wilts hard repeatedly never really recovers. This is the single biggest difference between
container and ground growing.

**Sitting in water.** The opposite, and just as fatal. A container without a drainage hole, or
standing in a full saucer, has waterlogged soil at the bottom where roots suffocate. Every
container needs a hole, and saucers should be emptied.

Everything else about container growing is a detail beside these two.

## Crops that do genuinely well

**Salad leaves and lettuce.** Fast, shallow-rooted, happy in six inches of mix, and they
tolerate part shade. A window box of cut-and-come-again leaves is one of the best returns on a
container anywhere.

**Herbs.** Basil, parsley, chives, thyme, oregano, mint. All suit pots, and mint is actively
better in one because it cannot take over a bed. Most want eight inches of depth; thyme and
oregano are happy in less.

**Tomatoes**, given enough room. A determinate or dwarf variety wants at least a twelve-inch pot;
an indeterminate one wants fourteen inches or more. They are among the heaviest drinkers on this
list and will need daily water in summer, but a well-fed container tomato produces very well.

**Peppers and chillies.** Ten to twelve inches, and they positively like the reflected heat of a
patio wall. Compact varieties suit containers particularly well.

**Bush beans.** Eight to ten inches of depth, several plants to a wide container. They crop
heavily for a few weeks and then finish, which suits a pot you want to replant.

**Radishes.** Ready in under a month, need almost no depth, and make a good filler crop between
slower things.

**Spring onions and garlic chives.** Narrow, shallow-rooted and productive for the space.

**Chard.** Handsome, productive over a long period, and content in a ten-inch pot.

**Strawberries.** Well suited to pots and hanging baskets, where they are also easier to keep
away from slugs. Four to a twelve-inch container.

## Crops that are a poor use of a pot

**Sweetcorn.** Needs a block of plants for pollination and takes enormous containers to do
badly. Skip it.

**Pumpkins and winter squash.** Enormous plants for one or two fruit. A zucchini in a half barrel
is just about worth it; a pumpkin is not.

**Parsnips and long carrots.** Possible in a deep container, awkward in anything else. Short or
round carrot varieties are the sensible compromise.

**Brussels sprouts.** Tall, slow, top-heavy, and they occupy a large pot for most of a year.

**Potatoes**, with a caveat. They grow fine in a large bag or bin and produce a modest crop, but
the volume of mix required per pound of potatoes is poor value compared with almost anything else
on the list.

## Work out how much mix you need

This is where container gardening gets unexpectedly expensive, and where people under-buy.

A twelve-inch pot filled ten inches deep holds about seventeen US dry quarts of mix — roughly
two thirds of a 25-quart bag. A half barrel holds around 126 dry quarts, which is five of those
bags. A 36-inch grow bag takes about 68.

Note the unit: bagged potting mix in the United States is sold in **dry** quarts, and a cubic
foot is 25.71 dry quarts, not the 29.92 that appears on a lot of websites. That figure is liquid
quarts, and using it will have you buying about 16% too few bags. The
[container calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works in dry quarts for that reason and
shows the other units alongside.

Pots taper towards the base, so expect to use slightly less than the calculated figure.

## Mix, feeding and watering

**Use a potting mix**, not garden soil and not bagged topsoil. Potting mix is built to hold air as
well as water in a confined space; garden soil compacts.

**Feed more often than you would in the ground.** A container has a finite nutrient supply and
watering leaches it out. A liquid feed every week or two through the growing season, at the
strength on the bottle, suits most crops.

**Water in the morning** if you can, and check by weight or by finger rather than by looking. A
pot that feels light is dry.

**Group containers together.** They shade each other's sides and create a slightly more humid
pocket, which reduces water loss noticeably.

**Mulch pots too.** An inch of compost or fine bark on the surface of a large container cuts
evaporation just as it does in a bed.

## The short version

Choose depth over width, twelve inches for fruiting crops and six to eight for leaves and herbs.
Make sure every pot drains. Use potting mix, feed regularly, and water more often than feels
reasonable in summer. Grow salad, herbs, tomatoes, peppers, beans and chard; leave sweetcorn and
pumpkins to people with beds.

A philodendron is the houseplant that forgives almost everything and still looks like it is thriving, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for philodendron. It grows in a dim office, in a bright kitchen, in a hanging basket above a sink, and up a moss pole in a living room — NC State Extension calls the genus a group of houseplants that prefer bright indirect sunlight but tolerate low light, Clemson Cooperative Extension calls philodendrons among the most common and easy-to-grow houseplants, and UF/IFAS Extension says that if you are looking for a fool-proof house plant, you could not do much better than a heartleaf philodendron — and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: a bright window, a mix that drains, and a watering only when the top inch of soil is dry. Then the same plant sits for a year making small, plain green leaves in the hands of an owner who keeps it in a dark corner, because how to care for philodendron is not the same as keeping a plant alive. A philodendron is a climbing vine from the warm tropics that starts on the forest floor and climbs into the canopy, and it wants bright, filtered light, a pot it can drain from, and something to climb or trail from. Give it those, and it grows into a vine six to thirteen feet long, with glossy heart-shaped leaves that get bigger with every new one once the plant is attached to a support.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A philodendron that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go limp and droop, and then they lift again within hours of watering. A philodendron that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its stems grow long and bare between leaves, and the new leaves come out small and plain green. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun yellows, and a plant that is fed too much burns brown at the leaf tips. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, yellowing leaves, fading variegation, stretch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for philodendron, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are NC State Extension, Clemson Cooperative Extension, UF/IFAS Extension, and Colorado State University Extension, with the ASPCA covering the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the yellow-leaves section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most. If you bought it for the glossy leaves and got small, plain ones instead, read the light section first — because the leaf size answers to light and support, not to fertilizer.

## How to care for philodendron: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and keep it out of direct sun.** A bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or west-facing window, with no sun falling directly on the leaves, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for philodendron, because the plant is a forest vine that grows under a canopy — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and loses its variegation, while a plant moved into direct sun in one day yellows.

**Let the top inch dry out between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the top inch feels dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for philodendron.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room by day and night, between 65 and 80°F if you can manage it, and inside long before the first cold snap. Cold is the third rule of how to care for philodendron — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single cold night can mark the leaves with brown patches that never heal.

### The last two rules in how to care for philodendron

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed at half strength every two to three months from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding burns the roots with salts, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that brown the leaf tips.

**Keep it in an airy mix, in a pot with holes, and give it something to climb or trail from.** An airy mix of potting soil, bark, and perlite in a pot with drainage holes, repotted when the roots fill the pot — and a moss pole or a hanging basket, because a philodendron without support trails and goes bare at the base, and a philodendron with a pole climbs and makes bigger leaves. Free drainage and a support are the fifth rule of how to care for philodendron, and they protect the roots and the stems for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for philodendron does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays full and colorful: glossy, heart-shaped leaves, bright green or marbled with yellow or cream, per NC State Extension — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it climbs or trails the way it wants to: a slender stem with aerial roots that grip a pole, per NC State Extension, and leaves that get bigger with every new one once the plant is attached — and that climbing habit is the reason the plant is worth keeping for years. Third, it grows fast: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and given a pole grows six to thirteen feet long indoors, per NC State Extension, and makes new leaves all summer. The five rules in how to care for philodendron are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the forest canopy, followed indoors.

## What you bought: two plants in one name

The first thing to get straight in how to care for philodendron is that the name covers two different plants, because the care splits along that line. NC State Extension says philodendron is a genus of over 600 species of herbaceous perennials in the arum family, Araceae, and that in their native habitats, the tropical forests of Central and South America, they tend to climb on tree trunks or grow as epiphytes — but in North Carolina, they are houseplants. The genus name comes from the Greek words *phileo*, meaning "to love," and *dendron*, meaning "tree," per NC State Extension — a tree-lover, which is exactly what the climbing kinds do. Colorado State University Extension says the same in fewer words: the genus is made up of hundreds of species and cultivars, many of which are native to tropical regions of the Americas, and thanks to their adaptability to low light conditions many varieties are popular houseplants. That background explains almost everything about how to care for philodendron. It is a plant from a warm, wet place where the rain comes often and the soil drains instantly, and it wants bright, filtered light, warm air, and a soil that dries out between rains. It is not a desert succulent and it is not a shade plant — it is a climbing vine that starts on the forest floor and grows up into the canopy, which is why it wants light from above and something to climb.

### Climbing or self-heading: which do you have?

NC State Extension divides the genus into two types, and that split is the most useful thing in how to care for philodendron. The climbing or vining types have aerial roots that attach themselves to surfaces such as a pole or trellis, and they include *Philodendron hederaceum* and *Philodendron erubescens*. The non-climbing or self-heading types grow upright and include *Philodendron bipinnatifidum* and hybrids such as 'Birkin' and 'Moonlight' — the latter types have very short stems and leaves held in a rosette, and their leaves are large and have a variety of shapes. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same: the vining types can be limited in height by the height of their support and by training and pruning, while the self-heading types eventually can become very large and should be given ample space. The care basics are shared — bright indirect light, a mix that drains, water when the top inch is dry, warmth — and the differences are in light, space, and support. A climber in a hanging basket trails and stays small-leaved; the same plant on a pole climbs and makes bigger leaves. A self-heading plant in a small pot stays a tabletop rosette; the same plant in a big pot in a bright room becomes a floor plant with leaves three feet long. Buy the habit for the spot, not just the leaf.

### The heartleaf: the one in most homes

The philodendron most people own is the heartleaf, and it is the plant this guide uses as its main example in how to care for philodendron. NC State Extension says *Philodendron hederaceum* is the most common variety of houseplant and has solid green leaves, and on its species page it calls the heartleaf a tropical evergreen trailing or climbing member of the arum family that grows up to 13 feet tall and is grown as a houseplant in temperate climates. The species epithet means ivy-like, per NC State Extension, and likens the plant's climbing, leafy habit to that of English ivy. Clemson Cooperative Extension calls it the common heartleaf philodendron, *Philodendron scandens*, and says it has 2- to 4-inch dark green, heart-shaped leaves, is commonly grown in hanging baskets, dish gardens and as groundcover in larger planters, and may also be trained upwards on bark- or moss-covered boards or totem poles. UF/IFAS Extension says the same: heartleaf philodendron, *Philodendron hederaceum* var. *oxycardium*, is a vining type with dark green heart-shaped leaves, generally 2 to 4 inches in size, often grown in hanging baskets which allow the thin stems and heart-shaped leaves to spill out of their container. It is desired for its glossy, green leaves on cascading stems, per NC State Extension, and it can survive for long periods in extremely low light — which is why it is the plant in the dim office and the north-facing flat. But surviving is not thriving, and a heartleaf in very low light grows slowly, with long bare stems and small leaves. Give it bright indirect light and it grows fast, with full, glossy leaves on every stem.

### The varieties in the shop

The shop will show you more than the plain green heartleaf, and the varieties differ mostly in color, not in care, in how to care for philodendron. Clemson Cooperative Extension lists the common forms. The common heartleaf, *Philodendron scandens* f. *oxycardium*, has glossy, green leaves that are bronzed when young, and its cultivar 'Aureum' has very showy chartreuse leaves on a sturdy plant, while 'Variegatum' has gray, green and cream streaked leaves and shows more variegation in a cool, shaded environment. The velvet philodendron, *Philodendron scandens* f. *micans*, has velvet-textured heart-shaped leaves that are usually bronze with reddish brown undersides. The red-leaf philodendron, *Philodendron erubescens*, is a sturdy climber with 10- to 16-inch, dark green leaves that are red to copper on the underside, and reddish-purple stems while young — and its cultivars include 'Burgundy', with reddish leaves, burgundy veins and red stems, and 'Black Cardinal', a self-heading philodendron with large, 8- to 10-inch long leaves whose new foliage emerges bright burgundy-red and ages to nearly black. 'Prince of Orange' has bright orange new leaves, is a compact grower, and needs high light to maintain the color and growth, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. The velour philodendron, *Philodendron melanochrysum*, has striking heart-shaped, velvety leaves up to 3 feet long, blackish green with pale green veins, and is a climbing philodendron — and as with most philodendrons, its leaves will not reach full size unless the plant is trained vertically for several years. The rule for all of them is the same: the more color in the leaf, the more light the plant needs to hold it, because variegated forms have less green leaf area doing the work.

### The self-heading kinds: big leaves, big space

The self-heading philodendrons are a different proposition in how to care for philodendron, and the shop tag rarely says so. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the tree philodendron, *Philodendron bipinnatifidum*, has a self-heading growth habit, with large, dark green leaves that have deep irregular slits and can grow up to 3 feet long on a robust, erect stem — and that this can grow to be a very large plant and will be too large for most homes. Its 'Dwarf Selloum' is more compact but can still get six feet tall and six feet wide, and 'Xanadu' is a dense low growing variety with deeply dissected lobes, three feet tall and five feet wide. NC State Extension says the tree philodendron is typically used in warmer climates, zone 9a or higher, as a foundation plant, growing up to 12 to 15 feet tall. These are floor plants, not shelf plants, and they want medium to bright light near an east, west or south window, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — more light than a heartleaf needs. They also have brittle, long leaf stalks, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, so locate them out of traffic paths to avoid damage. If you bought a small 'Xanadu' in a 6-inch pot, plan for a three-foot plant in a few years, and give it the floor space now, because moving a big self-heading philodendron is a two-person job.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for philodendron, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the length, because the plant grows fast and the vine decides how much room it needs. NC State Extension says the heartleaf grows up to 13 feet tall, and that depending on the species, the plant size in the genus can vary from 3 to 30 feet tall and 1 to 15 feet wide. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the vining types can be limited in height by the height of their support and by training and pruning. Buy the length for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a bright shelf, a standard plant for a hanging basket, and a long trailing plant only if you have somewhere for the vines to go — because a plant that reaches 13 feet needs room, and a plant without a pole or a basket trails across the floor instead.

### The age that matters: a plant that outgrows its pot

The second is the age, because the big leaves come only to a mature plant. NC State Extension says young and mature plants can have very different appearances, making species identification difficult — and that is as true of the leaves as of the name. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the velour philodendron's leaves will not reach full size unless the plant is trained vertically for several years, and UF/IFAS Extension says that outdoors, where a heartleaf is allowed to grow up trees, the leaves can grow quite large, reaching 12 inches or more in length, while indoors the growth depends on the height of the support, training, and pruning. A plant you buy this year will not make foot-long leaves this year — and a plant kept trailing from a basket may never make them at all — and that is normal, not a failure. The big leaves are a bonus, not the point, and a plant that never makes them is still a healthy plant. What the age buys you is the vine: a heartleaf kept for a few years trails six to thirteen feet, per NC State Extension, and a heartleaf kept for ten years fills a wall. That is why the plant is worth buying young and keeping: you are not buying a decoration, you are starting a vine, and that long view is the point of how to care for philodendron.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, glossy, and firm, bright green with clear markings on a variegated plant, with no yellow patches, no brown edges, and no bare stems where leaves have already dropped. Yellow leaves on a plant in the shop mean the plant has already been over-watered, and those leaves will not turn green again. Brown, curled leaf tips mean the plant has been over-fed, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — and a plant that is already brown at the tips in the shop has been neglected, and a plant with clean green edges is a better buy. The soil should be damp, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes and enough weight that the plant cannot tip over. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a light plastic pot with a heavy trailing vine will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. Buy the glossy, firm, right-sized plant in damp soil in a heavy pot, and the rest of how to care for philodendron is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for philodendron, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the decision that shapes the plant more than any other in how to care for philodendron, because the plant answers to it in its leaves. NC State Extension says the plants of this genus prefer bright indirect sunlight, warm temperatures, and moist, well-drained soil that is high in organic matter, and to avoid direct sunlight because that can scorch the leaves — and that some varieties may require more light than others. On the heartleaf page it says the plant prefers medium light but will tolerate low light. Clemson Cooperative Extension says most philodendrons prefer indirect or curtain-filtered sunlight but will tolerate low light, and that the common heartleaf philodendron will tolerate very low light. UF/IFAS Extension says heartleaf philodendrons enjoy bright diffuse light but will tolerate a range of lighting conditions from diffused light to shade, and to avoid direct sunlight as this can burn the leaves. Colorado State University Extension says philodendrons grow best in bright, indirect light but will survive low-light conditions. The spread is narrow and the direction is the same: bright indirect light is best, low light is survivable, direct sun burns. The planning figure is a bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or west-facing window, with no sun falling directly on the leaves. A heartleaf in a north-facing window will survive and grow slowly; a 'Brasil' or a 'Prince of Orange' in the same window will lose its color, because the variegated and brightly colored kinds need more light than the plain green one — Clemson Cooperative Extension says 'Prince of Orange' needs high light to maintain the color and growth. And a tree philodendron wants more light still: Clemson Cooperative Extension says tree philodendrons grow best with medium to bright light near an east, west or south window.

### The yellowing warning

The first warning that the light is wrong is yellow, and it comes in two forms in how to care for philodendron. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun yellows across the leaves that caught the sun, and the patches do not heal — move it back a few feet from the window and leave the marked leaves alone, because they still feed the plant. A plant in too little light yellows from the bottom up, the lower leaves going yellow and dropping while the growing tip stretches toward the window — and that is the plant telling you it cannot make enough food where it stands. UF/IFAS Extension says too much water or too little light can cause yellowing leaves, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says yellowing of the lower leaves and the death of the growing tips can be caused by too little light or overwatering. The two causes look alike, which is why you check the soil before you move the plant: if the soil is wet and the leaves are yellow, it is water; if the soil is dry and the leaves are yellow, it is light.

### The delayed warning

The second warning is slower, and it is the one owners miss in how to care for philodendron. A plant in too little light does not die — it stretches. The stems grow long and bare between the leaves, the new leaves come out small, and the variegated kinds go plain green. NC State Extension says the heartleaf can survive for long periods in extremely low light, and that is the trap: the plant survives, so the owner assumes the spot is fine, and a year later the plant is a bare vine with a tuft of small leaves at the end. The fix is the same as the fix for yellow leaves: move it to a brighter spot, with no direct sun, and prune the bare stems back to a node, because the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length. The new growth will show whether the light is right — full, glossy leaves, close together on the stem — and the old bare vine is the record of the year in the dark.

### Grow lights when there is no bright window

A grow light is a fair answer when there is no bright window in how to care for philodendron, and the plant is honest about whether it is working. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the heartleaf will grow well under artificial or existing room light, and that is the most shade-tolerant kind in the genus. A plain white LED or fluorescent tube a foot or two above the plant, on for twelve to fourteen hours a day, is enough for a heartleaf — and the test is the same as the test for a window: new leaves close together on the stem, full-sized, and glossy. If the new leaves are still small and far apart, the light is still too weak, and the plant needs to be closer to the lamp or under a brighter one. A self-heading philodendron wants more light than a heartleaf, and a grow light that suits a heartleaf may not suit a tree philodendron — give the big kinds the brightest spot in the room, and the small kinds the lamp.

### Water

Water is the decision that kills the most philodendrons in how to care for philodendron, and the sources are blunt about it. UF/IFAS Extension says to water your philodendron when the top inch of soil becomes dry to the touch, and to use a lightweight, well-drained potting media so the plant does not become too wet or water logged. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to water frequently enough to keep the soil evenly moist, but not soggy, and never to let the plants stand in water. NC State Extension says to keep the soil slightly moist and to slow down on watering in the winter. Colorado State University Extension says philodendrons should be watered frequently enough to keep the soil moist, but not soggy, and when watering to wet the soil thoroughly and allow excess water to drain through the bottom of the container and discard it. The spread is honest: "evenly moist" and "let the top inch dry" describe the same soil from two directions, and the finger test settles it. Push a finger an inch into the mix. If it feels damp, wait. If it feels dry, water thoroughly until it runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for philodendron

The rule is dry, then soak, and the order matters in how to care for philodendron. A little water every day keeps the top of the soil wet and the bottom dry, and the roots in the middle rot while the top looks fine. A thorough soak, only when the top inch is dry, wets the whole root ball and then lets it drain, and the roots get water and air in turn. That is why the sources all say the same thing in different words: water thoroughly, let it drain, and never let the pot stand in water. A pot without a drainage hole breaks the rule at the start, because the water has nowhere to go — and a saucer left full after watering is the same mistake in slow motion. If the plant is in a pot without holes, repot it into one with holes before you change anything else, because no watering schedule fixes a pot that cannot drain.

### How often to water

The honest answer to how often is that the calendar does not know, and in how to care for philodendron the soil does. ### The drooping leaves

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once and live with for a year in how to care for philodendron, and the sources agree on what it should do. NC State Extension says the plants of this genus prefer moist, well-drained soil that is high in organic matter. UF/IFAS Extension says to use a lightweight, well-drained potting media, so the plant does not become too wet or water logged. Colorado State University Extension says philodendrons grow in any well-draining potting soil mixture. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to repot overcrowded plants using a general-purpose potting soil. The mix should hold moisture without staying wet: a bagged indoor [potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) cut with roughly a third [perlite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) or orchid bark, or a general-purpose potting soil on its own in a pot with good drainage, both work — and the test is what happens after you water. If the mix is still soggy three days after watering, it is too dense, and the plant will rot; if it dries out in two days in a bright room, it is too light, and the plant will want water constantly. A mix that is damp on the second day and dry on the seventh is right, and that is the mix to keep.

### An airy mix that drains

The air in the mix matters as much as the water in how to care for philodendron, because the roots breathe. A dense, peaty mix that stays wet for a week starves the roots of air, and the roots rot — and that is the root rot every source names. NC State Extension says overwatering can cause root rot, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot usually results from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or overly frequent watering. The fix is in the mix, not in the watering can: perlite, orchid bark, or coarse pumice mixed into the potting soil opens air pockets that stay open after watering, and a pot with drainage holes lets the excess water out. Terracotta is a good choice for a philodendron that is watered too often, because the clay breathes and the mix dries faster; plastic is a good choice for a plant in a dry, bright room, because it holds moisture longer. Either way, the drainage hole is non-negotiable — a pot without one is a pot that will rot the plant.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the second half of the soil decision in how to care for philodendron, and the rule is simple: one size up, never three. A pot much bigger than the root ball holds a lot of wet mix that the small root system cannot drink, and that wet mix rots the roots — which is why a plant repotted into a pot three sizes too big often dies faster than a plant left pot-bound. NC State Extension says to repot your plant when it becomes root-bound or pot-bound, and that spring is the best time for repotting. Clemson Cooperative Extension says you can repot overcrowded plants at any season, using a general-purpose potting soil. The sign to act is unmistakable: roots circling the drainage holes, water running straight through the pot without wetting the mix, or a plant that needs water every few days because the pot is all roots and no soil. Choose a pot one size larger than the last, with drainage holes, and fresh mix — and if the plant is a self-heading kind, choose a heavy pot, because a three-foot plant with big leaves in a light plastic pot will tip over.

### Repotting: when the roots fill the pot

Repotting is rare work for a philodendron in how to care for philodendron, and the plant tells you when it is time. The method is the same either way: water the plant the day before, so the mix holds together; tip the plant out and shake off the loose mix; trim any brown, mushy roots with clean scissors; set the plant at the same depth in the new pot, with fresh mix around the roots; and water thoroughly, then leave it alone for a week. Do not feed a newly repotted plant for a month — the fresh mix already has enough food in it, and a stressed plant does not want fertilizer. A plant that wilts for a day or two after repotting is normal; a plant that wilts for a week was repotted too roughly, or into a pot too big, and the fix is to leave it alone in a bright, warm spot and let it settle.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the rule owners overdo in how to care for philodendron, and the sources are restrained about it. ### The planning figure: every two to three months, or none at all

The planning figure this guide uses in how to care for philodendron is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half strength, every two to three months from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. ### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding does not make a philodendron grow faster in how to care for philodendron — it makes it stop. The salts in fertilizer build up in the mix, the roots cannot take up water against the salt, and the leaf tips brown and curl while the plant sits in damp soil. ## Support: give it something to climb or trail from

Support is the decision that decides what the plant becomes in how to care for philodendron, because a climber without support is a different plant from a climber with one. ### The moss pole rule in how to care for philodendron

The moss pole rule is simple in how to care for philodendron: give the plant the pole early, while it is young, and let it climb from the start. A moss pole, a bark board, or a wooden totem all work, because the aerial roots grip any rough surface — and the pole should be tall enough for two years of growth, because a plant that outgrows its pole in a year needs a taller one, and moving a vine from one pole to another is fiddly work. Tie the stem loosely to the pole with soft twine or plant tape, and the aerial roots will do the rest, gripping the pole and climbing on their own. Keep the moss damp if you can, because damp moss encourages the aerial roots to grow into it, and a pole that is always dry is a pole the roots ignore. A plant on a damp pole in bright light makes the biggest leaves a heartleaf can make indoors — and a plant on a dry pole in a dark corner makes small leaves on a long, bare stem.

### Aerial roots: what they are and what to do with them

Aerial roots are the brown, wiry roots that grow from the stem at each node in how to care for philodendron, and they are the plant's climbing gear. In the wild they grip tree trunks and drink the rain that runs down the bark; indoors they grip a moss pole, or they dangle in the air looking for something to hold. They are not a problem and they are not a sign of distress — they are the plant doing what it evolved to do. You can tuck them into the moss pole, let them trail into the pot, or trim them back if they get in the way, and none of those choices harms the plant. What you should not do is bury them in wet soil and leave them there, because a root that is always wet rots — and what you should do, if the plant is climbing, is mist the pole now and then, because damp aerial roots grip better and grow faster than dry ones.

## Pruning a vine that never stops

Pruning is the maintenance that keeps a philodendron full in how to care for philodendron, because a vine that is never pruned goes bare at the base. ### Prune in spring, back to a node

Prune in spring, when the plant is growing, and cut just above a node in how to care for philodendron — the knuckle on the stem where a leaf and an aerial root grow. Every cutting from a pruning is a new plant in how to care for philodendron, and that is the cheapest way to fill a pot. ## Getting it to make bigger leaves

Bigger leaves are the reward for climbing in how to care for philodendron, and the sources are clear about how they happen. The rule for big leaves in how to care for philodendron is light plus a pole plus time, and all three are needed together. ### What about the variegated kinds?

The variegated kinds want more light than the plain green ones in how to care for philodendron, and they lose their markings in low light. ### The philodendron that are not philodendrons

The shop will sell you plants called philodendrons that are not philodendrons in how to care for philodendron, and the confusion is old enough that Clemson Cooperative Extension devotes a section to it. ## Philodendron or pothos? How to tell

The heartleaf philodendron and the pothos are the two vines most often confused in the shop in how to care for philodendron, and the sources give two checks that settle it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says one of the most common differences between common heartleaf philodendrons and pothos is that the petioles, the leaf stems, of pothos are grooved and those of philodendrons are not — and that when new foliage emerges, each leaf of a philodendron will have a leaf sheath that protects the new leaf, which eventually dries up and falls off, while new foliage of pothos will not develop a leaf sheath. The leaves themselves differ too: Clemson Cooperative Extension says pothos leaves are glossy and folded along the midrib, and are marked with fine dots or streaks of gold, white or yellow variegation — and that pothos need brighter light and warmer temperatures than philodendrons. A plant sold as a philodendron with grooved stalks and no sheaths is a pothos, and it wants a brighter spot and a warmer room than the heartleaf beside it.

## Making more plants for free

Propagation is the easiest part of how to care for philodendron, and the sources agree that it is easy. ### Stem cuttings: the fast method

Cut a stem a few inches long, just below a node, and keep one or two leaves attached in how to care for philodendron. The node is the knuckle on the stem where a leaf grows and where the aerial root comes out — and it is the part that makes roots, so a cutting without a node will not root, however long the stem is. In water, the roots show in a week or two, and the cutting is ready to pot when the roots are an inch or two long. ### The node rule in how to care for philodendron

The node rule is the whole of propagation in how to care for philodendron, and it is worth stating plainly: no node, no plant. A leaf on its own, without a piece of stem and a node, will sit in water for months, growing roots and nothing else, and it will never become a plant — because the growing point lives at the node, not in the leaf. A stem without a node is the same: it may stay green for a while, but it has no growing point, and it will not root. Cut below the node, keep the node and a leaf, and the cutting has everything it needs: a growing point, a leaf to feed it, and a place for roots to grow. That is the rule, and it is the reason a cutting taken carelessly fails while a cutting taken below the node succeeds.

Water is faster to watch and soil is faster to establish in how to care for philodendron, and both work. Either way, keep the cutting warm and bright, with no direct sun, and keep the mix damp, not wet. Seeds are not the way to make more philodendrons in how to care for philodendron, and the sources do not mention them for a reason. ## Troubleshooting

Most problems in how to care for philodendron are water or light, and the plant shows which one it is in its leaves. Work through the list in order: check the soil first, then the light, then the feeding — because the most common mistake is to feed a plant that is actually drowning, and the second most common is to move a plant that is actually thirsty.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the first problem in how to care for philodendron, and the sources name the same two causes. UF/IFAS Extension says too much water or too little light can cause yellowing leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says yellowing of the lower leaves and the death of the growing tips can be caused by too little light or overwatering. NC State Extension says overwatering can cause the leaves to turn yellow and should be avoided. If only the oldest leaves at the base are yellowing, that is the plant aging, and the fix is to pick them off. If many leaves yellow at once, check the soil: if it is wet, stop watering and let it dry; if the plant sits in a dark corner, move it to a brighter spot with no direct sun; and if the newest leaves are small and pale as well as yellow, the light is too low and the watering too frequent at the same time. A yellow leaf will not turn green again — the fix is in the new growth, not the old.

### Brown, blackened leaf edges and tips

Brown edges and tips are the second problem in how to care for philodendron, and they point at feeding or water. Clemson Cooperative Extension says too much fertilizer can cause the tips of leaves to curl and brown, and UF/IFAS Extension says the same: too much fertilizer can cause the leaf tips of your plant to brown and curl. The fix is to stop feeding, flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear, and wait — a plant that has been over-fed for a month needs a month of plain water before it starts growing again. Brown edges can also come from overwatering, per NC State Extension, which says root rot and blackening of the leaf margins can occur with overwatering — and the test is the soil: if the mix is wet and the edges are brown, it is water; if the mix is dry and the edges are brown, and there is a white crust on the soil, it is fertilizer salts. Salt buildup is the slow version of the same problem, and the fix is the same: flush with plain water, and feed less.

### Losing variegation

Losing variegation is the third problem in how to care for philodendron, and it is always light. A 'Brasil' that goes plain green, a 'Prince of Orange' that comes out green instead of orange, a 'Variegatum' that loses its cream streaks — all of them are in too little light, and the plant has dropped the variegation it cannot afford. Move the plant to a brighter spot, with no direct sun, and watch the newest leaves: those are the ones that show whether the light is right. A leaf that has already gone green will not regain its markings, and pruning the green leaves off does not bring the variegation back — it only makes the plant smaller. The fix is light, and time, and the new leaves.

### Leggy, stretched growth

Leggy growth is the fourth problem in how to care for philodendron, and it is the slow version of the light problem. The stems grow long and bare between the leaves, the new leaves come out small, and the plant looks like a vine with a tuft at the end. The cause is too little light, and the fix is twofold: move the plant to a brighter spot, and prune the bare stems back to a node, because the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length. The pruned vine branches from the node below the cut, and the cuttings root into new plants — so a leggy plant in spring becomes a full plant and a second plant by autumn. A plant that is leggy in a bright room is a plant that is pot-bound, and the fix is a bigger pot with fresh mix.

### Sunburn and yellowing in strong sun

Sunburn is the fifth problem in how to care for philodendron, and it is the only one caused by too much light. A plant moved suddenly into direct sun yellows across the leaves that caught the sun, and the patches are bleached and papery, and they do not heal. NC State Extension says to avoid direct sunlight because it can scorch the leaves, and UF/IFAS Extension says direct sunlight can burn the leaves. Move the plant back a few feet from the window, leave the marked leaves alone — they still feed the plant — and introduce the plant to brighter light gradually, a few feet at a time over a week. A heartleaf that has lived in a dark corner for a year will scorch in a bright window in a day, and the fix is to move it in stages, not in one jump.

### Drooping leaves

Drooping leaves are the sixth problem in how to care for philodendron, and they are the plant's own watering signal. A philodendron that is thirsty goes limp: the leaves droop, the stems feel soft, and the pot feels light when you lift it. Water it thoroughly and the leaves lift again within hours. A philodendron that is over-watered looks different: the leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and the pot feels heavy, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. The two kinds of droop need opposite fixes, and the soil test tells them apart: dry soil and drooping leaves means water; wet soil and yellow leaves means stop watering and let it dry.

### Soft, mushy stems

Soft, mushy stems are the seventh problem in how to care for philodendron, and they are the worst one, because they mean the roots are already rotting. NC State Extension says root rot can occur in overly wet soil, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot usually results from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or overly frequent watering. A stem that is soft and mushy at the base, with leaves that fall off at a touch, is a plant whose roots have rotted — and the fix is to take the plant out of the pot, cut away every brown, mushy root with clean scissors, and repot the healthy part in fresh, dry mix, in a pot with drainage holes. If the whole stem is mushy, the plant is gone, but the cuttings above the rot are not: cut a healthy stem above the mushy part, below a node, and root it in water — and the plant lives on as a cutting. That is the reason to keep a cutting whenever you prune: a plant that rots is a plant you can restart from a piece of itself.

### Cold brown patches

Cold brown patches are the eighth problem in how to care for philodendron, and they are the winter injury. A plant left on a cold windowsill, or in the path of a cold draft, develops brown patches in the middle of the leaves, and the patches do not heal. NC State Extension says philodendrons are very sensitive to cold and that drafts should be avoided, and UF/IFAS Extension says temperatures below 50°F are too cool for the heartleaf philodendron. Move the plant a few feet into the room for the winter, away from cold windows and exterior doors, and leave the marked leaves alone — they still feed the plant. A plant that has been chilled once is more sensitive to cold afterwards, so keep it warm for the rest of the winter, and do not feed it until spring.

### Mealybugs

Mealybugs are the first pest in how to care for philodendron, and they are easy to see once you know what to look for: white, cottony masses in the leaf axils, on the undersides of the leaves, and sometimes on the roots. NC State Extension says to monitor for mealybugs, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says mealybugs can infest philodendrons. The fix is to check the undersides of the leaves every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the leaf you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back. Wipe the visible mealybugs off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later, because the eggs hatch after the first treatment. A plant that is checked weekly never gets a bad infestation; a plant that is never checked gets one that takes a month to clear.

### Scale and spider mites

Scale and spider mites are the second pest problem in how to care for philodendron, and they are harder to see than mealybugs. NC State Extension says to monitor for scale and spider mites, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says aphids, mealybugs, scales and spider mites can infest philodendrons. Scale looks like small brown bumps on the leaves and stems, and spider mites are tiny, with the first sign fine webbing on the undersides of the leaves. The fix is the same as for mealybugs: check the undersides of the leaves every week, wipe the scale off with a cotton bud and rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later. Spider mites like dry air, so a plant in a dry winter room is more likely to get them — and a humidifier helps the plant and hurts the mites at the same time. Aphids, which NC State Extension also names, are soft green or black insects on the new growth, and they wash off with a strong spray of water.

## Is philodendron poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for philodendron, because the plant is so common that it trails within reach of pets in millions of homes. [The ASPCA lists heartleaf philodendron as toxic to dogs, to cats, and to horses](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/cordatum), with the toxic principle given as insoluble calcium oxalates and the clinical signs given as oral irritation, pain and swelling of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. Clemson Cooperative Extension says all parts of philodendrons are toxic if eaten, that they contain calcium oxalate crystals that will cause burning and irritation of the mouth, lips and tongue, along with excessive drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing, and that philodendrons should be kept away from any pets or young children that may eat plants. UF/IFAS Extension says philodendrons are toxic to pets, and that chewing on plants can cause oral pain, drooling, foaming, vomiting, and moderate to severe swelling of the lips, tongue, oral cavity, and upper airway.

### The riskiest trailer on the bookshelf

The riskiest philodendron in the house is the trailing one in how to care for philodendron, because it hangs where a cat can reach it. A heartleaf in a hanging basket trails down the wall, and a cat that bats at the trailing vine will chew it — and a dog that finds a fallen leaf on the floor will eat it. The plant is not deadly — the ASPCA does not list it as lethal, and Clemson Cooperative Extension calls the effect burning and irritation rather than poisoning — but a pet that chews it will be in pain, drooling and vomiting, and a pet that swallows a large piece can have swelling that makes swallowing difficult. The fix is placement: hang the basket where the vine cannot be reached, or grow the plant up a pole on a high shelf, or keep it in a room the pets do not enter. A plant on a high shelf with a trailing vine that ends above the cat's reach is a safe plant; a plant on a low table with a vine trailing onto the floor is not.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Keep it out of reach anyway in how to care for philodendron, because the risk is not worth the test. A pet that has never chewed a plant before will chew one eventually, and a child that has never eaten a leaf before will eat one eventually — and the plant is toxic to both, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, which says to keep philodendrons away from any pets or young children that may eat plants. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any philodendron and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. If a child eats a piece, call your pediatrician or your local poison control center. The plant is worth keeping, and the risk is worth managing — but only if the plant is placed where it cannot be eaten.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap can irritate skin in how to care for philodendron, and that is the second safety fact. UF/IFAS Extension says people can have mild allergic reactions to the sap, resulting in an itchy rash. Wear gloves when you prune or repot a philodendron, wash your hands afterwards, and keep the sap out of your eyes. A person who handles the plant every day without gloves may develop a rash over time, and a person who gets sap in a cut will feel it sting. The sap is not dangerous to healthy skin in small amounts, but it is an irritant, and the simplest habit — gloves on, hands washed — is enough.

## The short version

The short version of how to care for philodendron is five rules, and they fit on a card. Bright, indirect light, a few feet from an east- or west-facing window, no direct sun. Water when the top inch of soil feels dry, soak thoroughly, and empty the saucer. Keep it warm, between 65 and 80°F, nights no colder than 65°F, and inside before the first cold snap. Feed lightly at half strength every two to three months from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Keep it in an airy mix in a pot with holes, and give it a moss pole to climb or a basket to trail from. Do those five, and the plant grows into a vine six to thirteen feet long, with glossy heart-shaped leaves that get bigger every year — and the cuttings from one pruning become the next plant.

### And a second plant for the same bright room

A second plant for the same bright room in how to care for philodendron is the [pothos](/blog/how-to-care-for-pothos/), and the two belong together because they are the pair the shop confuses. A pothos wants brighter light and a warmer room than a philodendron, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and its leaves are folded along the midrib with gold or white variegation, while a philodendron's are smooth-stalked and glossy green. A [monstera](/blog/how-to-care-for-monstera/) is the third plant for the same room, and it wants the brightest spot of the three, and a pole to climb, and it will make split leaves with holes if the light is right. All three are in the arum family, all three climb, and all three are toxic to pets — and all three reward the same five rules, with the light turned up a notch for each one in turn. Start with the philodendron, add the pothos, and then the monstera, and the bright room fills itself.

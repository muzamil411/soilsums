Square foot gardening replaces rows with a grid of one-foot squares, each planted at a density
set by the crop. It suits raised beds, it removes the paths that rows need, and it makes
planning and rotation easy to keep track of. It is also slightly oversold, so this covers both
what it does well and where it gets in the way.

## The idea

Divide a bed into one-foot squares with a physical grid — string, thin battens, anything. Plant
each square as a unit: one crop per square, at a density that lets that crop reach full size.

The density is the whole method. A radish needs about three inches of room, so sixteen fit in a
square. A tomato needs two feet, so it gets a square to itself and arguably more. Because you
are never walking on the bed, there is no need for the access space that rows are designed
around, and the whole surface grows food.

## What goes in a square

| Per square         | Crops                                                                 |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 16                 | Carrot, radish                                                        |
| 9                  | Beet, spinach, bush bean, onion, garlic, cilantro                     |
| 8                  | Pea                                                                   |
| 4                  | Lettuce, basil, parsley, strawberry, chard                            |
| 2                  | Cucumber, trellised                                                   |
| 1                  | Tomato, pepper, aubergine, broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale, okra |
| 1 per 4 squares    | Potato, sweet potato                                                  |
| 1 per 9–10 squares | Zucchini, winter squash, melon                                        |
| 1 per 16 squares   | Pumpkin                                                               |

The bottom rows are where people go wrong. A zucchini planted one per square smothers
everything around it and gets mildew from the lack of air. Those crops genuinely need several
squares, and the honest thing is to give them the space or leave them out of a small grid.

The [square foot garden planner](/tools/square-foot-garden-planner/) holds each crop's density
and will tell you when a square count is too small for even one plant of a sprawling crop.

## Building the bed

Four by four feet is the classic size. Sixteen squares, reachable from all sides, and enough to
be worth the effort. Four by eight is the other common shape and fits standard timber.

Do not go wider than four feet. The method depends on never standing on the bed, and a bed
wider than four feet cannot be reached to the middle from either side.

Depth: six inches is the original specification and works for shallow-rooted crops if the bed
sits on open soil. Twelve inches is more forgiving and lets you grow carrots properly.

The grid itself is worth making physical rather than imagining it. It changes how you plant —
you fill a square rather than scattering seed — and it makes succession obvious, because an
empty square is visibly empty.

## Where it helps

**Small gardens.** This is the main case. Removing paths from a small bed adds a surprising
amount of growing area.

**Planning.** A grid is easy to draw, easy to record, and makes rotation trivial to track — you
know exactly what was in each square last year.

**Succession.** As a square of radishes comes out, it is immediately obvious that a square is
free, and it gets replanted. In a row system the same gap tends to sit.

**Beginners.** The density figures replace a judgement call that takes years to develop. You are
much less likely to plant a bed far too thickly or far too thinly.

## Where it gets in the way

**Sprawling crops.** Anything in the bottom three rows of the table above fits awkwardly into a
grid, and a small bed given over to a pumpkin is a bed with one crop in it.

**Tools.** A hoe and a grid do not get along. Everything is done by hand, which is fine for a
few beds and tiresome for a large plot.

**Dogma.** The original method comes with a specific soil recipe and a set of rules presented
fairly firmly. The grid and the densities are the valuable part; the rest is preference.

## Getting more out of it

**Go up.** Cucumbers, pole beans, peas and even small squash on a trellis at the north side of
the bed cost almost no floor space. This is where the method really pays.

**Mind the shade.** In the northern hemisphere, tall crops belong on the north side or they will
shade the rest from midsummer onwards.

**Replant immediately.** The grid's advantage is visibility of empty space. Use it — a square
that sits empty for three weeks is the one thing the method cannot forgive.

**Plan in rounds, not once.** Draw an early-season plan, a main-season plan and a late-season
plan rather than trying to capture a whole year in one grid.

## The short version

Build a bed no more than four feet wide, mark out a real grid, plant each square at the crop's
own density, trellis anything that climbs, keep the sprawlers out or give them the squares they
need, and replant a square the day it empties.

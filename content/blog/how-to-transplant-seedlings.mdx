The move from tray to soil is the most dangerous day in a seedling's life, and learning how to transplant seedlings correctly is what separates a full bed from a row of wilted stems. A seedling that spent weeks in stable indoor conditions suddenly faces wind, full sun, cool nights, and disturbed roots. Penn State Extension notes that most plants experience some transplant shock, when disturbed roots cannot absorb enough water for a while. This guide to how to transplant seedlings follows the order university extension sources recommend: readiness, hardening off, soil temperature, the move itself, and aftercare.

If you started your own plants, the readiness rules below build on the indoor routine in our [seed-starting guide](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/). Bought nursery transplants skip the hardening schedule but still need the soil-temperature and depth rules.

## When your seedlings are ready to move

The first checkpoint in how to transplant seedlings is the leaves — true leaves, not the seed leaves. Penn State Extension says seedlings are ready when they grow their first pair of identifiable true leaves, and at that point they can go into larger individual containers or, if the weather allows, straight into the garden. Ohio State's transplanting module is slightly more conservative: wait until 2 to 3 true leaves have emerged and the pot shows good root growth.

Do not wait much beyond that. Penn State warns that delaying until several sets of leaves have appeared in a crowded seedbed produces weak, spindly seedlings that are easier to damage when you finally move them. Roots poking out of the drainage holes are another sign the plant has outgrown its cell. Ohio State's module frames the same problem as pot-bound plants: excessive root growth with too little soil for oxygen, nutrients, and water, which raises the chance of failure after planting.

The honest edge case: seedlings that are already tall and leggy from weak indoor light will never become sturdy transplants no matter how carefully you move them. It is better to start a fresh batch than to transplant seedlings that are doomed to snap in the first wind. Knowing when to start is half of how to transplant seedlings well; the other half is the move itself.

## Harden them off before the move

Indoor seedlings have thin, tender growth; outdoor conditions are full sun, wind, and cool nights. Hardening off is the acclimation process between the two, and skipping it is the single most common reason home gardeners watch transplants collapse in their first week. Plants taken directly from the house may brown or turn white from direct sunlight and wind, and even when they survive, Penn State Extension notes the damage reduces their vigor and delays establishment.

Penn State's schedule: begin hardening off at least two weeks before transplanting. Place seedlings in a sheltered spot and expose them to outdoor conditions for several hours on mild days, and at night move them somewhere sheltered — a porch, shed, or garage away from exhaust fumes — to protect them from cool nighttime temperatures. Then, gradually over 10 to 14 days, move them into sunlight for increasing periods each day. Two guardrails from Penn State: do not put tender seedlings outdoors on windy days or when temperatures are below 45°F (7°C), and reduce the frequency of watering to prevent lush, weak growth — but never let plants wilt.

Ohio State's lighter version works for a shorter runway: put the plants outside for a few hours a day for the week leading up to the transplant, gradually increasing exposure so they acclimate to outdoor temperatures before the permanent move. Either way, the principle of how to transplant seedlings outdoors is the same — the first full day in the garden should never be the seedling's first day outside.

## Check the soil temperature, not the calendar

Air temperature lies; soil temperature decides whether a transplant grows or sits. This is the step most gardeners skip when figuring out how to transplant seedlings in spring, and it is the one that matters most for warm-season crops.

University of Georgia Extension publishes minimum soil temperatures for transplants, and the crop-specific numbers are worth memorizing: tomatoes, cucumbers, and snap beans need soil at 60°F or above; sweet corn, lima beans, and mustard greens need 65°F; peppers, watermelons, and squash need 70°F; okra, cantaloupe, and sweet potatoes need 75°F. UGA is blunt about the consequences: tomato transplants need soil above 60 degrees to grow, and setting out pepper plants before the soil reaches 70 degrees could stunt their growth for the entire growing season. Planting too early, before the soil has warmed, can also cause seed rot, slow germination, poor growth, and disease.

Measure it properly. UGA's method: take readings on three straight mornings, at 1 to 2 inches deep for seeds and 4 to 6 inches deep for transplants. Ohio State's gardening newsletter gives the same advice in simpler form — push the thermometer to about 4 inches for transplants — and adds that the minimum soil temperatures used for germination apply equally to transplants: cool-season crops such as broccoli, cabbage, beets, and carrots handle 40 degrees, while warm-season crops like beans, tomatoes, peppers, and squash want 55 to 60 degrees as a floor. Ohio State's transplanting module adds the hard stop: never transplant when temperatures are below 50°F or the ground is frozen or frosty.

Note the honest tension between sources: Ohio State's general warm-season floor of 55–60°F is looser than Georgia's crop-specific minimums. For tomatoes and especially peppers, follow the crop-specific number — the 70°F pepper warning exists because the damage is season-long.

## Water an hour before you touch them

Penn State Extension's most practical timing tip: water the seedlings within an hour before transplanting. Moist root balls hold together and stay intact when handled; dry root balls crumble, exposing and breaking the fine roots that do most of the absorbing. Ohio State's module gives the same instruction for kit-grown seedlings — water the plants in the seed-starting kit before transplanting, and moisten the destination soil too.

The handling rule matters as much as the watering when you learn how to transplant seedlings without losses. Penn State says to hold the seedling by its leaves, not the stem, which is easily injured — a crushed stem cannot be repaired, while a damaged leaf can be replaced. Work with a pencil, dibble, or small spoon to loosen the root ball from underneath rather than yanking the plant by its top.

## How to transplant seedlings step by step

With hardened-off seedlings, warm soil, and watered root balls, the move itself is straightforward. This is how to transplant seedlings into the garden or a larger container, in the order Penn State and Ohio State describe it:

1. **Dig the hole first.** Penn State recommends holes two times larger than the soil mass around the roots; Ohio State's module says slightly larger and deeper than the current pot. Space the holes for the mature plant, not the seedling — check the seed packet or the mature spacing for the variety.
2. **Pre-moisten the destination.** Ohio State says to prepare the site by smoothing the soil, removing rocks and debris, and moistening the soil before the seedling goes in.
3. **Lift gently, by the leaves.** Loosen the root ball from underneath with a pencil or dibble, tip the container, and support the root ball as it slides out. Penn State's rule: hold by the leaves, never the stem.
4. **Set at the same depth.** Penn State warns that many seedlings die because they are transplanted too deeply — excess moisture excludes air around fragile buried stems, the tissue rots, and the plant dies. The top of the root ball should sit just under the soil surface, no deeper than it grew in the germination container. Tomatoes are the exception (see below).
5. **Backfill and firm.** Gently firm the soil around the plant to close air pockets, without packing it hard.
6. **Water immediately.** Penn State's routine: firm the soil, water the plant in, then apply a light layer of soil or mulch to reduce evaporation from the surrounding soil. Ohio State says to water the bed and each plant around its base.
7. **Time it for clouds or evening.** Penn State: cloudy conditions are best for transplanting; late afternoon or early evening, when temperatures are cooler and the sun is less intense, is the acceptable alternative. University of Minnesota Extension says the same for tomatoes — transplant in late afternoon or on a cool, cloudy, calm day.

If you are potting up rather than planting out — moving seedlings from a crowded flat into individual containers because the garden is not ready — Penn State says to fill the new containers with the same sterile soilless or seed-starting mix used for germination, level it about 2 inches from the top, and make a dibble hole large enough for the root system without crowding. Our [potting soil guide](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) covers mixing a batch if you are out of bagged mix.

## The tomato exception: plant them deeper

Everything above says not to bury stems — except for tomatoes, where burying the stem is the whole point. Tomatoes grow new roots along any stem that touches soil, and University of Minnesota Extension turns that quirk into a technique: dig a trench 3 to 4 inches deep, remove the lowest leaves from the seedling, and lay the plant down in the trench, burying the stem up to just below the lowest remaining leaves. New roots will emerge from the buried part of the stem, giving a stronger, more vigorous plant with better access to moisture and nutrients.

So when the question is how to transplant seedlings of the tomato family, the answer flips: deeper is better, as long as the leaves you removed do not end up underground to rot. Install the stake or cage at planting time, since UMN notes driving supports in later risks spearing the new root zone.

## Aftercare: the first two weeks

The transplant is not finished when the watering can goes down. For the first week or two, the roots are still re-establishing, and the plant runs on a reduced water supply — which is exactly what makes this the danger window for how to transplant seedlings successfully.

Keep the soil consistently moist, not soggy. Penn State's watering guidance for transplants is to check whether water is needed and avoid both under- and overwatering — a finger pushed 1¼ to 1½ inches deep (to the second knuckle) is their low-tech moisture meter. A light mulch layer around (not against) the stems slows evaporation; our [mulch calculator](/tools/mulch-calculator/) works out how much you need if you are covering a whole bed. If you are filling that bed from scratch, our [raised bed fill guide](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/) covers the layers.

Watch for transplant shock: temporary wilting in the first day or two is normal, since Penn State notes disturbed roots cannot absorb enough water at first. Wilting that persists past a few days, yellowing, or collapse usually traces back to one of the preventable causes — dry root balls at planting, a midday planting in full sun, or soil that was too cold for the crop.

Penn State also suggests fertilizing newly set plants with a high-phosphorus starter solution such as 15-30-15 to promote root development — follow the product label for dilution, since more is not better with young roots.

## Mistakes that set transplants back

The short list of what goes wrong when gardeners learn how to transplant seedlings — all of it avoidable:

- **Planting too deep.** The number-one killer for most vegetables. Same depth as before; only tomatoes get the deep treatment.
- **Skipping hardening off.** A seedling's first day outdoors should not be transplant day.
- **Transplanting into cold soil.** Peppers set out before the soil hits 70°F can be stunted all season (UGA) — the calendar date means nothing next to a thermometer reading.
- **Handling by the stem.** Leaves are replaceable; a crushed stem is not.
- **Planting at noon on a sunny day.** Cloudy or late afternoon, every time.
- **Letting root balls dry out before the move.** Water within the hour before, per Penn State.

Get these six right and the rest is patience. That is how to transplant seedlings the extension-backed way: move them when they show true leaves, harden them off over one to two weeks, wait for warm soil, handle by the leaves, set at the right depth, and water them in on a cloudy evening.

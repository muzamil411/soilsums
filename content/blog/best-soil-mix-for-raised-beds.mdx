The question "what is the best soil for a raised bed" gets answered online with a lot of
recipes and not much reasoning. The reasoning matters more than the recipe, because what you
should put in a bed depends on what the bed is sitting on and what you are growing in it.

## What a bed actually needs to do

Three things, and they pull against each other.

It has to **hold water** for long enough that a plant is not stressed between rains. It has to
**drain** so that roots are not sitting in water, which suffocates them. And it has to **hold
its structure**, staying open enough for air and roots to move through it for years rather than
compacting into a brick.

Pure compost does the first brilliantly, the second passably at first, and the third not at
all. Pure topsoil does the third reasonably and the first two badly inside a frame. The reason
every good raised bed recipe is a mixture is that no single material does all three.

## A starting point

For a general vegetable bed, something close to:

- **60% topsoil or a bagged garden soil.** This is the bulk and the mineral structure. It holds
  nutrients in a way that pure organic matter does not, and it does not disappear.
- **30% compost.** The biology and the water-holding. Whatever you can get: municipal compost,
  bagged compost, well-rotted manure, leaf mould, or your own.
- **10% something coarse.** Perlite, coarse bark, pumice or sharp sand. This is the part people
  skip and then wonder why the bed is heavy. It keeps air pockets open as the organic matter
  settles.

Treat those proportions as a sensible default rather than a formula. The
[raised bed soil calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) will split whatever total volume
your bed needs into those three parts, and the percentages are editable.

## Adjust for your soil and your bed

**If your native soil is heavy clay**, increase the coarse fraction to 15 or 20% and be
generous with compost. Clay's problem in a raised bed is not fertility, it is air.

**If your native soil is sandy**, you can use more of it and lean harder on compost for water
retention. Sandy soil in a bed drains almost too well.

**If the bed sits on a patio, a deck or a solid liner**, treat it more like a very large
container. Nothing can root down into the ground beneath, so drainage has to work entirely
within the bed. Increase the coarse fraction and make sure there are drainage holes.

**If the bed is deeper than about 18 inches**, you do not need bought mix all the way down. The
bottom third can be coarse woody material, turf laid upside down, or native soil. It will
settle noticeably in the first year, so plan to top up.

## What to avoid

**Fresh manure.** It burns roots and, for root crops in particular, causes forked and hairy
growth. Composted manure is excellent; fresh is not.

**Fresh wood chips mixed into the soil.** On the surface as a mulch they are fine. Dug in, they
tie up nitrogen for a season while they break down, and your plants pay for it.

**A layer of gravel at the bottom "for drainage".** This does not work, in a bed or in a pot.
Water does not cross from fine material into coarse until the fine material above is saturated,
so a gravel layer raises the waterlogged zone rather than lowering it. Skip it.

**Topsoil of unknown origin, in quantity.** Cheap bulk topsoil is sometimes subsoil, sometimes
full of perennial weed root, and occasionally contaminated. Ask what it is and where it came
from, and buy a small amount first if you are unsure.

## Work out the volume before you shop

This is where most of the frustration comes from. A four by eight foot bed filled twelve inches
deep needs 32 cubic feet — which is 22 bags at 1.5 cubic feet each, or about 1.2 cubic yards
loose. People routinely buy four bags and are surprised.

Measure the **inside** of the frame, not the outside. Two-inch lumber makes a bed four inches
narrower inside than it looks.

Then buy about 10% more than the calculation says, because a fresh mix settles as the organic
matter in it continues to break down.

## Topping up, year after year

A raised bed is not filled once. Expect the level to drop an inch or two a year as the organic
matter is consumed, and top it up each spring with compost rather than a full mix. That annual
compost is what keeps the bed alive: it feeds the soil biology, replaces what the crops took,
and maintains the structure.

Mulching the surface through the season slows the loss and does the same job more gradually.

## The short version

Do not fill a bed with one thing. Aim for roughly 60% mineral soil, 30% compost and 10% coarse
material, adjust the coarse fraction for your soil and whether the bed can drain into the
ground, skip the gravel layer, work out the volume before you go shopping, and plan to add
compost every spring for as long as you have the bed.

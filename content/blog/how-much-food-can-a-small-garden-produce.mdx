"How much can I grow in a small space" gets answered with some remarkably confident numbers.
Most of them are optimistic, and a few are drawn from commercial per-acre figures scaled down,
which is not how a home garden behaves. Here is a more honest attempt.

## Start with the range, not the number

Yield depends on variety, season length, soil, water, pest pressure and — more than anything —
how often you pick. A two-fold spread between a poor year and a good one is normal for most
crops. Anyone quoting a single figure is hiding that.

So the useful output is a range, and the number to plan against is the low end.

These are typical published ranges for home gardens and worth checking against your own
extension service, which will have figures tuned to local conditions.

## A worked 100 square foot bed

Say a bed ten feet by ten, planted for a household of two through one season:

- **4 tomatoes** — 32 to 60 lb, in roughly 16 sq ft
- **2 zucchini** — 12 to 24 lb, in roughly 18 sq ft
- **4 peppers** — 8 to 20 lb, in roughly 8 sq ft
- **3 cucumbers on a trellis** — 15 to 30 lb, in roughly 6 sq ft
- **20 bush beans** — 5 to 10 lb, in roughly 9 sq ft
- **6 kale** — 6 to 12 lb, in roughly 6 sq ft
- **Salad and radishes in succession** — 15 to 25 lb, in roughly 20 sq ft
- **Herbs** — hard to weigh, disproportionately useful, roughly 6 sq ft
- **Paths and edges** — the remaining 11 sq ft

That comes to roughly **95 to 180 pounds** over a season from 100 square feet. It is a genuine
contribution to a household's summer eating. It is not self-sufficiency, and it does not extend
much past autumn without storage crops or preserving.

## Which crops earn their space

Three different questions, with three different answers.

**Most weight per square foot:** the fruiting crops that keep producing. Tomatoes, cucumbers,
zucchini, pole beans and chard. A pole bean on a trellis produces for months from a few inches of
ground.

**Most value per square foot:** herbs and salad leaves. A pot of basil replaces a great many
expensive supermarket packets, and cut-and-come-again leaves cost almost nothing to grow and a lot
to buy.

**Worst value per square foot:** maincrop potatoes, winter squash, pumpkins and sweetcorn. All
are cheap in shops, all occupy a bed for most of a season, and all take a lot of room. Grow them
because you want to, not because they will save money.

## What actually increases the total

**Pick constantly.** Beans, cucumbers, zucchini and peas slow right down once mature fruit sits
on the plant. Regular picking is the cheapest yield increase available and the one most often
neglected.

**Grow vertically.** Cucumbers, pole beans, peas and small squash on a trellis cost almost no
floor space. This is the single biggest structural gain in a small garden.

**Succession sow.** A square that grows radishes in April, beans in June and spinach in September
has produced three crops where most gardens get one. Fast crops are the ones to keep moving.

**Space properly.** Six well-spaced plants in full sun reliably beat twelve crowded ones. If an
estimate looks too low, squeezing more in is rarely the answer.

**Water consistently.** A dry fortnight during fruit set costs more yield than any fertiliser will
recover.

## What limits it

**Sun.** Below about six hours of direct sun, fruiting crops struggle. Leafy crops and herbs will
still do well, so a shady garden is not a lost cause — it is a salad garden.

**Season length.** A short season rules out long-maturing crops regardless of space. Your first and
last frost dates set this, and they are worth knowing.

**Attention.** The difference between an unmanaged and a well-managed bed of the same size is
larger than the difference between a 100 and a 150 square foot bed. Time is usually the real
constraint, not area.

## On self-sufficiency

The figure often quoted for growing all of a person's vegetables is around 200 square feet per
person, and that assumes storage crops, season extension, preserving and competent management. A
small garden should be judged on what it does well — fresh vegetables in season, at their best,
picked minutes before eating — rather than against a target it was never going to meet.

## The short version

Expect roughly 100 to 180 pounds a season from a well-run 100 square foot bed. Plan against the
low end of every yield figure. Grow fruiting crops and herbs rather than storage crops if space is
tight, trellis anything that climbs, replant squares as they empty, and pick relentlessly.

The first frost is the deadline for geraniums, and the question of how to overwinter geraniums is really a choice between three methods, not one. The geraniums on your porch are tender plants, and a hard frost finishes them — but the same plant can spend winter as a houseplant in a sunny window, as a handful of small cuttings on a shelf, or as a bare-root plant resting in a paper bag in a cool basement. All three methods are published by university extension services, and all three work, but they ask for different things: the houseplant wants your brightest window, the cuttings want a few weeks of patience, and the dormant plant wants a spot that stays cool and never freezes. This guide to how to overwinter geraniums follows the extension services that publish the method: which geranium can be saved, the date to act, the three methods step by step with the numbers each service actually gives, a worked example for one porch, and what to do when the plant goes leggy, drops its leaves, or refuses to root.

## Which geranium can you save?

The first step in how to overwinter geraniums is making sure you are saving the right plant, because the word geranium covers two different groups. The geraniums sold for pots, window boxes, and flower beds are usually Pelargonium — tender perennials, often called zonal, ivy-leaf, or scented geraniums — and they are the plants this guide covers. Hardy true geraniums, in the genus Geranium and often called cranesbills, are different plants that stay in the ground all winter and need none of this. If your plant has rounded, slightly fuzzy leaves and big puffy flower clusters and it sat in a pot or a bed all summer, it is almost certainly a Pelargonium, and a frost will finish it outdoors.

That distinction is the whole reason the work exists. Illinois Extension says annual geraniums (Pelargonium) are among the most popular flowering plants grown in gardens, providing color from May until the arrival of frost, and that after the first hard frost they are typically destined for the compost pile — but that does not have to be their fate. Iowa State University Extension says the same in the other direction: geraniums are popular bedding plants, blooming from May through frost, and the first hard frost does not have to be the end of them. The University of Florida IFAS Extension lists Pelargonium x hortorum, the common garden geranium, as an annual for all zones, and gives winter planting months of February, November, and December only for zones 10 and 11 — the frost-free end of the country. Everywhere else, the plant is an annual unless you intervene.

### What the plant looks like, and what it needs

A garden geranium grows 1 to 2 feet tall and 1 to 2 feet wide, according to the University of Florida IFAS Extension, which also gives its spacing in the bed as 18 to 24 inches apart — 12 to 24 inches if you are planting a solid ground cover. It grows best in full to partial sun in loamy, well-drained soil, and the same source notes that setting plants too deeply could cause stem rot. Those are summer numbers, but they matter for how to overwinter geraniums in one way: the plant you bring in is a foot or two of leafy growth, and the pot it goes into has to hold it. Iowa State University Extension says to dig each plant up and place it in a 6- to 8-inch pot, which is the size that fits a plant cut back for the winter.

### Only healthy plants are worth saving

Every extension service says the same thing before any method begins: save only healthy plants. Illinois Extension is the most direct — before bringing geraniums indoors, inspect them for pests and diseases, and discard any plants with spots or discoloration on leaves and stems, wilting, shriveled or rotting parts, or insects, because diseased plants will be difficult to overwinter and the disease or pests can spread to other plants. The University of Wisconsin Extension ends its guide the same way: always remember to select only healthy plants to overwinter, because that gives the best chance of success next year. A sick plant rarely improves indoors, and a plant carrying aphids or whiteflies into a warm house can infest everything else on the windowsill. If a plant fails the inspection, compost it and spend the winter effort on the strong ones — that triage is the first real step in how to overwinter geraniums.

## When to bring geraniums in

Timing is the first job in how to overwinter geraniums, and every source gives the same deadline: before the first frost, never after it. Illinois Extension says geraniums must be dug or moved indoors before the first frost, so do not wait until the last minute. Iowa State University Extension agrees — regardless of the method, the plants should be removed from the garden prior to the first frost. The Wisconsin Extension repeats it for each method: dig the plants up before a hard freeze, and for cuttings, take them before frost while the parent plant is still healthy.

### The earlier warning sign

The frost itself is the deadline, but the University of Minnesota Extension gives the earlier signal to watch for: when fall nighttime temperatures begin to cool in late September, consider bringing in the houseplants you kept outside during the summer, because depending on the species, temperatures dipping below 45 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit can cause damage. Geraniums are tender plants, and a plant that has already taken a cold night is a stressed plant. The practical rule is to watch the forecast once nights start cooling, and move the plants on a mild day — not on the day the frost warning appears. A plant moved from cold nights straight into a warm house can suffer shock, and the University of Minnesota Extension warns that an abrupt move indoors can cause yellow leaves, wilt, dieback, or even death, so take it slow and let the plant acclimate a little at a time. That slow move is the part of how to overwinter geraniums most gardeners skip, and it is the part that saves the plant.

### Find your date, then work backwards

The first frost date is an average, not an appointment, and it changes by a month or more across the country. Find your average first frost date with our [Planting Date Calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/), and put the move-in date on the calendar now, while the plant is still healthy and the days are still mild. That date is the real deadline for how to overwinter geraniums, and it arrives before the frost does. If you are weighing the move against the other autumn jobs, our [winterizing guide](/blog/how-to-winterize-a-garden/) covers the rest of the November work, including the beds a geranium is coming out of.

## The three ways to overwinter geraniums

There are three methods, and the extension services all describe the same three. Iowa State University Extension lists them in one sentence: geraniums can be overwintered indoors by potting up individual plants, taking cuttings, or storing bare-root plants in a cool, dry place. Illinois Extension gives the same three: growing them as houseplants, storing them as bare-root plants, or taking cuttings. The University of Wisconsin Extension agrees: bring them indoors as a houseplant, take cuttings, or store them bare-root. The choice in how to overwinter geraniums is not about which method is correct — it is about what you have: window space, a cool spot, and how many plants you want to save.

| Method | What you need | Effort through winter | Reliability |
| ------ | ------------- | --------------------- | ----------- |
| Keep as a houseplant | Bright sunny window, a 6- to 8-inch pot, cool room | Water when dry, pinch occasionally, March cutback | Most straightforward; plant stays alive and growing |
| Take cuttings | Small pots, rooting medium, rooting hormone, bright indirect light | Keep medium moist, wait 4 to 8 weeks for roots | Good; one plant becomes several small ones |
| Store bare-root | Cool dry spot at 45 to 50°F that never freezes, paper bags | Soak roots once a month, restart in March | Least reliable; Wisconsin Extension calls it a challenge |

### Which method fits your space?

The houseplant method suits a gardener with a sunny window and one or two favorite plants. Cuttings suit a gardener short on space: a single plant becomes several small pots that fit on a shelf. Bare-root storage suits a gardener with a cool basement or unheated room and no window space at all — but it is the method most likely to fail, and Wisconsin Extension advises experimenting with several plants and taking cuttings just in case the original plants do not survive. A sensible plan uses more than one method on the same plant: pot up the best plant, take cuttings from the next, and try bare-root storage with the rest. That way a failure in one method does not lose the variety.

### A note on what overwintering does not promise

No method promises spring flowers, and no method promises survival. Illinois Extension's annuals guide warns that saved geraniums will look awful for most of the winter, constantly dropping leaves and getting leggy, with under-sized blooms until the longer days in March. The goal of how to overwinter geraniums is a live plant in spring, not a pretty plant in January. Judge the method in May, when the plant goes back outside, not in February, when it looks its worst.

## How to overwinter geraniums as houseplants

Keeping the whole plant growing indoors is the most straightforward way to learn how to overwinter geraniums, and it is the method to choose when you have a bright window and a plant worth keeping whole. It is also the easiest method if the plant already lives in a pot: you carry it in once you have checked it over. For a plant growing in the ground, you dig it up carefully, take as much of the root ball as you can, and set it into a container with drainage holes filled with fresh potting mix.

### Step 1: Inspect, then discard the weak

Before anything crosses the doorway, look the plant over. Turn the leaves over and look for tiny insects, sticky residue, or webbing, and check the stems for spots or soft, rotting parts. Illinois Extension says to discard any plant with spots or discoloration on leaves and stems, wilting, shriveled or rotting parts, or insects. The University of Minnesota Extension lists the pests to look for — aphids, mealybugs, and whiteflies — and warns that they are not usually a problem while potted plants are outside, but they can quickly turn into a major infestation during the winter if they are brought inside with the plants. Its recommended treatment before the move is to bathe or soak the plant in a bucket of water with mild dishwashing soap, or if the plant is too big for the bucket, to spray it with water to remove outdoor dust and soft-bodied insects, then wash the top and undersides of the leaves with water and dishwashing soap and rinse — making sure the soapy water also gets into the soil, to kill pests living there. Wash the outside of the pots as well. Once the plant is inside, check it with each watering for any sign of infestation, and treat with insecticidal soap until the problem is resolved. That inspection is the least glamorous step in how to overwinter geraniums, and the one that protects every other plant in the house.

### Step 2: Pot it and cut it back

Dig the plant up and place it in a 6- to 8-inch pot, per Iowa State University Extension, with drainage holes in the bottom and fresh potting mix. Then cut it back. The services differ on how hard, and the difference is worth stating plainly. Illinois Extension says to prune the plant back by one-third to one-half when preparing it for indoor life, and the University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: trim the plants back by one-third to one-half. Iowa State University Extension's older guidance cuts harder — prune the geraniums back to one-half to one-third of their original height, which removes a half to two-thirds of the plant. Treat the Illinois and Wisconsin figure as the standard move-in trim, and the Iowa figure as the heavier cut for a large, overgrown plant that will not fit the window otherwise. Either way, the cut makes the plant easier to manage indoors and reduces the leafy growth the roots have to support through a winter of weak light. Water the plant thoroughly after potting. The cut is what makes how to overwinter geraniums fit a windowsill at all.

### Step 3: Give it bright light and a cool room

Light is the make-or-break factor for a geranium kept growing indoors. Set the pot in your brightest, sunniest window, or add a grow light if the windows fall short. Illinois Extension says to treat potted geraniums like other houseplants and place them in a bright, sunny window or provide supplemental lighting. Iowa State University Extension says the same: place the geraniums in a bright, sunny window or under artificial lighting. The University of Minnesota Extension adds the practical detail for the move itself: most likely the plant was exposed to a lot of sun outdoors, so temporarily put it in a very sunny window and gradually reduce its sun exposure before moving it to a less sunny location — the gradual step is what prevents the shock that causes yellow leaves and dieback, and that gradual step is part of how to overwinter geraniums without losing the plant to shock.

### How cool should the room be?

Temperature is a range, not a rule, and the extension services do not all give the same number. Iowa State University Extension gives daytime temperatures near 65 degrees Fahrenheit and nighttime temperatures around 55 degrees as ideal, and its FAQ gives daytime temperatures of 65 to 70 degrees with slightly cooler nights. Illinois Extension says the plants will do best when daytime temperatures are around 65 degrees, with nighttime temperatures slightly cooler. The University of Wisconsin Extension is the coolest of the three: geraniums fare much better in cooler temperatures of 60 to 65 degrees, and should be kept away from heat vents and the fireplace. The shared point is a cool room, not a warm one — a living room in the low 70s is warmer than a geranium wants in winter, and a cool bright room is the goal. A spare room, a sunroom, or an enclosed porch often lands in the right range. Pick the coolest bright room you have: that choice is the temperature rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Step 4: Water by dryness, pinch for shape

Water the plant thoroughly when the soil becomes dry, and do not water on a calendar. Iowa State University Extension says to water the plants thoroughly when the soil becomes dry, and its FAQ says to water when the soil surface becomes dry. Illinois Extension says the pots should be watered when the growing media becomes dry. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: water the geraniums when the soil feels dry to the touch. The shared rule is damp, never soggy — and that is the watering rule inside how to overwinter geraniums. Illinois Extension's annuals guide adds the seasonal detail: cut back on the water through winter, and do not fertilize until the plant begins to put out new growth.

Pinch the plant occasionally to keep it stocky. Iowa State University Extension says to occasionally pinch the geraniums to produce stocky, well-branched plants, and Illinois Extension says the same: plants can be pinched back occasionally to produce well-branched, stocky plants. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives the how: prune or pinch back stems just above a leaf node to produce new growth. Expect the plant to look rough anyway — Illinois Extension warns that a saved geranium will drop leaves constantly and get leggy through winter, with under-sized blooms until the longer days in March. Leaf drop after the move is not proof of failure, and a plant that loses leaves in November can still leaf out again. That patience is part of how to overwinter geraniums — the plant looks worse before it looks better.

### Step 5: Cut it back hard in March

By late winter the plant will likely be tall and lanky, and March is the month the extension services use for the reset. Iowa State University Extension's FAQ is the most specific: in March, prune back the plants, cutting the geraniums back by one-half to two-thirds, and the geraniums will begin to grow again within a few days and should develop into nice specimens by May. Illinois Extension's annuals guide gives the same shape of advice for houseplant geraniums: when new growth begins, prune them back by a half or more for the new growing season. Keep the plant indoors until the threat of frost has passed, then move it outside — the March cutback is the last indoor job in how to overwinter geraniums. Iowa State University Extension says the plants pruned and potted in March should produce green, attractive plants that can be planted outdoors in May.

## How to overwinter geraniums from cuttings

Cuttings are the space-saving way to learn how to overwinter geraniums, and they are the method to choose when the window is full or the parent plant is too big to bring in whole. One healthy plant can give you several new starts, each in a small pot, and the young plants are often more compact and vigorous in spring than a plant that spent the winter as a leggy houseplant. The University of Minnesota Extension lists geraniums first among the plants that can be propagated by cuttings to generate a new crop for next summer, and the University of Wisconsin Extension says the method works especially well if you have limited space and cannot overwinter large pots.

### Step 1: Take the cuttings before frost

Take the cuttings before frost, while the parent plant is still healthy — not after the cold has damaged it. Use a sharp knife or pruning shears and take 3- to 4-inch cuttings from the ends of branches, the terminal shoots. All three services give the same length: Iowa State University Extension says to take 3- to 4-inch stem cuttings from the terminal ends of the shoots; Illinois Extension says to take 3- to 4-inch cuttings from the ends of branches; the University of Wisconsin Extension says to cut off a 3- to 4-inch section of the plant's stem tip. The University of Minnesota Extension agrees at approximately 4 inches long, taken from vigorous, healthy plants. Choose firm, leafy stem tips without flowers or signs of pests, and cut just below a leaf node. A clean cut just below a node is the small detail that makes how to overwinter geraniums from cuttings work.

### Step 2: Strip, dip, and stick

Pinch off the lower leaves, then dip the base of each cutting in a rooting hormone. Iowa State University Extension says to pinch off the lower leaves, dip the base of each cutting in a rooting hormone, and stick the cuttings in a rooting medium of coarse sand or a mixture of coarse sand and sphagnum peat moss. Illinois Extension says to remove the lower leaves, dip the cut end into rooting hormone, and stick the cuttings into a pot with drainage holes, with rooting media like perlite, vermiculite, or a sand-peat moss mixture. The University of Wisconsin Extension lists peat moss, sand, or perlite, or a commercial rooting mix. The University of Minnesota Extension recommends lightweight growing media like vermiculite as best for developing new roots. The University of Florida IFAS Extension adds a detail the northern services do not: cuttings need at least three nodes, and are usually taken in late summer — remove the lower leaves, leaving only the tuft of leaves at the top, and geranium cuttings root well in vermiculite. Rooting hormone mixtures are available at most garden centers, and while it helps, the services treat it as standard practice rather than optional — that hormone dip is standard practice in how to overwinter geraniums from cuttings.

### Stick the cuttings and cover them

Insert the cuttings into the medium just far enough to be self-supporting, and use a pot with drainage holes. Water the cuttings and the medium thoroughly, and let it drain. Then place a clear plastic bag over the cuttings and the container to prevent the cuttings from wilting, as Iowa State University Extension describes — Illinois Extension gives the same step, with a warning to monitor for mold growth under the bag. Place the cuttings in bright light, but not direct sunlight, which can burn them.

### Step 3: Wait 4 to 8 weeks

Rooting takes time, and the services give a spread rather than a single number. Illinois Extension says it can take anywhere from 4 to 8 weeks for the cuttings to root. Iowa State University Extension says the cuttings should root in 6 to 8 weeks. The University of Wisconsin Extension says cuttings should be well-rooted after about 6 weeks. Treat 4 to 8 weeks as the range and 6 weeks as the typical case — and keep the medium moist the whole time, so the cutting does not dry out. That waiting is the hard part of how to overwinter geraniums from cuttings: nothing visible happens for weeks. The University of Minnesota Extension says the same about water: provide a consistent water supply while the cuttings root.

### Step 4: Pot up and grow on

Once the cuttings have good root systems, remove them from the rooting medium and plant each rooted cutting in its own pot. Iowa State University Extension says to plant each rooted cutting in its own pot; Illinois Extension says that once the cuttings have rooted, they can be placed into 3- to 4-inch pots and grown indoors until being brought outdoors; the University of Wisconsin Extension says to transplant each cutting into its own container filled with potting soil, place the newly potted geranium in a bright spot, and water thoroughly when the soil becomes dry. From here the young plants are treated like any indoor geranium: bright light, a cool room, water when dry, and a move outside only after the danger of frost has passed. A cutting taken in fall and rooted by midwinter is a compact, fresh plant by spring — which is why the cutting method is the backup for every other method, too. That backup role is why cuttings belong in any plan for how to overwinter geraniums.

## How to overwinter geraniums bare-root

Bare-root storage is the old method for how to overwinter geraniums — the one grandparents used — and it is also the least reliable of the three. The plant spends winter dormant, without soil, in a cool dark place, and wakes up in March. It asks for almost no space and almost no care, which is its appeal, but it also asks for a spot that stays cool and dry for months, and not every plant survives. The University of Wisconsin Extension is candid about it: storing bare-root geraniums can be a challenge, and you might want to experiment with several plants and take cuttings just in case the original plants do not survive. Use this method for spare plants, not for the one plant you cannot replace.

### Step 1: Lift before frost and strip the soil

Dig the geraniums up before the first frost and carefully shake all the soil from their roots. Iowa State University Extension says to dig the geraniums and carefully shake all the soil from their roots, then hang the plants upside down in a cool, dry place. Illinois Extension says the same: dig them from the ground or remove them from their pots and remove the soil and potting media from their roots. The University of Wisconsin Extension adds the timing again — digging up the plants before a hard freeze — and gently shaking the soil from the roots. Do this while the plant is still healthy: a frost-damaged plant is already stressed, and a stressed plant stores badly. That timing rule — lift before frost, while the plant is healthy — is the first rule of how to overwinter geraniums bare-root.

### Step 2: Bag it or hang it, cool and dry

There are two ways to store the bare plant, and both are published. The first is to place one or two plants in a large paper bag and store it in a cool, dry location. Iowa State University Extension gives the paper sack as the alternate method and calls it much cleaner than hanging; Illinois Extension gives the paper bag as the main method. The University of Wisconsin Extension says you can also store the plants in a large paper bag if you do not have a suitable place to hang them, and to keep the bag open to allow for good air circulation. The second way is to hang the plants upside down from the rafters in a cool, dry location. Iowa State University Extension's county guide describes hanging them upside-down from the rafters in a cool, dark location, and its main article describes hanging the plants upside down in a cool, dry place. Either way, the spot must be cool, dry, and dark, and it must never freeze. That never-freezes rule is the one condition in how to overwinter geraniums bare-root that has no exceptions.

### Step 3: Keep it at 45 to 50°F

The storage temperature is a spread across the services, and the spread itself is the honest figure. Iowa State University Extension's main article gives a cool 45 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit for hanging storage, and Illinois Extension gives the same 45 to 50 degrees for the paper-bag method. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives a slightly wider 45 to 55 degrees, and an Iowa State University Extension FAQ gives the same 45 to 55 degrees for paper-sack storage, suggesting an unheated bedroom or an indoor porch as a suitable location. Treat 45 to 50 degrees as the safest range, and 55 as the warm end — warmer than that, and the plant may sprout weak growth in the dark. A fruit cellar worked well in the Wisconsin educator's grandmother's time because the temperature stayed cool and it was not too dry; today, an unheated room, an indoor porch, a cool basement, or a garage that does not freeze may work. The one absolute is the freeze: the spot must never drop below freezing. That spread — 45 to 50 degrees as the safest range, 55 as the warm end — is the honest answer to the storage question inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Step 4: Soak the roots through winter

A dormant geranium still needs some water, and the services differ on how often. Iowa State University Extension's main article says to soak the roots of each plant in water for 1 to 2 hours once a month during winter, and its county guide says to take the plants out two or three times during the winter and soak the roots in water for 1 or 2 hours. The University of Wisconsin Extension is looser: some gardeners will soak the dormant plant's roots in water for a few hours several times throughout the winter to prevent the roots from completely drying out, then hang the plants back up or place them back in the paper bag. The shared rule is to keep the roots from drying out completely without keeping them wet — a monthly soak of an hour or two is the published schedule, and a plant whose stems have shriveled is a plant that dried out too far. Discard any plant with shriveled stems, since it will most likely die. That monthly soak is the only care a dormant plant needs, and it is the part of how to overwinter geraniums bare-root that is easiest to forget.

### Step 5: Wake it up in March

Expect the stored plant to look rough. Most of the leaves will fall off, and branch tips may die back — Illinois Extension says so directly, and the University of Wisconsin Extension warns not to be surprised when the leaves start to dry up and fall off. That is normal for a dormant plant, not proof of failure. In March, prune or cut back each plant and remove all shriveled, dead material. Iowa State University Extension says to prune back to firm, green, live stem tissue — healthy, live stems will be firm and solid. Illinois Extension gives the same instruction: come March, cut the plants back to firm, green tissue, then plant them in pots and care for them like other houseplants until they can be placed outdoors in May. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives the cut as pruning to about one-third of the plant's original height, or to firm, green, live tissue. After pruning, pot the plants up, water thoroughly, and place them in a sunny window or under artificial lighting. Geraniums pruned and potted in March should produce green, attractive plants that can be planted outdoors in May — but the Wisconsin Extension warns that plants overwintered this way may take a while before they begin growing again, so be patient. It can take several weeks for plants to initiate growth after dormant storage, per the Iowa State county guide. That slow wake-up is normal in how to overwinter geraniums bare-root — judge the plant in May, not in March.

## A worked example: one porch, three plants

A worked example makes the choice concrete. Suppose you have three potted geraniums on a porch and two more in a bed, your average first frost is in mid-October, and you have one sunny south-facing window, a shelf with room for small pots, and a cool basement that holds around 50 degrees all winter. Here is how to overwinter geraniums from that porch, using all three methods at once.

### Late September: inspect and choose

In late September, when the nights start cooling, inspect all five plants. One has spotted leaves and a sticky stem — discard it, per the Illinois Extension rule, rather than carrying its problems indoors. The remaining four are healthy. Check the forecast and pick a mild day before the first frost to do the move.

### Before frost: move, cut, and store

The best potted plant, the one with the color you want to keep, becomes the houseplant. Carry the pot in, cut it back by a third to a half, water it thoroughly, and set it in the south-facing window in the coolest room you have — 60 to 65 degrees if you can manage it, per the Wisconsin Extension. Pinch it occasionally through winter, and cut it back by a half to two-thirds in March, per the Iowa State FAQ, when it gets leggy.

### The bed plants: cuttings and a bare-root trial

The two bed plants become cuttings and a bare-root trial. From the healthiest bed plant, take six 3- to 4-inch stem tips, strip the lower leaves, dip the ends in rooting hormone, and stick them in a perlite and peat mix in small pots under a clear plastic bag, in bright light but not direct sun. At 4 to 8 weeks to root, they should be rooted by December; pot each into its own 3- to 4-inch pot. From the second bed plant, try bare-root storage: lift it before frost, shake the soil from the roots, put it in a large paper bag in the basement at around 50 degrees, soak the roots for an hour or two once a month, and cut it back to firm green tissue in March.

The second potted plant is the spare. If the basement runs warm or the bag method fails, you still have the houseplant and the cuttings. ### March to May: wake up and move out

In March, the houseplant gets its hard cutback, the cuttings are growing on, and the bare-root plant either wakes up or it does not — and if it does not, the cuttings carry the variety into May. When the danger of frost has passed, the plants go outside, spaced 18 to 24 inches apart in the bed per the University of Florida IFAS Extension. That is the whole point of how to overwinter geraniums with all three methods at once: no single failure loses the plant.

## Winter care: light, water, and temperature

The three methods share one winter routine, and it is simpler than most gardeners expect. Light matters most, water matters second, and temperature matters third — and that order is the winter routine inside how to overwinter geraniums. The common mistake is getting all three backwards, with a warm, dim room and a plant watered on a schedule.

### Light

A geranium that keeps growing wants the brightest spot you have. A south-facing window is the usual first choice, and a grow light fills the gap if the windows fall short. The University of Minnesota Extension warns that the move itself needs care: a plant that spent the summer in full sun should go into a very sunny window first, with its sun exposure reduced gradually, or the shock shows as yellow leaves and dieback. For cuttings, the light is bright but not direct — direct sun can burn a cutting that has no roots yet. For a dormant bare-root plant, light does not matter at all, because the plant is resting in the dark; what matters is that the spot stays cool. That difference — bright for growing plants, dark for dormant ones — is the light rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Water

Water by dryness, never by the calendar. For a houseplant, water thoroughly when the soil becomes dry — Iowa State, Illinois, and Wisconsin all give the same instruction in different words. For cuttings, keep the medium moist so the cutting does not dry out, and water thoroughly after sticking them. For a dormant plant, soak the roots for an hour or two once a month. Through winter generally, cut back on the water and do not fertilize until the plant begins to put out new growth, per Illinois Extension's annuals guide — a geranium fed in January in a dim room grows weak, leggy stems that will be cut off in March anyway. That restraint — water by dryness, feed nothing until spring growth — is the winter feeding rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Temperature

Keep the growing plants cool and the dormant plants cold-but-never-freezing. For a houseplant, the services give 60 to 70 degrees across the board — Wisconsin at 60 to 65, Illinois around 65 with cooler nights, Iowa near 65 by day and around 55 at night, and its FAQ at 65 to 70 with slightly cooler nights. Plan for the cool end of that range: 60 to 65 degrees in bright light. For a dormant plant, 45 to 50 degrees is the published range, with 55 as the warm end. Keep growing plants away from heat vents and fireplaces, which dry the air and push the temperature up — the Wisconsin Extension names both. That cool-room rule is the temperature rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### The garage as a middle way

A garage or shed that gets cold but never freezes is a fourth option, between the houseplant and the bare-root method. Illinois Extension describes it directly: large container-grown annuals like geraniums can be put in a garage or shed that gets cold but does not freeze, ever, where they overwinter as live but semi-dormant plants. Keep the soil just moist, not letting the potting medium shrink and pull away from the pot edges. In spring, when other indoor plants are starting to grow, prune the plant back to 4 to 6 inches, start giving it more light, and move it outside when it is safe. This is the method for a gardener with a cold garage and no window space — but the word ever matters. A garage that freezes on the coldest night of the year is not a storage spot, and a plant left there will not survive it. That ever matters: the garage method inside how to overwinter geraniums only works where the cold never reaches freezing.

## Troubleshooting overwintered geraniums

Most winter problems in how to overwinter geraniums have one of three causes: too little light, too much water, or a pest that came in with the plant. The fixes are small, but they only work if the cause is named correctly.

### Leggy, stretched stems

Leggy growth is the most common complaint, and the cause is warm air plus weak light. Iowa State University Extension says geraniums become tall and spindly when grown in warm, poorly lit areas. Illinois Extension says they will become lanky when grown in warm conditions and not given enough light. The University of Wisconsin Extension says they fare much better in cooler temperatures of 60 to 65 degrees, and that plants start looking leggy when light is reduced. The fix is to move the plant to the brightest window, keep the room cool, and pinch the stems back just above a leaf node to produce new growth — and in March, cut the plant back hard, by one-half to two-thirds per the Iowa State FAQ, so the spring growth starts from a compact frame. A plant that is leggy in February is not a failed plant; it is a plant waiting for its March cutback. That March cutback is the fix the extension services agree on inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Leaves dropping after the move

Leaf drop after coming indoors is normal, and it is not proof of failure. The University of Minnesota Extension warns that an abrupt move from outdoors to indoors can cause yellow leaves, wilt, dieback, or even death, which is why the move should be gradual — a very sunny window first, then a less sunny spot. Illinois Extension's annuals guide says the same about the whole winter: saved geraniums will be messy, slowly dropping leaves, and have under-sized blooms until the longer days in March. For a dormant bare-root plant, leaf drop is the method working as described — most of the leaves fall off in storage, and the plant regrows from firm, green stem tissue in March. Worry when the stems go soft or shriveled, not when the leaves fall. That distinction — leaves falling is normal, stems shriveling is not — is the diagnostic rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves have more than one cause, and the extension sources split them usefully. A lower leaf turning yellow and falling on a plant allowed to get too dry is ordinary — the University of Florida IFAS Extension notes that the lower leaves will turn yellow and fall if the plant is allowed to get too dry. Yellow leaves on a plant kept wet through winter point the other way, toward root rot from overwatering, which the Wisconsin rosemary guidance names as the biggest cause of decline — and the same wet-soil warning applies to geraniums, which want water when the soil is dry, not a schedule. Check the soil before watering: if it still feels damp to the touch, wait. That finger test is the watering check inside how to overwinter geraniums. And check the light: a plant in a dim corner yellows from the bottom up as it sheds the leaves it cannot support.

### Pests indoors

Aphids, mealybugs, and whiteflies are the pests the University of Minnesota Extension names for plants brought indoors, and the University of Florida IFAS Extension adds the geranium-specific list: aphids suck plant juices and heavy infestations cause distorted growth; greenhouse whitefly and sweet potato whitefly can rapidly build up, and early detection and control is necessary; four-lined plant bug causes small, round, sunken, brown spots on the leaves. Check the plant with each watering, looking under the leaves, and treat with insecticidal soap until the problem is resolved — that is the Minnesota Extension's instruction. Bacterial leaf spot causes spots on the leaves, worse on the lower leaves, and the Florida guidance is to use proper spacing and pick off and destroy infected leaves. Keep a new plant apart from the other houseplants for the first few weeks, so a pest that came in with it does not spread before you notice it. That quarantine is the pest rule inside how to overwinter geraniums.

### Mold under the plastic bag

Cuttings kept under a clear plastic bag can grow mold, and Illinois Extension warns to monitor for mold growth when the bag is on. The fix is air: lift the bag for a while each day, or take it off once the cuttings have stopped wilting, and keep the medium moist but not wet. If mold appears, remove the affected leaves, let the surface dry slightly, and put the bag back looser. A cutting that rots at the stem has usually been kept too wet in a medium with no drainage — which is why every service specifies pots with drainage holes. That drainage rule is the mold rule inside how to overwinter geraniums from cuttings.

### Cuttings that will not root

A cutting that sits for eight weeks with no roots has usually failed for one of three reasons: the cutting was taken from a weak or flowering stem, the medium stayed dry, or the light was too dim. Take cuttings from vigorous, healthy plants, about 4 inches long, per the University of Minnesota Extension, and keep the medium moist in bright light but not direct sun. The University of Florida IFAS Extension adds that cuttings need at least three nodes, and that stem rot or blackleg attacks cuttings — the stems turn black with rot — so use healthy cuttings and sterile rooting media. If one batch fails, take more cuttings from the parent plant while it is still healthy; a second batch in fresh, sterile mix often succeeds where the first did not. That second batch is the recovery plan inside how to overwinter geraniums from cuttings.

## Moving geraniums back outside in spring

The return trip in how to overwinter geraniums has its own deadline: after the danger of frost has passed. Iowa State University Extension says the geraniums pruned and potted in March should produce green, attractive plants that can be planted outdoors in May, and Illinois Extension says the same — care for the potted plants like houseplants until they can be placed outdoors in May. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to keep the potted geraniums indoors until the threat of frost has passed, then plant them outdoors or show them off in a sunlit container garden. Treat May as the regional example from the northern services, not a date for every part of the country: the rule is your local last frost date, and the [Planting Date Calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) gives it from your own frost date.

### Harden the plant off

Do not move a plant from a windowsill straight into full sun and wind. The University of Minnesota Extension's warning about the autumn move applies in reverse: an abrupt change can cause yellow leaves, wilt, dieback, or even death, so take it slow and let the plant acclimate a little at a time. Set the plant outside for a few hours a day in a sheltered spot, bring it in at night, and lengthen the time outdoors over a week or two before it stays out. Give it more light gradually as the days lengthen, per the Illinois Extension's spring advice for garage-kept plants. When it goes into the bed, space it 18 to 24 inches from its neighbors, per the University of Florida IFAS Extension, in full to partial sun in loamy, well-drained soil. That slow hardening-off is the last step in how to overwinter geraniums.

### Repot when the mix is spent

A geranium kept as a houseplant for several winters will eventually need fresh potting mix. Illinois Extension's annuals guide says that after 3 or 4 years, the potting mix of the large annuals will become hard and compacted, and in the spring you should remove the plant from its container and either shake off the old soil and repot it in a slightly larger pot with new potting mix, or prune the roots and repot it in the same pot with fresh mix. Spring is the moment to do it — the plant is about to grow again, and fresh mix gives the roots something to grow into. That spring repot is the maintenance step inside how to overwinter geraniums for plants kept for several winters.

## Warm-climate exception: Florida and the Gulf Coast

Not every gardener needs to bring geraniums in. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says geraniums can be used nearly year-round in warm climates, and do best in cool weather in most parts of central and southern Florida. Planting in a full to partial sun location in the landscape can be done from October to about March in south and central Florida, and in the spring after the danger of frost in the north and western part of Florida. In north Florida, if plants can be brought indoors on cold nights, geraniums perform nicely most of the winter in a full sun location. The same source notes that flowering fades by mid to late summer, and to cut plants back in north Florida in early fall and fertilize to stimulate new growth — plants should begin to grow and do nicely again in the fall and winter. If you garden where frost is rare, the winter question is not how to overwinter geraniums indoors but whether to plant them for the cool season instead — and our [seed-starting guide](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/) covers the indoor setup for the rest of the garden while the geraniums stay outside.

## The short version

How to overwinter geraniums comes down to three decisions made before the first frost: which plants are healthy enough to save, which method fits the space you have, and where the plant will spend the winter. A healthy plant in a bright, cool window, watered when dry and cut back hard in March, is the simplest version. A handful of 3- to 4-inch cuttings in a small pot is the version for a full windowsill. A bare-root plant in a paper bag at 45 to 50 degrees is the version for a cool basement — the cheapest in space and the riskiest in outcome. Use more than one method on the same variety, expect the plant to look rough by February, and judge the work in May, when it goes back outside. Our [rosemary overwintering guide](/blog/how-to-overwinter-rosemary/) follows the same pattern for a plant that stays outside in mild zones — the two guides together cover the tender plants most gardeners actually grow.

Square foot gardening works by dividing a bed into one-foot squares with a physical grid, then
planting each square with a single crop at a set density — **16 radishes, 9 carrots, 4 lettuces,
1 pepper, or one tomato per two squares**. Because you never walk on the bed, the paths that rows
need disappear and the whole surface grows food.

## Where the per-square numbers come from

This is worth being clear about, because almost no page that ranks for this question says it.

Those densities are **Mel Bartholomew's square foot gardening convention**, as described by
[Cornell CALS](https://cals.cornell.edu/school-integrative-plant-science/school-sections/horticulture-section/outreach-and-extension/pandemic-vegetable-gardening/pandemic-vegetable-gardening-2021-archive/square-foot-gardening).
They are a method for laying out an intensively amended bed. They are not extension spacing
research, and they are not the same thing as the row spacings published for field growing.

They work well on their own terms — deep, rich, evenly watered soil that is never compacted —
and badly as general advice. So it is worth knowing which number you are using.

## Building the bed

Four by four feet is the classic size: sixteen squares, reachable from all sides, and enough to
be worth the effort. Four by eight is the other common shape and fits standard timber.

Do not go wider than four feet. The method depends on never standing on the bed, and a bed wider
than four feet cannot be reached to the middle from either side.

Depth: six inches is the original specification and works on open soil. Twelve inches is more
forgiving and lets you grow [carrots](/crops/carrot/) properly —
[how deep should a raised bed be for vegetables](/blog/how-deep-should-a-raised-bed-be/) works
through the trade-off.

Make the grid physical rather than imagining it. It changes how you plant — you fill a square
rather than scattering seed — and it makes succession obvious, because an empty square is
visibly empty.

## Is square foot gardening worth it?

For a small garden, yes. For a large one, it gets in the way. Here is the honest split.

**Where it helps**

- **Small gardens.** Removing paths from a small bed adds a surprising amount of growing area.
- **Beginners.** The grid turns "how far apart?" into "how many in this square?", which is a much
  easier question.
- **Planning and rotation.** A grid is a record. You can see at a glance what was where.
- **Succession.** An empty square is an obvious prompt to sow something.
- **Weeding.** Dense planting shades the soil and suppresses weeds without mulch.

**Where it gets in the way**

- **Sprawling crops.** Squash, melons and pumpkins do not fit the model. Giving a zucchini ten
  squares in a sixteen-square bed defeats the point of the bed.
- **Long rows of one thing.** If you want fifty leeks or a serious potato crop, rows are simply
  better. The grid adds bookkeeping and nothing else.
- **Cost.** Mel's Mix — equal parts compost, peat and vermiculite — is expensive per cubic foot,
  and the method is usually presented as requiring it. It does not.
- **Rigidity.** Some crops want a wide, shallow footprint rather than a square one. Forcing
  everything into squares occasionally costs you yield.
- **Watering.** Dense planting means more competition for water. A square foot bed dries faster
  than a sparsely planted one of the same size.

The method is best understood as a layout convention for a small, rich, well-watered bed, not a
complete gardening system. Used that way it is genuinely good.

## Common mistakes

**Ignoring the big-crop numbers.** One zucchini per square is the commonest, and it ruins the
squares around it.

**Treating six inches as enough everywhere.** Fine on open soil for salad and herbs. Not fine on
a patio, and not fine for carrots.

**Planting all sixteen squares on the same day.** You then harvest all sixteen in the same
fortnight. Stagger it — [succession planting schedules](/blog/succession-planting-schedule/)
covers how.

**Buying the exact mix.** Peat has an environmental cost and vermiculite is hard to source in
quantity. A good bed mix with plenty of compost grows the same vegetables — see
[what to fill raised garden beds with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/).

## If your garden is bigger than a couple of beds

Use the grid where it earns its place and rows where they do. A garden can have a square foot
bed for salads and herbs near the door and conventional rows for potatoes and leeks further out.

[How to plan a vegetable garden layout](/blog/planning-a-vegetable-garden-layout/) covers the
wider decisions — bed width, orientation, paths and rotation — that the grid does not touch.

The poinsettia on the kitchen table in mid-November is six weeks from Christmas, and the question behind it is how to care for poinsettia so the color is still there on Christmas morning — not faded in the first week of December. Most poinsettias do not die from disease. They die from the ride home in a cold car, from water standing in the foil, or from a spot next to a heat register, and all three are preventable once you know what the plant actually wants. This guide to how to care for poinsettia follows the order the extension services recommend: pick a healthy plant, get it home without a chill, give it the right light, temperature, and water, and then decide whether to compost it in January or run the rebloom schedule for next Christmas.

The plant is more popular than its reputation for fussiness suggests. Clemson Cooperative Extension puts the scale at more than 70 million poinsettias sold nationwide each year, and Iowa State University Extension calls it the number one flowering potted plant in the United States. It is also older than the holiday habit: the plant is native to Mexico, where it grows as a shrub or small tree 10 to 15 feet tall, and it was brought to the United States in the 1820s by Joel Roberts Poinsett, the first US minister to Mexico. That history is the first clue to how to care for poinsettia — it is a tropical plant that happens to be sold in winter, and everything it wants is warmth, bright light, and soil that drains.

## How to care for poinsettia: the five rules

Every care decision in how to care for poinsettia comes back to five rules, and the numbers below are the ones extension services publish — not folk rules:

- **Light: at least 6 hours of bright, indirect light a day.** Clemson Cooperative Extension says to give the plant at least 6 hours of bright, indirect sunlight each day. The University of Minnesota Extension says the same in its own words: bright daylight near a south-, east-, or west-facing window.
- **Temperature: 60 to 70°F, never below 50°F.** Iowa State University Extension gives 60 to 70°F as the preferred range, and the University of Minnesota Extension agrees at 65 to 70°F. Below 50°F the plant takes chilling injury and drops leaves, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and freezing temperatures kill it, per the University of Minnesota Extension.
- **Water: check daily, water through, never let it stand.** Iowa State University Extension says to check the potting soil daily and water when the surface becomes dry to the touch, until water flows out the bottom of the pot — and to take the plant out of its decorative pot cover first, water it in the sink, and set it back only after it has drained.
- **No fertilizer while it is blooming.** Clemson Cooperative Extension says not to fertilize a poinsettia when it is in bloom, and the University of Minnesota Extension says the same.
- **No cold, no drafts, no heat blasting across it.** Iowa State University Extension says to keep the plant away from cold drafts and heat sources such as exterior doors that open and shut or heat registers, and Clemson Cooperative Extension adds fireplaces and ventilating ducts to the same list.

Memorize the five, and the rest of how to care for poinsettia is just applying them.

### What the five rules buy you

Get all five right and the plant stays attractive for several weeks, well after the Christmas and New Year's Day holidays — that is Iowa State University Extension's promise, and it is the whole answer to how to care for poinsettia in the holiday window. Miss on water alone and the same plant fails fast: Iowa State University Extension says improper watering is the primary cause of most poinsettia problems in the home, and that overwatering can lead to root rot that makes the entire plant wilt and collapse, sometimes rapidly. The five rules are the whole method for how to care for poinsettia through the holidays; everything else in this guide is how to hit them, and what to do with the plant once the holidays are over.

## Choosing a healthy poinsettia at the store

How to care for poinsettia starts before you buy it, because a tired plant at the store never recovers into a good one at home, however well you water it. Iowa State University Extension gives the selection checklist, and it is worth using in the aisle rather than guessing:

- **Dark green foliage and brightly colored bracts.** The leaves and bracts should not have yellow or brown spots or edges.
- **True flowers that have not opened yet.** The true flowers are the yellow to green, button-like objects in the center of the colorful bracts, called cyathia, and Iowa State University Extension says they should be shedding little or no pollen. A plant already shedding pollen is near the end of its display, because the bracts drop after pollination.
- **Full lower leaves.** Avoid poinsettias with wilted foliage or few to no lower leaves — a bare stem at the bottom is a plant that has already been stressed.
- **Unbroken stems.** The branches break easily when moved, so check for broken or partially broken stems and handle the plant carefully after purchase.
- **No insects.** Check the plant for insects before you buy it; whiteflies are the most common pest, and Iowa State University Extension warns that no one wants to bring an insect-infested poinsettia into the home.

### Clemson's checklist agrees

Clemson Cooperative Extension adds the same checks in its own list: look for fully mature, thoroughly colored bracts; dark, rich green foliage all the way down the stem; a plant that is balanced, full, and attractive from all sides; stiff stems with good bract and leaf retention and no signs of wilting, breaking, or drooping; and yellow flowers in the center that are not quite open. A plant that passes all five checks is the right starting point for how to care for poinsettia — a plant that fails one is a plant you will be nursing from day one.

### The color is a free choice

The color itself is a free choice — modern varieties come in red, pink, white, and gold, with marbled and bicolored types as well, per Iowa State University Extension — but Clemson Cooperative Extension notes that red still accounts for over three-quarters of sales. One more thing to know at the store: the blue and purple poinsettias are cream-colored varieties that have been spray-painted, per the University of Minnesota Extension, sometimes sprinkled with glitter. They are not a variety, and the paint does not change how to care for poinsettia — the plant underneath still wants the same five rules.

## Getting it home without a chill

The most common way to kill a poinsettia is the trip home, and it happens before the plant ever reaches your table. That cold-ride warning is the first step in how to care for poinsettia, because the damage is done in minutes and cannot be undone. Iowa State University Extension says to purchase the poinsettia at the end of your shopping trip, place it in a plant sleeve or carefully wrap it before going outdoors, and set it in a heated vehicle. Most garden centers and greenhouses will wrap the plant for you, but many big box stores do not — you may have to request a bag or sleeve and wrap it yourself. Exposure to freezing temperatures, even for a brief moment, may cause the leaves to blacken and drop.

### Why a brief chill is enough

The details matter because the plant is tropical. Clemson Cooperative Extension says chilling injury can cause premature leaf drop if the temperature drops below 50°F, and the University of Minnesota Extension says poinsettias suffer damage below 50°F and freezing temperatures kill them. That means a plant carried unwrapped across a cold parking lot in November, or left in a cold car while you finish shopping at other stores, can lose its leaves within the week — and Iowa State University Extension is blunt that there is no cure for cold damage once it happens. Make the poinsettia the last thing you buy before going home, move it from the warm store directly to a warm car and then into your warm house as quickly as possible, and do not leave it in the car. Once home, unwrap it carefully — Iowa State University Extension says it is usually best to cut off the protective sleeve rather than slide it down and off, which risks snapping the brittle stems. That careful unwrapping is the last step in getting the plant home safely, and it is part of how to care for poinsettia that most people skip.

## Light: at least six hours of bright, indirect light

Light is the easiest of the five rules to get right, and the one most often got wrong by putting the plant in the wrong room. The rule in how to care for poinsettia is bright but not direct: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to set the plant in a bright location so it receives at least 6 hours of bright, indirect sunlight each day, and warns that putting it in direct sunlight may fade the color of the bracts — if direct sun cannot be avoided, filter it with a light shade or sheer curtain. The University of Minnesota Extension gives the same placement in window terms: a south-, east-, or west-facing window where the plant receives bright daylight. NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox agrees on the outdoor version of the same rule — the plant grows best in part shade — and says that a plant set outdoors should get 4 to 5 hours of sun per day.

### Two placement mistakes

Two placement mistakes undo good light. The first is the cold window pane: Iowa State University Extension says not to let the plant touch the cold window pane, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says not to let the foliage touch the cold glass, because that will damage the plant. A bright window is right; leaves pressed against freezing glass are not. The second is the dark corner: a poinsettia in a dim hallway is a poinsettia dropping leaves, and Clemson Cooperative Extension lists poor light among the factors that cause premature leaf drop. If the only bright spot in the house is a cold window, pull the plant back a few inches from the glass and let the light reach it without the touch — that small gap is the practical version of the light rule inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is the difference between a plant that holds its color and one that sheds.

### What about a grow light?

A grow light is a reasonable substitute if the house has no bright window, and the logic is the same as the window rule: bright, indirect, and on for most of the day. Our [seed-starting guide](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/) covers the light hours and distances for indoor growing in general, and the same idea applies here — the plant wants a bright spot for at least 6 hours a day, not a dim corner with a lamp switched on for an hour at night. Keep the light above the plant rather than beside it, and keep the plant away from the heat a lamp throws off, for the same reason you keep it away from a heat register. A grow light does not change how to care for poinsettia; it only changes where the bright spot is.

## Temperature: 60 to 70°F, never below 50°F

Temperature is where the extension services differ slightly, and the honest version of how to care for poinsettia is a spread rather than a single number. Iowa State University Extension gives 60 to 70°F as the preferred range. The University of Minnesota Extension gives 65 to 70°F as ideal, and NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox agrees at 65 to 70°F. Clemson Cooperative Extension gives 65 to 75°F on its holiday-care page, but its own factsheet says the daytime temperature should not exceed 70°F — so Clemson's two pages bracket the same band from above and below. The University of Florida IFAS Extension gives 65°F at night and 75 to 80°F during the day, but that is for poinsettias grown as Florida landscape plants, not for a potted plant in a living room, and it should not be read as the indoor target.

### The honest temperature split: 60–70°F indoors, never below 50°F

Put the spread together and the planning figure is 60 to 70°F indoors — Iowa State University Extension's range, which contains Minnesota's and NC State's 65 to 70°F and sits under Clemson's 70°F daytime ceiling. That is the range to aim for in how to care for poinsettia, and it is also the range most heated homes already hold in December. The hard floor is where every source agrees: do not let the temperature drop below 50°F. Clemson Cooperative Extension says chilling injury is a problem and can cause premature leaf drop below 50°F, the University of Minnesota Extension says poinsettias suffer damage below 50°F, and freezing temperatures kill them. The other direction matters too: Clemson Cooperative Extension says excess heat causes the leaves to yellow and fall off and the flower bracts to fade early, so a room that runs hot shortens the display just as surely as a cold one. The practical test is the spot, not the thermostat: if the plant sits where a cold draft crosses it from an exterior door, or where dry air blows across it from a heat register, a fireplace, or a ventilating duct, move it — Iowa State University Extension and Clemson Cooperative Extension both name those exact spots as the ones that cause leaf drop. A steady 65°F in a bright room away from drafts and heat is the whole temperature answer inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is easier to hold than most people expect.

## Watering: check daily, water through, never let it stand

Watering is where most poinsettias are lost, and it is the one rule in how to care for poinsettia that has no schedule — the pot decides. Iowa State University Extension says to check the potting soil daily, and to water when the soil surface becomes dry to the touch, until water begins to flow out the bottom of the pot. The University of Minnesota Extension gives the same rule with a second test added: water when the soil surface feels dry to a light touch, or when the pot feels lightweight when you lift it — and never allow the plant to get so dry that it wilts. Clemson Cooperative Extension agrees: water thoroughly when the soil surface feels dry to the touch, and never let the potting mixture completely dry out.

### The method: out of the foil, water through, drain

The method matters as much as the timing, and every source describes the same three steps. First, take the plant out of its decorative foil or pot cover: Iowa State University Extension says to carefully remove the pot cover, water the plant in the sink, and then set it back into the pot cover, and the University of Minnesota Extension says to remove the plant from decorative foil or outer pot before watering. If the foil has no drainage hole, punch one in the bottom or take the pot out entirely — Clemson Cooperative Extension gives the same choice, remove the foil or punch a hole in the bottom to prevent water retention. Second, water until it runs through: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to water until water seeps out of the drainage hole and the soil is completely saturated, and Iowa State University Extension says the same, until water begins to flow out the bottom. Third, let it drain completely and empty the saucer: the University of Minnesota Extension says never allow poinsettia pots to sit in excess water, because constant wetness will rot plant roots, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says never let the plant sit in standing water. A dribble on top every day does the opposite of what you want — it wets the surface while the roots stay dry, and it leaves water standing in the foil, which is the fastest route to root rot. Those three steps — out of the foil, water through, drain completely — are the watering half of how to care for poinsettia, and they take less than a minute.

### The two watering failures, and how to tell them apart

Both failures show up as dropped leaves, which is why the finger test matters more than the calendar. Over-watering is the more common one: Iowa State University Extension says improper watering is the primary cause of most poinsettia problems in the home, and that keeping plants too wet — watering too frequently or allowing water to collect in the decorative pot cover — often results in the yellowing and loss of the lower leaves, and can lead to root rot that makes the entire plant wilt and collapse, sometimes rapidly. Under-watering shows up differently: Iowa State University Extension says leaves will curl and drop when plants are allowed to get too dry, and the University of Minnesota Extension says never to let the plant get so dry that it wilts. So read the plant before you reach for the can: yellow lower leaves on wet soil mean too much water; curled, wilting leaves on dry soil mean too little. In most heated rooms the finger test lands at roughly once a week, but that is an observation, not a rule — a small pot by a heat source dries faster than a large one in a cool room, and the pot, not the calendar, is what decides. That finger test is the watering rule inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is the cheapest tool in the whole guide.

### The foil trap

The decorative foil deserves its own warning, because it is the single most common cause of a poinsettia dying in January. The foil looks like part of the pot, but it is a watertight sleeve, and every drop that drains out of the pot collects inside it. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says to remove any excess water from the saucer or decorative pot sleeve because poinsettias do not tolerate soggy soil, and NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox says to water when the soil surface feels dry but not to overwater, with a well-drained potting mix of loam, organic matter, and peat moss. The fix is simple and takes ten seconds: lift the pot out of the foil, water it in the sink, let it drain until it stops dripping, and only then set it back. If you would rather leave the pot in the foil, punch a hole in the bottom of the foil and set the whole thing on a saucer you empty after every watering. Either way, the rule is the same — the pot must never stand in water — and that rule is the part of how to care for poinsettia that saves more plants than any other single habit.

## What the red parts actually are — and why it matters

The red parts of a poinsettia are not its flowers, and knowing that changes how you judge the plant. The colorful parts are modified leaves called bracts, and the true flowers are the small yellow to green, button-like objects in the center of the bracts, called cyathia — that is Iowa State University Extension's description, and the University of Minnesota Extension says the same: the colorful bracts attract insects to the flowers and will drop after pollination. Clemson Cooperative Extension adds the history: the true flowers, called cyathia, are small clusters of yellow blooms in the open center of the red floral bracts, surrounded by red bracts that evolved their color to attract insects for pollination. The University of Florida IFAS Extension agrees — the colorful and showy "flower" is actually a cluster of modified leaves called bracts, and the true flowers are small and clustered in the centers of the bracts.

### Why it matters at the store

That matters for two practical reasons. First, it tells you how to judge the plant at the store: a plant whose cyathia are still closed has weeks of color ahead of it, because the bracts drop after the flowers are pollinated, and a plant already shedding pollen is near the end of its display. The cyathia check at the store is the most useful single test in how to care for poinsettia — closed buttons mean weeks of color left. That bracts-are-not-flowers fact is the piece of botany inside how to care for poinsettia that explains both the shopping choice and the January fade. Second, it explains why the color fades even on a well-cared-for plant: once the true flowers have done their work, the bracts have served their purpose and fall. The bracts are the display, not the plant, and a poinsettia that has lost its color has not necessarily died — it has finished flowering, and the green plant underneath can be kept, pruned, and brought back into color next year. That distinction is the quiet fact inside how to care for poinsettia that stops people throwing away a healthy plant in January.

### The color range

The color range is wider than the red on the table. Modern varieties come in red, pink, white, and gold, with marbled and bicolored types, per Iowa State University Extension, and the University of Florida IFAS Extension adds burgundy, peach, and yellow to the list. The spray-painted blue ones are the novelty inside how to care for poinsettia, and the paint changes nothing about the care. Whatever color you choose, the care is the same — color is a variety choice inside how to care for poinsettia, not a care choice. Clemson Cooperative Extension notes that red still accounts for over three-quarters of sales, and that the variety 'Prestige' accounts for the majority of the red poinsettias sold in the United States. The wild plant behind all of them is a different creature: in its native Mexico it grows 10 to 15 feet tall with only one or two stems, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox says it can grow up to 10 feet high and 8 feet wide in its native habitat, while a potted plant rarely exceeds 2 to 3 feet. The compact, branching plant on your table is the product of a century of breeding, and the oldest named variety still in use, 'Oakleaf', was developed in 1923, per Clemson Cooperative Extension.

## After Christmas: compost it, or keep it

Once the holidays are over, every poinsettia owner faces the same choice, and the extension services are honest that both answers are reasonable. Iowa State University Extension says it plainly: poinsettias are intended to be temporary plants, and you should compost or dispose of the plant after the holidays when you have grown tired of it or it becomes unattractive. There is no shame in that — the plant did its job, and a new one next November costs less than the effort of keeping this one. But for gardeners who enjoy a challenge, Iowa State University Extension says it is possible to get a poinsettia to bloom again next season by following the steps below, and the University of Minnesota Extension agrees the plant can be grown as an attractive green plant throughout the year. The choice is yours, and the rest of this guide is for the people who choose to keep it — which is the longer version of how to care for poinsettia, running from January to next December.

### The first months if you keep it

If you keep it, the first months are simple. Iowa State University Extension says to provide good care from December to March — a bright location with consistent temperatures, watered when needed — and the University of Minnesota Extension adds to fertilize if you see new growth, and to keep providing adequate light and water for a prolonged bloom for several weeks. Do not fertilize while it is still in bloom, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, but once new green growth appears, the University of Minnesota Extension says to start fertilizing with an all-purpose household plant fertilizer, mixed at half the recommended strength, every 3 to 4 weeks. That feeding schedule is the bridge between the holiday plant and the growing plant, and it is the part of how to care for poinsettia that most keepers get backwards — they feed it in December, when it wants nothing, and starve it in spring, when it wants food.

## A worked example: one poinsettia, 13 November to next Christmas

A worked example makes the calendar concrete. Suppose you buy a poinsettia on Friday 13 November 2026, six weeks before Christmas, and you want it colorful on Christmas Day and colorful again next Christmas. The arithmetic below uses the extension services' own dates, and the worked example is the calendar inside how to care for poinsettia.

### 13 November: the purchase

You buy the plant last on your shopping trip, sleeve it, and take it straight to a heated car and then into the house, per Iowa State University Extension. At the store you chose a plant with dark green foliage, bright bracts, no yellow or brown spots, and cyathia shedding little or no pollen. Christmas is 42 days away, and with good care the plant stays attractive for several weeks past Christmas and New Year's Day, per Iowa State University Extension — so the color you bought on 13 November should carry you into January. That 42-day runway is the reason mid-November is the right moment to start how to care for poinsettia for Christmas. That unhurried trip home is the first dated entry in how to care for poinsettia for this plant, and it costs nothing.

### 13 November to January: the holiday window

The plant sits near a south-facing window, a few inches back from the cold glass, at 60 to 70°F, per Iowa State University Extension. You check the soil daily with your finger and water when the surface feels dry, lifting the pot out of the foil, watering in the sink until water runs out the bottom, letting it drain, and setting it back. You do not fertilize — Clemson Cooperative Extension says not to fertilize a poinsettia when it is in bloom. Those six weeks of steady care are the holiday window inside how to care for poinsettia, and they are the easiest weeks of the whole year. By Christmas the bracts are still bright, because the cyathia had not yet opened when you bought the plant, and the bracts drop only after pollination, per the University of Minnesota Extension.

### March: the cut-back

When the colorful bracts fade, you prune. Iowa State University Extension says to cut the stems back to within 4 to 6 inches of the soil in March, and to repot at the same time if the plant needs it; Clemson Cooperative Extension says to prune back to about 8 inches in March to April, and the University of Minnesota Extension says to cut back to about five inches tall around Valentine's Day to promote compact growth. The honest range is 4 to 8 inches, and the planning figure is 4 to 6 inches — Iowa State University Extension's number, which the University of Florida IFAS Extension also gives for cutting off fading bracts. The plant looks bare after pruning, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, but new growth emerges from the nodes up and down the stem. The 4-to-6-inch cut is the planning figure for how to care for poinsettia after the holidays, and it is the one the worked example uses. That bare-looking plant in March is the start of the second year inside how to care for poinsettia, not the end of the first. When that new growth appears, you move the plant to a sunny window, keep watering when the soil feels dry, and fertilize every 2 weeks with a dilute fertilizer solution, per Iowa State University Extension — or every 3 to 4 weeks at half strength with an all-purpose houseplant fertilizer, per the University of Minnesota Extension. If you are repotting, our [potting soil guide](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) covers the mix, and the University of Minnesota Extension says to use a container about 2 to 4 inches bigger than the original pot, with a mix that has a good amount of organic matter such as peat moss and drains well.

### Late May: outdoors for the summer

Once the night temperature remains above 50°F, you move the plant outdoors, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. Iowa State University Extension says to harden it first — a shady, protected area for 2 or 3 days, then gradually longer periods of direct sun, properly hardened in 7 to 10 days — and then to dig a hole in an area that receives 6 to 8 hours of sunlight and set the pot into the ground. The University of Minnesota Extension gives the same move in holiday terms: move the plant outside for the summer around Father's Day, in indirect light at first, and move it into full sun around the Fourth of July. To keep it compact and bushy rather than large and lanky, Iowa State University Extension says to pinch or cut off the shoot tips once or twice from late June to mid-August. Clemson Cooperative Extension agrees — pinch back the shoot tips or prune back the branches — and adds the cutoff: do not pinch back after September 1. The University of Florida IFAS Extension gives the same discipline in its own numbers: cut back once a month if needed, leaving four leaves on each shoot, and discontinue pruning after September 10. Water and fertilize through the summer: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to fertilize every two to three weeks during spring, summer, and fall with a well-balanced complete fertilizer such as 10-10-10. The 7-to-10-day hardening is the patient part of how to care for poinsettia outdoors — a plant moved straight into full sun scorches. That summer outside is the growing season inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is what builds the branches that carry next Christmas.

### Mid-September: back indoors

Iowa State University Extension says to bring the plant indoors in mid-September and place it in a bright, sunny window. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to bring it in when night temperatures become cool, 55 to 60°F. The University of Minnesota Extension says the same in its own calendar: move indoors around Labor Day, to a spot that gets at least six hours of direct light daily, preferably more, and reduce the fertilizer to one-quarter the recommended strength as new growth begins. Our [geranium overwintering guide](/blog/how-to-overwinter-geraniums/) covers the same move indoors for another tender plant, and the logic is identical — a tropical plant comes in before the cold, not after it. That move indoors is the last easy step in how to care for poinsettia before the demanding dark treatment begins.

### 1 October to early December: the dark treatment

This is the demanding part, and it is covered in full in the next section. In short: from the first week of October, the plant gets 14 continuous hours of complete darkness every night for eight to ten weeks, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and 6 to 8 hours of bright sunlight daily. The color appears during November or December, depending on the cultivar. That October-to-December stretch is the part of how to care for poinsettia that most keepers fail — one lit evening can cost the color.

### Christmas 2027: the second display

If the dark treatment held, the bracts color up in time for Christmas, a little over thirteen months after you bought the plant. That is the full cycle inside how to care for poinsettia — six weeks of holiday color, three months of green growth, a summer outdoors, and ten weeks of dark nights — and it is the reason the extension services call reblooming a challenge rather than a routine. Most people compost the plant in January, and that is a reasonable choice; the people who keep it get a second Christmas out of a plant they already own.

## Getting a poinsettia to rebloom

Reblooming is the hard part of how to care for poinsettia, and the reason it is hard is that the plant measures the night. The poinsettia is a short-day plant: it grows vegetatively during the long days of summer and produces flowers when the day length becomes shorter in fall, per Iowa State University Extension. The University of Florida IFAS Extension explains the mechanism — the extended period of darkness is the natural trigger for flowering, and if the dark period is interrupted by artificial light from any source after October 1, flowering will be delayed or interrupted. Flower buds begin to develop in October, and delay or disruption follows if fall temperatures are too warm or too cold, or if the required dark period is interrupted. That sensitivity is why the rebloom schedule is strict, and why every extension service describes it in hours rather than in general advice.

### The honest darkness spread: 13 to 16 hours, starting late September or early October

The four extension services that publish a darkness schedule agree on the mechanism and differ on the hours, so the honest version of how to care for poinsettia is a spread, not a single number:

- **Illinois Extension** says the plant must have at least 13 hours of continuous dark during a 24-hour period, with the shade treatment usually started in mid-October to have plants in bloom by December, taking about 6 weeks, and the temperature during the dark period about 60 to 62°F. As little as light from a porch light or security light can prevent blooming.
- **Clemson Cooperative Extension** says to start in the first week of October and keep the plant in total darkness for 14 continuous hours each night for eight to ten weeks, with 6 to 8 hours of bright sunlight daily, and the plant comes into full bloom during November or December.
- **Iowa State University Extension** says the plant must receive complete darkness from 5:00 pm to 8:00 am daily — 15 hours — from early October until the bracts develop good color, usually early December.
- **The University of Minnesota Extension** starts earliest and goes longest: from on or near September 21, give 16 hours of uninterrupted darkness and 8 hours of bright light every day, with night temperatures in the low 60s°F, and discontinue the treatment at Thanksgiving.

Those four numbers are the spread inside how to care for poinsettia — 13 to 16 hours, and the planning figure sits in the middle.

### The planning figure: 14 hours from early October

Put the spread together and the planning figure is 14 hours of complete darkness every night, starting in the first week of October, for eight to ten weeks — Clemson Cooperative Extension's schedule, which sits in the middle of the range and lands color in November or December. If you want color by Thanksgiving rather than by Christmas, use the University of Minnesota Extension's earlier start and longer nights: 16 hours from September 21, stopped at Thanksgiving. Either schedule works if it is kept without a single break, and neither works if it is broken — NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox warns that even a brief break in the dark can stop the plant from blooming, and the University of Minnesota Extension stresses that during the dark period the plant cannot receive even the slightest bit of light at any time. That 14-hour figure is the planning number for how to care for poinsettia, and it is the one to set the phone reminder by.

### How to run the dark treatment

The method is low-tech: put the plant in a closet or cover it with a box each evening, and bring it back to a sunny window each morning. Iowa State University Extension says to protect the plant from light by placing it in a closet or covering it with a box, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same — move it to a closet or cover it with a large box. During the remainder of the day the plant should be in a sunny window, per Iowa State University Extension, and it should receive 6 to 8 hours of bright sunlight daily, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. Keep the plants well-watered and fertilize every 2 weeks during the forcing period, per Iowa State University Extension, and rotate the plant daily to give all sides even light, per the University of Minnesota Extension. The night temperature matters too: the University of Minnesota Extension says to maintain night temperatures in the low 60s°F, and Illinois Extension puts the dark-period temperature at about 60 to 62°F. The low 60s°F night temperature is part of how to care for poinsettia during forcing, and a warm room slows the coloring. That closet-and-window routine is the daily practice of how to care for poinsettia through the forcing period, and it takes less than a minute a day.

### The common failure is the lamp

The common failure is not the closet — it is the lamp. A living room lamp switched on in the evening, a television, a porch light through a window, or a streetlight outside are all enough to break the dark, and the University of Florida IFAS Extension is explicit that artificial light from any source after October 1 delays or interrupts flowering. If the plant sits in a room where anyone turns a light on after dark, it must go into a closet or under a box — a room that stays dark on its own is ideal, and Illinois Extension calls a cool spare room in which you never turn on the lights ideal for exactly that reason. Set a phone reminder for the same time every evening and the same time every morning, and treat the schedule like a prescription: ten weeks of unbroken nights is the price of a second Christmas, and one lit evening can cost you the color. That unbroken-dark rule is the demanding heart of how to care for poinsettia past its first season, and it is the reason the extension services warn that poinsettias are challenging to flower in homes — while adding, as Iowa State University Extension does, that proper care can reward home gardeners with a colorful plant for the holiday season.

## Troubleshooting

Most poinsettia problems in how to care for poinsettia have one of three causes: the plant got cold, the water was wrong, or the air was still. The fixes are small, but they only work if the cause is named correctly.

### Leaves dropping

Leaf drop is the most common complaint, and it is almost always environmental stress rather than disease. Iowa State University Extension says the drop is likely due to some type of environmental stress, and that improper watering is the most common reason — over-watering turns the lower leaves yellow and they drop, while a plant allowed to get too dry wilts and also drops leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension lists the same causes together: temperatures dropping below 50°F, poor light, poor nutrition, and drafts or dry air from appliances, fireplaces, or ventilating ducts. Check the soil first with your finger, then check the spot: wet soil with yellowing lower leaves means too much water; dry soil with wilting leaves means too little; good soil in a drafty doorway or above a heat register means the spot is the problem, not the can. Move the plant to a bright, steady spot, fix the watering, and the drop usually stops — but leaves that have already fallen do not come back, and the plant recovers by growing new ones, not by reattaching the old ones. That soil-first, spot-second diagnosis is the leaf-drop check inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is the first thing to try before reaching for any product.

### Leaves wilting and turning black

Leaves and bracts that wilt and turn black, especially on the top or outside edges of the plant, are cold damage — and Iowa State University Extension says there is no cure for the problem, only prevention. The cause is exposure to freezing temperatures, even for a brief moment, and the leaves eventually fall off; depending on the severity, the entire plant may die. The fix is to prevent the next one: always cover any exposed part of the plant with a bag or sleeve when it goes outdoors, move it from the warm store directly to a warm car and then into your warm house as quickly as possible, and never leave it in a cold car while you finish shopping. If the blackening is already there, remove the damaged leaves so they do not rot on the plant, keep the plant warm and watered correctly, and wait — the green growth underneath may still be alive, and the plant can recover from the base even when the top is lost. That prevention-first rule is the cold-damage lesson inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is the reason the ride home matters more than any care you give later.

### Sudden wilt and death

A poinsettia that suddenly wilts and dies was most likely killed by root rot. Iowa State University Extension says Pythium and Rhizoctonia root rots typically occur when plants are watered too frequently and the potting soil remains saturated, and the advice is the same as the watering rule: allow the surface of the potting soil to dry to the touch before watering, and do not allow the pots to sit in water. NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox agrees — overwatering can cause root and stem rot — and the University of Florida IFAS Extension adds that plants growing in poorly drained or over-watered areas are often killed by fungi that rot the roots and stems. A plant that has collapsed from root rot rarely recovers, because the roots that take up water are gone, and the kindest action is to discard it and check the habit that killed it: the foil with standing water, the saucer never emptied, or the daily dribble that kept the soil wet. The next plant survives on the finger test and the empty saucer, and that is the root-rot lesson inside how to care for poinsettia.

### Small white flies when you move the plant

If small white insects flutter up when you water or handle the plant, they are whiteflies — the most common insect pest of poinsettia, per Iowa State University Extension and Clemson Cooperative Extension.

### The whitefly life cycle

The whitefly adults are small, white, moth-like insects, per Iowa State University Extension; the females lay eggs on the undersides of the foliage, the eggs hatch after 5 to 7 days into small, pale green nymphs, the nymphs crawl a short distance before settling down to feed for 2 to 3 weeks, and heavy infestations may cause stunting, yellowing of leaves, leaf drop, and a decline in plant health. ### Why whiteflies are hard to control

Whiteflies on poinsettias and other indoor plants are extremely difficult to control, per Iowa State University Extension, and insecticides are not a good control option because they are not very effective. Prevention is the best management strategy: inspect the plant carefully before you buy it, and avoid purchasing an insect-infested plant. For a small infestation on a plant you already own, Iowa State University Extension says it is often best to tolerate it and then promptly discard the plant after the holidays. ### The other pests

Other pests show up less often — Clemson Cooperative Extension lists mealybugs, soft scales, and spider mites alongside whiteflies, and NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox adds red spider mites and scale to the same list — but whiteflies are the ones you will actually see, and the answer is the same for all of them: check before you buy, and do not expect a spray to save a plant that is already infested. If the rest of your indoor plants need a pest check, our [fungus gnat guide](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers the most common indoor-plant pest and the same inspect-first habit. That inspect-before-you-buy rule is the pest lesson inside how to care for poinsettia, and it is cheaper than any treatment.

## Are poinsettias poisonous?

The poinsettia's reputation as a poison is the most out of step with the evidence of any houseplant myth, and the extension services have been correcting it for decades. The short answer is that the plant is not poisonous to people, and only mildly irritating to pets — and that correction is part of how to care for poinsettia, because people who believe the myth throw the plant away, or keep it locked away from the family for no reason.

### The human evidence

The human evidence is a poison-center study, and Iowa State University Extension reports it in full: researchers studied 22,793 cases of poinsettia exposures reported to the American Association of Poison Control Centers. In these cases, 98.9% were accidental in nature and 93.1% involved children. There were no fatalities, and fewer than five percent of exposures caused sufficient concern to require treatment at a medical facility. The plant is not intended for human or animal consumption, and on rare occasions — less than 8% of exposures — it causes gastric upset, vomiting, and diarrhea, but the study found no poisoning. That study is the evidence that settles the poison question inside how to care for poinsettia — the plant is not poisonous, and the reputation is the myth. Clemson Cooperative Extension reaches the same conclusion: poinsettia plants are not considered toxic, because the milky sap makes the foliage taste unpleasant, so it would be unlikely that a child would consume a significant amount — though eating foliage may lead to a stomachache, diarrhea, or vomiting. The University of Minnesota Extension agrees: poinsettias are not harmful to animal or human health, but they should not be eaten. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says it most briefly: poinsettias are not poisonous, although some people are mildly allergic to their white, milky sap.

### Pets: mildly irritating, not deadly

For pets, the ASPCA lists poinsettia as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, because the milky sap irritates the mouth and stomach and can sometimes cause vomiting — and in the same listing calls the plant "generally over-rated in toxicity." The ASPCA's own holiday guidance says the same in plain language: poinsettias cause only mild to moderate gastrointestinal irritation, keeping the plant out of pets' reach is still a good idea, but there is no need to banish it altogether. NC State Extension's Plant Toolbox rates the plant low in severity, noting that a significant amount would have to be ingested before registering a mild stomach ache. So the practical rule is simple: keep the plant where a pet or a small child cannot chew it, wear gloves when you prune it, and wash sap off skin with soap and water — Clemson Cooperative Extension says the milky sap can be a skin irritant and cause a mild rash, and to wash the skin with soap and water, and to flush the eyes with water if sap contacts them. The University of Minnesota Extension adds the same precautions: the sticky white sap can cause a skin rash, so gloves are recommended when working with these plants, and you should avoid contact with eyes and mouth. If a pet does chew a bract, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 for advice. That mild-but-real rating is the honest toxicity answer inside how to care for poinsettia — not deadly, not harmless, and not a reason to give up the plant.

## The short version

How to care for poinsettia comes down to five rules and one decision. Buy a healthy plant — dark green foliage, bright bracts, cyathia not yet shedding pollen — and make it the last thing you buy, sleeved, straight into a heated car. Give it at least 6 hours of bright, indirect light a day, a few inches back from cold glass. Hold the room at 60 to 70°F and never below 50°F, away from drafts and heat registers. Check the soil daily with your finger, water when the surface feels dry until water runs out the bottom, take the pot out of the foil first, and never let it stand in water. Do not fertilize while it is blooming. With that, the color lasts several weeks past Christmas. After the holidays, either compost it — a reasonable choice, and the one Iowa State University Extension expects most people to make — or keep it: cut the stems to 4 to 6 inches in March, move it outdoors once nights stay above 50°F, pinch the tips through the summer, bring it in mid-September, and give it 14 hours of complete darkness every night from the first week of October for eight to ten weeks. If you are putting the rest of the garden to bed for the winter, our [winterizing guide](/blog/how-to-winterize-a-garden/) covers the November work that goes with it — and if you want another holiday plant to force indoors, our [paperwhite guide](/blog/how-to-force-paperwhite-bulbs/) covers the bulbs that bloom in four to six weeks with no chilling at all. That is the whole method — bright light, steady warmth, water through and drain, and dark nights in autumn.

landscape planting: choose a sunny spot that will not receive any artificial light at night, plant as soon as danger of frost is past, cut off the fading bracts leaving 4 to 6 inches of stem on each branch, moist well-drained soils with a soil pH between 5.5 and 6.5, fertilize monthly from March to October in central and south Florida and May to September in north Florida, prune in early spring, cut back to within 12 to 18 inches of the ground, cut back once a month if needed leaving four leaves on each shoot, discontinue pruning after September 10, flower buds will begin to develop in October, delay or disruption of flowering if fall temperatures are too warm or too cold or if the required dark period is interrupted; pests include aphids, mealybugs, soft scales, whiteflies and spider mites; plants in poorly drained or over-watered areas are often killed by fungi that rot the roots and stems)

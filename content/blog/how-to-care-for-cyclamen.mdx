A cyclamen in full bloom looks like it should be easy — a small plant, a small pot, flowers like butterflies over silver-marbled leaves — and then it collapses within a few weeks of coming home. The collapse is almost never your fault, and that is the first thing to know about how to care for cyclamen. It is not that you forgot to water it, or watered it too much. It is that the plant wants a room you do not live in: a cool, bright room at 60 to 65°F by day and around 50°F at night, the opposite of a heated living room in November. That single fact is the whole of how to care for cyclamen, and everything else in this guide follows from it.

This guide follows the extension sources, not the plant tag. Everything below comes from six sources — the University of Wisconsin–Madison, Iowa State, Maryland, and Clemson extensions, NC State Extension, and the New York Botanical Garden — verified on the source pages themselves. Where the sources disagree — and they do, on how long the plant blooms and how often to feed it — you will see the spread, and the planning figure this guide uses.

Before the care details: the cyclamen on your windowsill is a Mediterranean plant that flowers through cool, damp winters and rests through hot, dry summers. Indoors, it still keeps that calendar. It blooms from early winter to spring and goes dormant in the summer, per NC State Extension — so a plant that yellows and dies back in May is not dying. It is doing exactly what it evolved to do; the dormancy section below shows you how to let it rest, the last piece of how to care for cyclamen.

## How to care for cyclamen: the five rules

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember these five rules — they are the whole of how to care for cyclamen in one paragraph each.

**Keep it cool, especially at night.** Days of 60 to 65°F and nights around 50°F keep the flowers coming longest. A warm living room is the main reason cyclamen fail: above 70°F the buds stop developing and the plant goes dormant early.

**Give it bright, indirect light.** An east-facing window is the classic spot. A south window can be better in the darkest weeks of a northern winter. Hot direct sun through glass scorches the leaves.

**Water from below, when the surface is dry.** Stand the pot in a bowl of room-temperature water for about 15 minutes, then let it drain. Never pour water onto the crown in the center — a wet crown rots, and crown rot is the one problem that kills the plant outright.

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing and blooming.** A dilute fertilizer for flowering houseplants, every two weeks, at half strength. Stop feeding when the leaves yellow in late spring.

**Let it rest in the summer.** When flowering ends, water less and less, stop feeding, and keep the dormant tuber cool and shaded for six to eight weeks. Start watering again in late summer, and it should bloom again by midwinter.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules does three things a neglected one does not. First, it holds its flowers: kept cool, it can flower for up to three months, per the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension, instead of collapsing in three weeks on a warm mantelpiece. The sources differ on how long the flowers last — the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says up to three months when kept cool, while Clemson Cooperative Extension says up to four weeks — and the difference is the temperature: the cooler you keep it, the longer each flower lasts. Second, it stays compact and full, with dark green marbled leaves and flowers held high on straight stalks, instead of stretching pale and leggy toward a dim window. Third, it comes back. A cyclamen that gets its cool summer rest can bloom again next winter — and the year after that. The five rules in how to care for cyclamen are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the plant's own calendar, followed indoors.

## Is yours a florist cyclamen? Check before you plan

The cyclamen in the shops in November is almost always a florist's cyclamen, *Cyclamen persicum* — a hybrid bred from a wild plant of the eastern Mediterranean, from Turkey and the Levant to Algeria. The wild plant grows in cool, shady places where winters are wet and summers are dry, and the florist's hybrids keep that rhythm: bloom in the cool months, rest in the hot ones. Knowing which one you bought is the first step in how to care for cyclamen, because the tender kind and the hardy kind want opposite things. Iowa State University Extension notes that plants are readily available in florist shops and garden centers in the winter and spring seasons, especially around Valentine's Day and Christmas, and they come in three sizes — miniature, 6 inches or less in height, intermediate, and standard, around 12 inches tall. NC State Extension gives the plant's size as a mounded 6 to 9 inches tall, with flowers in white, pink, red, magenta, or lavender, blooming from early winter to spring.

### The hardy cousins are different plants

There are about 23 species in the genus *Cyclamen*, per NC State Extension, and most of them are not houseplants at all. The hardy species — *Cyclamen hederifolium*, *Cyclamen coum*, and *Cyclamen cilicium* — are garden plants that tolerate USDA Zones 4a to 8b and are planted under trees, where they come back every year. The florist's cyclamen is winter hardy only in Zones 9 to 11, and it is frost tender: the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension calls it a tender plant that will not tolerate frost. If your plant came in a pot from a florist, a garden center, or a supermarket, it is the tender kind, and everything in how to care for cyclamen applies to it. Do not plant it in the garden expecting it to survive the winter — in most of the United States, it will not.

### What to look for when you buy one

Iowa State University Extension recommends picking a plant with dark green leaves that has only just started to flower, with plenty of unopened buds still to come — that gives you the longest bloom at home. Check the tuber, the round, flat base sitting at the surface of the mix: it should feel firm, like an onion, with no soft spots or mold. Check under the leaves for aphids or whitefly, and look at the soil surface for fungus gnats. A plant already in full bloom with yellowing leaves has been kept too warm in the shop and will finish quickly; a plant with tight buds and firm green leaves has weeks of flowers ahead of it. Choosing well is the cheapest step in how to care for cyclamen, and it is the one most people skip.

## Light: bright, indirect — and brighter than you think

The first thing to get right in how to care for cyclamen is the light, and it is brighter than most people expect for a plant that hates heat. The plant wants a lot of light, just not hot sun. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says cyclamen prefer bright, indirect light, that an east window is often a good spot, and that in the winter in Wisconsin a southern exposure may be better. Iowa State University Extension agrees: bright, indirect light. The University of Maryland Extension adds that the plant likes very bright light, conditions that are rare in most homes. The New York Botanical Garden gives the same advice — bright, indirect light, with a south window in the darkest weeks of winter.

### Where to put it

An east-facing windowsill is the classic spot — gentle morning sun, then indirect light for the rest of the day. In the darkest weeks of a northern winter, a south-facing window may be better, because the sun is low and weak and the plant needs all the light it can get. A north-facing window is usually too dim on its own: in low light the plant gets weak, the flowers are smaller, the leaves turn pale, and low light is one of the main reasons a cyclamen fails to bloom again indoors. If your only windows face north, a small grow light a foot or two above the plant, on for 12 hours a day, is a reasonable substitute — but keep the room cool, because light without cool air still pushes the plant toward dormancy. Light is the first decision in how to care for cyclamen, and the window you choose answers it.

### The signs you have the light wrong

Too much direct sun shows up as scorch: pale or bleached patches on the leaves, or leaves that curl away from the glass. The Missouri Botanical Garden warns that too much direct sun can scorch the leaves, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension notes that hot sun is the one thing to keep the plant out of. Too little light shows up as growth: long, thin, stretched leaf stalks reaching toward the window, small flowers, and pale leaves. If the plant looks stretched, move it closer to the window. If the leaves look scorched, move it back a foot or behind a sheer curtain. The plant will tell you within a couple of weeks, and reading those two signals is the practical skill at the center of how to care for cyclamen.

## Temperature: cool nights are the whole game

Temperature is where the extension sources give you a spread rather than a single number, and the honest version of how to care for cyclamen is to show you that spread and then give you a planning figure. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension puts the ideal at 60 to 65°F during the day and around 50°F at night, and says keeping the plant cool extends both the life of each flower and the whole blooming season. Clemson Cooperative Extension gives the same ideal — 60 to 65°F by day and around 50°F at night — and warns that above 70°F the buds fail to develop. Iowa State University Extension gives a broader range, 50 to 60°F, with bright, indirect light. The University of Maryland Extension gives 50 to 65°F. The New York Botanical Garden gives 55 to 65°F by day and 50 to 55°F at night.

### The planning figure: 60 to 65°F by day, about 50°F at night

Every source agrees on the shape of the answer, even where the numbers differ: cool days, cooler nights, and never a warm room. The planning figure this guide uses is days of 60 to 65°F and nights around 50°F. If your house is warmer than that during the day — and most heated homes are — then the night temperature matters most. A cool bedroom, a hallway, or a frost-free porch often suits a cyclamen better than the living room, because the plant gets its cold night even if the day is warm. Keep it away from radiators, fireplaces, heating vents, and the top of the television: the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension and the University of Maryland Extension both warn that warm drafts shorten flowering and signal the plant to go dormant. A room that stays at 70°F or warmer will make the leaves turn yellow, the flowers fade quickly, and the plant go dormant early — however well you water it. That is the single most common reason a cyclamen dies within weeks of coming home, and it is the reason temperature comes first in how to care for cyclamen.

### Frost is the hard limit

Cool is good; freezing is not — and knowing where the line sits is part of how to care for cyclamen. The florist's cyclamen is a tender plant that will not tolerate frost, per the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension, and NC State Extension lists it as hardy only in Zones 9 to 11. A porch that stays frost-free is fine. A porch that drops below freezing overnight will kill the plant. If the plant has been sitting outside on a mild autumn day, bring it in before the first frost — and keep it away from a freezing windowpane in midwinter, where the glass itself can chill the leaves enough to damage them.

## Water: from below, when the surface is dry

Watering is the other half of how to care for cyclamen, and it is the part people get wrong most often after temperature. The plant wants moist soil while it is flowering, but it does not tolerate wet soil — and the difference between moist and wet is where the tuber sits. The tuber is the round, flat base at the surface of the mix, and water pooling on top of it causes crown rot, which is the one watering mistake that kills the plant outright.

The extension sources agree on the method. Iowa State University Extension says the plant prefers moist soils but does not tolerate wet soils, that it is best to check the potting soil daily, and that if the soil surface is dry to the touch, then water thoroughly. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says the same: water thoroughly when the soil looks and feels dry on the surface, and keep the soil moist while the plant is flowering. Both sources warn against the same mistake: Iowa State University Extension says to avoid watering the corm directly because it is susceptible to overwatering and rot, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says to avoid watering the crown or center of the plant, which may rot if it remains too wet.

### How to water from below

Stand the pot in a saucer or a bowl of room-temperature water for about 15 minutes, so the mix drinks it up through the drainage holes, then lift the pot out and let it drain completely. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension and the New York Botanical Garden both recommend this method, and Iowa State University Extension adds that warm room-temperature water is best, to prevent damage to the foliage — cold water straight from the tap can shock the leaves. Watering from below is the safest habit in how to care for cyclamen, because it keeps the crown dry by design. If you would rather water from the top, pour slowly around the rim of the pot, at the edge, never over the center where the leaves and flowers come out of the tuber. Never leave the pot sitting in water after watering: a cyclamen left standing in a saucer of water will rot from the roots up, and by the time the leaves droop, the tuber is usually already soft.

### How often, in practice

There is no fixed schedule, because the plant's thirst changes with the room. A plant in full bloom in a small pot in a cool, bright room may need water every few days; the same plant in a larger pot in a cooler room may go a week. Iowa State University Extension says to check the soil daily, because a plant in bloom in a small pot dries quickly — and that daily check is the habit, not a daily watering. Water when the surface feels dry, water thoroughly, and let it drain. Overwatering is one of the most common ways cyclamen die, and it usually looks like the opposite problem: the leaves droop and yellow, the owner waters more, and the rot spreads. If the soil is wet and the plant is drooping, stop watering and let the mix dry — the answer is almost never more water. That discipline is the quiet skill inside how to care for cyclamen, and it is worth more than any fertilizer.

## Soil and pot: drainage first, and the tuber stays half out

A cyclamen needs a mix that holds moisture without staying soggy, and a pot that lets the extra water out. NC State Extension says the plant prefers rich, well-drained soils, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says the plant needs high humidity and moist soil to continue blooming — moist, not wet. A good-quality houseplant potting mix works well, and a mix labeled for African violets, which is light and drains freely, is a good match. If you mix your own, the airy blend in our guide to [making your own potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) is the right starting point, and the [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a repot will take. What the mix must not be is heavy garden soil, which stays wet and suffocates the roots. Getting the mix right is the foundation of how to care for cyclamen, because everything else — watering, feeding, the summer rest — depends on roots that can breathe.

### Repotting: rare, and only when the plant is growing

A cyclamen does not need repotting every year, and it should never be repotted while it is in full bloom. The right time is when new growth resumes in late summer, after the dormancy rest, when the plant is starting to grow again. Go up only one pot size, use fresh mix, and set the tuber so its top half sits above the soil line — a buried tuber rots, and an exposed tuber dries out, so half out is the balance. Make sure the new pot has drainage holes, and water the plant in well, then let the mix dry on the surface before watering again. If the plant is still in the pot it came in and it is blooming happily, leave it alone: repotting a blooming cyclamen is one of the fastest ways to lose the flowers. Patience with the pot is part of how to care for cyclamen, and the plant rewards it by blooming where it is.

## Feeding: little and often, only while it blooms

The extension sources disagree on exactly how often to feed a cyclamen, and the spread is worth seeing. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says to use a low-nitrogen fertilizer or a houseplant food for blooming plants every two weeks when growing actively, and warns that too much fertilizer promotes overly lush growth that is more susceptible to disease. Iowa State University Extension agrees: fertilize with a dilute fertilizer solution every two weeks while in bloom. The University of Maryland Extension gives the same interval at half strength. Clemson Cooperative Extension is the outlier, feeding monthly rather than every two weeks.

### The planning figure: every two weeks, at half strength

Three sources against one makes the planning figure easy: feed every two weeks while the plant is growing and blooming, with a low-nitrogen liquid fertilizer for flowering houseplants, diluted to half the strength on the label. A low-nitrogen food matters, because too much nitrogen grows leaves at the expense of flowers — the plant gets lush and green and stops blooming. Do not feed while the plant is dormant: the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension and Iowa State University Extension both say to stop feeding when the leaves yellow in late spring, and to resume only when growth restarts in late summer. A cyclamen fed through its summer rest will push soft growth that rots in the cool, dry rest, and the fertilizer salts build up in a pot that is barely being watered. Feed the bloom, not the rest — that is the rule inside how to care for cyclamen, and it is the cheapest mistake to avoid.

## Humidity: the plant likes damp air, not wet leaves

Cyclamen come from Mediterranean winters, where the air is cool and damp, and they notice when a heated house dries the air out. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says the plant needs high humidity to continue blooming. The New York Botanical Garden puts a number on it: above 50% relative humidity while the plant is flowering. Most heated homes in winter sit at 30 to 40%, so a cyclamen on a dry mantelpiece is breathing drier air than it wants, and the flowers fade faster for it.

### The pebble tray

The simplest fix is a pebble tray: set the pot on a shallow tray filled with pebbles and water, with the base of the pot sitting on the pebbles, above the water, not in it. As the water evaporates it humidifies the air around the plant, and the pot itself stays dry underneath. A small humidifier in the room works too, and a bathroom with a bright window is often the best room in the house for a cyclamen — bright, cool, and humid after a shower. Getting the humidity right without wetting the crown is one of the finer points of how to care for cyclamen. What does not work is misting the leaves: water sitting on the leaves and in the crown invites the same rot that overwatering causes, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension's warning about keeping the crown dry applies to misting as much as to watering. Damp air, dry crown — that is the balance in how to care for cyclamen, and the pebble tray is how you get it.

### Removing spent flowers

While the plant is blooming, remove spent flowers as they fade, taking the whole stalk with them, so nothing rots on the tuber. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to securely grab the spent flower stem and pull it off completely from the plant's crown, and that removing spent flowers can also help encourage more flowers to develop. Do the same with yellowing leaves. A plant kept clean of dead flowers and leaves stays healthier, and it keeps putting its energy into the unopened buds instead of into seed. This is a small job, done once or twice a week while the plant is in bloom, and it is the kind of attention that stretches the flowering season by weeks.

## After flowering: the summer rest

This is the part of how to care for cyclamen that most people never learn, because the plant looks dead when it happens. In late spring the flowers stop, the leaves start to yellow, and the plant slowly declines to a bare tuber in a pot of dry mix. That is not failure. That is dormancy, and it is the plant doing exactly what it evolved to do in the Mediterranean, where summers are hot and dry. NC State Extension says the plant typically blooms from early winter to spring and is dormant during the summer. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says that in late spring the plants stop blooming and go into dormancy for two to three months. Iowa State University Extension gives the same sequence: after blooming, the leaves yellow and die as the plant slowly declines, and the dormancy or rest period should last approximately six to eight weeks during late spring and early summer.

### How to rest the tuber

When the leaves start to yellow, water less and less over several weeks, and stop feeding. Remove the dead leaves as they die off. Then keep the pot dry in a cool, shaded place — Iowa State University Extension gives about 45 to 50°F for the rest, and the New York Botanical Garden gives about 50°F. A cool basement, a shaded corner of a garage that stays frost-free, or a cool cupboard all work. Clemson Cooperative Extension adds one qualification: keep the soil from drying out completely during the rest, so a small drink now and then is fine — the tuber should rest dry, not desiccate. Do not feed it, do not repot it, and do not throw it away. The tuber underneath the dry mix is alive, and it is waiting for late summer. Trusting that wait is the hardest part of how to care for cyclamen. Getting this rest right is the difference between a plant that blooms once and a plant that blooms every winter, and it is the part of how to care for cyclamen that separates a one-season decoration from a perennial.

## Getting it to bloom again next year

The honest answer is that reblooming a florist's cyclamen is possible but not easy, and the extension sources say so plainly. Iowa State University Extension says the cyclamen is best discarded after flowering, because it can be difficult to keep plants healthy and to get them to rebloom. The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension agrees that it may be difficult to provide the right conditions to get them to rebloom, and that many people treat florist's cyclamen as a short-lived, temporary indoor plant. The University of Maryland Extension says the same: it is not easy to get it to flower a second time. If you buy a cyclamen, enjoy the flowers, and throw the plant away in spring, you have done nothing wrong — that is what most people do, and what the extension services expect most people to do, and it is a perfectly good way to enjoy how to care for cyclamen for one season.

### The rebloom plan, step by step

If you want to try, here is the plan, and it follows the sources exactly. In mid to late summer, after six to eight weeks of rest, bring the pot back into warmth — Iowa State University Extension gives 50 to 65°F — and start watering again. Clemson Cooperative Extension waits until new leaves appear, around mid-September, which is the same signal seen from the other side: when the tuber wakes up, you will see it. Once leaves appear, give the plant your brightest window, feed it every two weeks with a food for blooming plants, and keep nights near 50°F and days at 60 to 65°F. With enough light and cool air, it should bloom again by midwinter. Low light is the main reason a rebloom fails: a plant kept in a dim room through the autumn will grow leaves and no flowers, however cool you keep it. If the plant does not bloom the second winter, it has not necessarily died — give it another summer rest and try again. Some tubers take two seasons to find their rhythm indoors, and the ones that do are the plants that get the cool rest and the bright autumn. That patience is the long game inside how to care for cyclamen.

### A worked example: one cyclamen, bought today

Here is the plan as a calendar, for a cyclamen bought in bloom on Saturday, 15 November 2026. It blooms through the winter in a cool, bright room at 60 to 65°F by day and around 50°F at night, watered from below when the surface is dry and fed every two weeks at half strength — and with that care, the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says it can keep flowering for up to three months, into February. In late spring, around May, the flowers stop and the leaves yellow: you water less and less, stop feeding, remove the dead leaves, and set the pot in a cool, shaded spot at about 45 to 50°F for its rest — six to eight weeks, per Iowa State University Extension, so roughly June into July. In late August you bring it back into a room at 50 to 65°F and start watering again; new leaves appear in September, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and you feed every two weeks and keep the nights near 50°F. By December 2027 the plant is blooming again — thirteen months after you bought it, and about six weeks into its second flowering season. That is the full year inside how to care for cyclamen: one winter of flowers, one summer of rest, and a second winter that costs you nothing but a cool corner and a little patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most cyclamen problems have one of four causes: too much warmth, too much water, too little light, or a change after the buds formed. Work through the list below before you reach for a spray or a repot. Each fix below is a piece of how to care for cyclamen, applied to one symptom at a time.

### Plant droops

Drooping is almost always thirst or heat, and the two look alike, so check the soil first. If the surface is dry, the plant is thirsty: Iowa State University Extension says a cyclamen in bloom dries out fast and to check the soil daily. Stand the pot in room-temperature water for about 15 minutes and let it drain — a thirsty plant usually perks up within a few hours. If the soil is already moist and the plant still droops, heat is the cause: the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension puts the ideal at 60 to 65°F by day and around 50°F at night, and the University of Maryland Extension warns that warmer temperatures shorten flowering and push the plant toward dormancy. Move it to the coolest bright room you have, away from radiators and heat vents. Drooping with wet soil and a soft, mushy tuber is different — that is crown rot, and the plant usually cannot be saved. Check the soil first, then the temperature — that order of checks is the habit at the heart of how to care for cyclamen.

### Leaves turn yellow in winter

Yellow leaves in winter usually mean too warm or too wet, and the fix is the same as for drooping: check the temperature first, then the watering. A cyclamen kept in a room at 70°F or warmer will yellow and go dormant early, however much you water it. A cyclamen kept in soggy soil will yellow from the roots up. Move the plant somewhere cooler, let the surface of the mix dry before you water again, and remove the yellow leaves. Yellow leaves in late spring are different: they mean dormancy, not a problem, and the section above on the summer rest tells you what to do. Telling winter yellowing from spring yellowing is a matter of the calendar, and the calendar is the diagnostic tool in how to care for cyclamen.

### Plant does not bloom, or buds fail to open

The causes are specific, and they are all in the five rules. The New York Botanical Garden notes that buds may fail to open in a room that is too warm, and Clemson Cooperative Extension warns that above 70°F the buds fail to develop — so check the temperature first, and give the plant nights near 50°F. Low light is the second cause: a plant in a dim room grows leaves and no flowers, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says the plant needs bright, indirect light to keep blooming. The third cause is feeding: too much nitrogen grows leaves at the expense of flowers, so use a low-nitrogen food for blooming plants at half strength, every two weeks. Check in order: night temperature, light, then feeding. One of those three is almost always the answer in how to care for cyclamen when a healthy plant refuses to flower.

### Plant looks wilted even though the soil is wet

This is root rot or crown rot, and it is the one problem that can kill the plant. Too much water causes the roots and the tuber to rot, so the plant wilts even though the soil is wet — the roots are gone, and the plant cannot drink. The action is to let the mix dry out, make sure the pot drains freely, and never let the plant sit in water. If the tuber is soft and mushy, the plant is beyond saving: take it out, throw the mix away, and start with a new plant. Prevention is the whole story here: water from below, keep the crown dry, and water only when the surface is dry. Those three preventions are the cheapest insurance in how to care for cyclamen.

### Small flying insects around the soil

Those are fungus gnats, which thrive in soil that stays wet all the time. The first step is the same as for rot: let the top of the mix dry, because gnats cannot breed in dry soil. Our guide to [getting rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers the same methods in detail — drying the surface, trapping the adults, and repotting in fresh mix for a bad infestation — and the first step is the same in both: let the top of the mix dry. Dry topsoil is the gnat control that costs nothing in how to care for cyclamen.

### Aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, and thrips

The University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension says cyclamen do not have many pests, but can occasionally be attacked by aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, or thrips. Check under the leaves and in the center of the plant before you bring a new cyclamen home, and check again before you move it next to your other houseplants. A few aphids can be washed off with a strong spray of room-temperature water; mealybugs can be dabbed off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. A heavy infestation on a plant that is also dropping buds is usually a sign to discard the plant rather than to treat it — the plant is cheap, the pests are not worth spreading to your [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) and your [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) on the same windowsill. Checking for pests before the plant joins the collection is the inspection step in how to care for cyclamen.

## Is cyclamen poisonous to pets?

Yes — and unlike the Christmas cactus, this is a plant to keep out of reach, which is an important safety note in how to care for cyclamen. The ASPCA lists cyclamen as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, because of the terpenoid saponins in the plant, and NC State Extension adds that the dark brown tuberous root is poisonous and is the most dangerous part of the plant. A pet that nibbles a leaf may drool or vomit; a pet that digs up and eats a large piece of the tuber can suffer severe diarrhea, and the ASPCA lists heart rhythm problems, seizures, and even death after a large amount of tuber is eaten. That makes the cyclamen one of the most toxic plants on the holiday windowsill — compare it with the other plants on the same sill: the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is non-toxic, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating, and amaryllis and paperwhite bulbs are toxic too. The cyclamen is the one you must keep out of reach.

Keep the plant on a high shelf or in a room pets cannot reach, never leave a repotted tuber where a dog can dig it up, and if a pet eats any part of the plant, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. Children are at risk too — NC State Extension notes vomiting and diarrhea in people — so keep the plant out of reach of toddlers as well. That safety is the one real drawback of the plant in how to care for cyclamen, and it is worth knowing before you buy one for a house with a curious cat.

## The short version

How to care for cyclamen, in one paragraph: give it bright, indirect light and a fast-draining mix; keep it cool, 60 to 65°F by day and around 50°F at night, away from radiators and heat vents; water from below with room-temperature water when the surface of the mix is dry, and never pour water onto the crown; feed every two weeks at half strength with a low-nitrogen food for blooming plants, only while it is growing and blooming; keep the air humid with a pebble tray and never mist the leaves; when the flowers stop in late spring, water less, stop feeding, and rest the tuber cool and shaded at about 45 to 50°F for six to eight weeks, then start watering again in late summer for flowers by midwinter. Do that, and the plant you buy this November will flower for up to three months — and, with a little patience, again next winter. If you want a second holiday plant for the same windowsill, [paperwhite bulbs](/blog/how-to-force-paperwhite-bulbs/) take only three to six weeks and need no chilling at all, and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) blooms on the same cool nights with none of the toxicity worry.

This is a short, practical question with one genuine trap in it. Container volumes are easy
arithmetic, and the unit that potting mix is sold in is not the unit most calculators use.

## The trap: dry quarts, not liquid quarts

Bagged potting mix in the United States is sold in **US dry quarts**. That is a distinct unit
from the liquid quart most people think of.

- One US dry quart is exactly 67.200625 cubic inches.
- One cubic foot is 1,728 cubic inches.
- So **one cubic foot is 25.71 dry quarts**.

The figure 29.92 appears on a great many websites, and it is the _liquid_ quart conversion. Use
it and you will believe a container holds about 16% more than it does, then buy 16% too few bags
and make a second trip.

Everywhere outside the US the unit is litres, and one cubic foot is 28.32 litres. No ambiguity.

## The arithmetic

A round pot or a half barrel is a cylinder:

```
cubic inches = pi x (diameter / 2)^2 x depth
```

A square or rectangular planter is a box:

```
cubic inches = length x width x depth
```

Then divide by 1,728 for cubic feet, and convert from there:

```
cubic feet    = cubic inches / 1728
US dry quarts = cubic feet x 25.714
litres        = cubic feet x 28.317
US gallons    = cubic feet x 7.4805
```

Measure the diameter across the **top of the soil**, and use the **soil depth** rather than the
full height of the pot — leave an inch of freeboard so watering does not wash mix over the rim.

### Worked example

A twelve-inch round pot, filled ten inches deep:

- Radius 6 inches, so volume = pi x 36 x 10 = **1,131 cubic inches**
- 1,131 / 1,728 = **0.654 cubic feet**
- 0.654 x 25.714 = **16.8 US dry quarts**
- 0.654 x 28.317 = **18.5 litres**

So one 25-quart bag fills that pot with a little left over. Four of them need three bags.

## Practical notes

**Pots taper, so buy for the calculated figure and expect to use less.** The cylinder maths
treats a pot as straight-sided; a typical tapered pot holds ten to twenty per cent less than its
top diameter suggests. That margin is useful anyway, because mix settles.

**Do not put gravel in the bottom.** It does not improve drainage — water will not cross from
fine material into coarse until the fine material is saturated, so a gravel layer raises the
waterlogged zone rather than lowering it. A drainage hole and a free-draining mix is what a pot
needs.

**Buy by volume, not weight.** Bag weights vary enormously with how wet the mix is. The quart or
litre figure is the one that tells you whether it will fill your pots.

**Reusing old mix.** Old mix has lost structure and nutrients and may carry disease. Mixing the
top half with an equal volume of fresh mix and compost works well for annuals; start fresh for
seedlings.

**One big container beats two small ones.** The larger the volume, the slower it dries out, and
drying out is what kills most container plants. If you are choosing between sizes, take the
bigger one.

## For raised beds, switch units

Beds are big enough that quarts stop being useful. Bagged soil and compost for beds are sold by
the cubic foot, and bulk by the cubic yard — 27 cubic feet. A four by eight foot bed twelve inches
deep takes 32 cubic feet, which is 22 bags at 1.5 cubic feet, or about 1.2 cubic yards loose. The
[raised bed calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) handles that end.

## The short version

Volume is pi r squared times depth for a round pot and length times width times depth for a
square one, divided by 1,728 for cubic feet. Multiply cubic feet by 25.71 for dry quarts, which
is the unit bags are sold in — not 29.92, which is liquid quarts. Measure to the soil line, not
the rim, and buy the bigger pot.

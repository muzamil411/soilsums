Plan a vegetable garden layout in this order: **sun, then water, then bed width**. Put beds where
they get six hours of direct sun and within hose reach of a tap, make them no wider than four
feet so you never step on the soil, and leave paths wide enough for a wheelbarrow. Everything
else can be changed later.

## Start with sun

Watch the site for a day before you build anything. Note where the sun falls at nine, at noon and
at four.

| Crop group                                  | Direct sun needed | Where to put them                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Fruiting — tomato, pepper, squash, cucumber | 8 hours           | The sunniest ground you have                           |
| Root — carrot, beet, potato                 | 6 hours           | Next best                                              |
| Brassicas                                   | 6 hours           | Next best                                              |
| Leafy — lettuce, spinach, chard             | 4 hours           | Shadier corners; they prefer afternoon shade in summer |
| Herbs                                       | 4–6 hours         | Near the kitchen door, whatever the aspect             |

Those figures come from our own [crop data](/data-sources/). If you have a mix of aspects,
allocate deliberately. A shady garden is not a failed garden — it is a salad garden.

## Then water

Work out how you will water before you site the beds, not after. Carrying cans across a lawn
twice a week in July is the kind of chore that quietly ends a garden.

Beds within hose reach of a tap get watered. Beds at the far end get watered less than they need.
If the far end is the sunny end, the answer is a longer hose or a water butt, decided now rather
than in August.
[How much water does a vegetable garden need per week](/blog/how-much-to-water-a-vegetable-garden/)
puts numbers on what you will be carrying.

## How wide should a vegetable garden bed be?

Four feet, if you can reach it from both sides. The width is set by your arms, because the one
rule that matters is that you never step on the soil.

| Access                                  | Maximum width | Why                                          |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| From both sides                         | 4 ft          | Most adults reach about two feet comfortably |
| From one side — against a wall or fence | 2–3 ft        | Same reach, half the access                  |
| From a seat or kneeler                  | 2.5 ft        | Lower position, shorter reach                |
| Children's bed                          | 3 ft          | Shorter arms                                 |

Footfall compacts soil, and compacted soil holds less air and drains worse for years afterwards.
Everything else in this article follows from that single constraint.

Length is unconstrained except by your plot and how far you are willing to walk around. Eight
feet is common because it suits standard timber.

## Paths

Make them wide enough for what will actually go down them.

| Path width | What fits                                |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| 18 in      | A person, walking carefully              |
| 24 in      | A wheelbarrow                            |
| 36 in      | A barrow with room to kneel beside a bed |

Narrow paths get trodden into the beds either side. Mulch them with bark, gravel or cardboard and
they stay usable in wet weather and grow fewer weeds.

## Which direction should garden rows run?

North to south, so both sides of a row get sun as it moves across the sky. But this matters far
less than the two exceptions.

**Tall crops go on the north side** in the northern hemisphere, whichever way the rows run. Sweet
corn or staked tomatoes on the south side of a bed shade everything behind them from midsummer
onwards. This is the single most common layout mistake, and it costs more yield than row
direction ever will.

**On a slope, run rows across the slope**, not down it. Rows running downhill channel water and
soil away in heavy rain.

In the Southern Hemisphere, reverse the compass direction: tall crops go on the _south_ side.

Otherwise, run rows whichever way suits the shape of the bed. A 4 × 8 ft bed with short rows
across its width is easier to sow and harvest than one with long rows down its length.

## Fitting the plants in

Once the beds exist, spacing decides what actually fits. Plants need their published in-row
spacing whatever layout you use — a bed does not make a tomato smaller.

| Crop       | In-row spacing | Row spacing |
| ---------- | -------------- | ----------- |
| Tomato     | 24 in          | 36 in       |
| Pepper     | 18 in          | 30 in       |
| Lettuce    | 8 in           | 12 in       |
| Carrot     | 3 in           | 12 in       |
| Bush bean  | 4 in           | 24 in       |
| Sweet corn | 10 in          | 30 in       |

Spacings from our [crop data](/data-sources/), checked against US extension sources. The
[plant spacing calculator](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) works out how many of a crop a given
bed will hold, including whether a staggered layout fits more in — and the
[yield estimator](/tools/garden-yield-estimator/) turns that into a rough harvest.

If you want to plant more densely than row spacing implies,
[how does square foot gardening work](/blog/how-does-square-foot-gardening-work/) covers the grid
method and, importantly, where it stops working.

## Rotation without a spreadsheet

Rotation exists to stop soil-borne diseases and pests building up. It needs to be simple enough
that you actually do it.

Group crops by family and move each group on one bed a year:

| Group      | Crops                                | Follows well after                   |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Legumes    | Peas, beans                          | Anything                             |
| Brassicas  | Cabbage, broccoli, kale, cauliflower | Legumes — they leave nitrogen behind |
| Roots      | Carrot, beet, parsnip                | Brassicas                            |
| Solanaceae | Tomato, potato, pepper, eggplant     | Roots                                |

With four beds that is a four-year cycle and one decision each spring: everything moves along
one. With fewer beds, the principle still holds — just do not grow tomatoes where potatoes were,
because they share blight.

## How big should the first garden be?

One 4 × 8 ft bed. Possibly two.

That is 32 square feet, which is enough to grow a genuinely useful quantity of food and small
enough to keep on top of in under half an hour a week. Almost everyone who starts bigger abandons
part of it by August, and an abandoned bed is a weed nursery that makes the rest harder.

Expand in year two, once you know which corner actually gets the sun and how much watering you
are prepared to do.

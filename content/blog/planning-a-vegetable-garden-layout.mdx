A garden layout is mostly decided by two things you cannot change — where the sun is and where
the water tap is — and one you can, which is how you divide the space. Getting the layout right
is the cheapest improvement available, because it costs only thought and it affects every season
afterwards.

## Start with sun

Watch the site for a day before you build anything. Note where the sun falls at nine, at noon and
at four.

Most vegetables want six hours of direct sun, and fruiting crops — tomatoes, peppers, squash,
cucumbers — want eight. Leafy crops and herbs will do well on four, and in high summer they
prefer some afternoon shade.

If you have a mix, allocate deliberately: the sunniest ground to the fruiting crops, the shadier
corners to salad, chard and herbs. A shady garden is not a failed garden, it is a salad garden.

## Then water

Work out how you will water before you site the beds, not after. Carrying watering cans across a
lawn twice a week in July is the kind of chore that quietly ends a garden.

Beds within hose reach of a tap get watered. Beds at the far end of the garden get watered less
than they need. If the far end is the sunny end, the answer is a longer hose or a water butt,
decided now rather than in August.

## Bed width, which is set by your arms

Four feet is the standard, and the reasoning is simple: you should never step on a bed. Footfall
compacts soil, and compacted soil holds less air and drains worse for years afterwards.

- **Four feet** if you can reach from both sides.
- **Three feet** if the bed is against a wall or fence and reachable from one side only.
- **Two and a half feet** if you are working from a seat or a kneeler.

Length is unconstrained by anything except the length of your plot and how far you are willing
to walk round.

## Paths

Make them wide enough for what will actually go down them. Eighteen inches is walkable. Two feet
takes a wheelbarrow. Three feet is comfortable with a barrow and room to kneel.

Narrow paths get trodden into the beds either side. Mulch them with bark, gravel or cardboard and
they stay usable in wet weather and grow fewer weeds.

## Which way to run rows

Less important than people think, with two exceptions.

**Tall crops go on the north side** in the northern hemisphere, whichever way the rows run.
Sweetcorn or staked tomatoes on the south side of a bed will shade everything behind them from
midsummer onwards. This is the single most common layout mistake.

**On a slope, run rows across the slope**, not down it. Rows running downhill channel water and
soil away in heavy rain.

Otherwise, run them whichever way suits the shape of the bed.

## Rotation, without a spreadsheet

Crop rotation stops soil-borne diseases and pests building up, and evens out the demands on soil
fertility. The full four-bed rotation with a fixed sequence is more bookkeeping than most gardens
need.

What matters is the family, not the crop:

- **Nightshades** — tomato, pepper, aubergine, potato
- **Brassicas** — cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, radish, turnip
- **Legumes** — beans, peas
- **Alliums** — onion, garlic, leek
- **Cucurbits** — cucumber, squash, zucchini, melon
- **Roots** — carrot, beet, parsnip

A simple, effective rule: do not grow the same family in the same bed two years running, and
follow a legume with something hungry. That catches most of the benefit.

The only real requirement is writing down what went where. A photograph of each bed in July,
kept on your phone, is enough.

## Getting more out of the space

**Go vertical.** Cucumbers, pole beans, peas and small squash on a trellis at the north edge of a
bed cost almost no ground. This is the biggest structural gain available in a small garden.

**Interplant.** Radishes along a carrot row, lettuce between young brassicas. Fast crops in space
a slow crop has not needed yet.

**Plan in rounds.** Draw an early-season layout, a main-season one and a late one rather than
trying to capture a whole year in a single plan. Squares empty and get refilled.

**Put the herbs by the door.** Anything you pick in small quantities while cooking should be the
closest thing to the kitchen. Herbs at the bottom of the garden do not get used.

## Sketch it before you build it

Graph paper, one square per square foot, is all this needs. Mark the beds, the paths, the tap, and
where the shade falls at four in the afternoon. Then place the crops.

The [square foot garden planner](/tools/square-foot-garden-planner/) will do this for a bed and
tell you how many plants each square holds, and the
[raised bed calculator](/tools/raised-bed-soil-calculator/) will tell you what filling the beds
you have drawn is going to cost before you buy any timber.

## The short version

Follow the sun, stay within hose reach, keep beds no wider than you can reach across, make paths
wide enough for a barrow, put tall crops on the north side, and write down what grew where. Then
trellis everything that climbs and replant squares the day they empty.

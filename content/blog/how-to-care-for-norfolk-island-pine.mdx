Every November, garden centers fill with small, symmetrical trees that look like miniature Christmas trees. They are Norfolk Island pines — and how to care for norfolk island pine starts with one correction: it is not a pine at all. It is *Araucaria heterophylla*, a tropical conifer from a small South Pacific island, and it wants the opposite of what a real pine wants. A real pine wants cold winters, dry air, and neglect; a Norfolk Island pine wants a bright, cool room, moist soil, and humid air. Give it those, and this November's tree can still be green and symmetrical in ten years.

That slow decline is the usual story — and it is almost always preventable. The tree tolerates low light for two or three years before dropping branches, so a bad spot looks fine until it suddenly is not. It stores no water in thick leaves the way a succulent does, so a missed watering in dry winter air browns the branch tips — and a browned branch never greens up again. And it grows from one central leader, so one bad cut at the top ends its upward growth forever. Learning those three facts early, while every tier is still green, is the whole of how to care for norfolk island pine.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are NC State Extension, Iowa State University Extension, and two UF/IFAS Extension county guides, with the New York Botanical Garden, the University of Illinois Extension, the Royal Horticultural Society, and the ASPCA corroborating. If you bought it as a living Christmas tree, the decorating section covers that.

## How to care for norfolk island pine: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright light, and turn it every week.** An east, west, or south window, a couple of hours of gentle sun, and a quarter turn every week so it grows straight. Light is the first rule of how to care for norfolk island pine, because the tree can survive dim light for years, then drop its lower branches all at once.

**Water evenly — never soggy, never bone dry.** Water thoroughly when the soil surface is dry, and empty the saucer after it drains. The tree hates both extremes: soggy soil rots the roots, and a dry root ball browns the branch tips. Even moisture is the second rule of how to care for norfolk island pine.

**Keep it cool and humid, especially in winter.** A bright, cool room at 55 to 70°F, away from radiators, vents, and cold drafts, with a humidifier or pebble tray under the pot when the heat is on. Dry winter air is the third rule of how to care for norfolk island pine — it causes most brown tips in December and January.

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A dilute liquid fertilizer every two to four weeks from spring to early fall, and nothing in autumn and winter. Over-feeding is as damaging as over-watering, and a slow-growing tree needs very little.

**Never cut the top — and never expect a brown branch back.** The tree grows from a single central leader; cutting it stops upward growth there. Remove dead lower branches, but nothing regrows from bare wood. Pruning restraint is the fifth rule of how to care for norfolk island pine, and it protects the tree's shape for a decade.

### What the five rules buy you

A tree kept to these rules does three things a neglected one does not. First, it keeps its shape: bright light and a weekly turn keep the tiers level and symmetrical, the way NC State Extension describes it — an even pyramid with branches in level tiers around a single straight trunk. A tree that leans toward one window for a year grows lopsided, and a lopsided Norfolk Island pine cannot be straightened. Second, it keeps its lower branches: even watering, cool air, and humidity stop the slow loss of the bottom tiers that is the most common complaint about this plant. A tree that keeps its lower branches at five years old is a tree that still looks like a Christmas tree; a tree that has lost them is a trunk with a tuft. Third, it stays small enough to live with: slow, steady growth of 3 to 6 inches a year, per the University of Illinois Extension, means the two-foot tree you buy this November is a five- to eight-foot floor plant after ten years, per UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County — a tree you can keep, not a tree you have to give away after two Christmases. The five rules in how to care for norfolk island pine are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the tree's own island climate, followed indoors.

## It is not a pine — know what you bought

The first thing to get straight in how to care for norfolk island pine is what the plant actually is, because the name misleads in both halves. It is not a pine — it is an *Araucaria*, an ancient group of conifers from the Southern Hemisphere — and it is not from a mainland, but from Norfolk Island, a small island in the Pacific Ocean between New Zealand and New Caledonia. NC State Extension says the *Araucaria* genus holds approximately 19 species of pine-like conifer trees indigenous to the Southern Hemisphere, and the Norfolk Island pine is endemic to that one island: it grows naturally nowhere else on Earth. It is not native to North America at all, but it is suitable for growing indoors as a houseplant — and that suitability, not hardiness, is why it is in your living room.

In its native habitat the tree is enormous. NC State Extension says it reaches heights of 200 feet where it grows wild, and cultivated trees grown in subtropical or Mediterranean climates typically grow to 60 to 100 feet with a 12- to 60-foot spread. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County gives the same scale in plainer terms: it is possible for this plant to reach heights of 200 feet, but it more commonly grows up to 80 feet. That is the tree's true size, and it is worth knowing before you buy one, because the two-foot tree in the garden center is a seedling of a forest giant. Indoors, NC State Extension says it will reach heights of 9 feet — and slowly, which is the only reason it works as a houseplant at all.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a small evergreen with a single straight trunk and branches arranged in symmetrical, horizontal tiers — whorls — around it. NC State Extension describes it precisely: a graceful pyramidal shape with graceful, wide-spaced branches symmetrically arranged in whorled, horizontal tiers around a single straight trunk, with typically five branches per whorl. The leaves are saber-like and turn inward in an overlapping herringbone pattern on the tiered branches. That herringbone pattern is how you tell a healthy tree from a struggling one: on a healthy tree the needles lie flat and overlap neatly, tier after tier; on a dry or light-starved tree the needles curl, brown at the tips, and the tiers thin out from the bottom up. Learning to read the tiers is the first practical skill in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it takes about ten seconds once you know what to look for.

### The other names on the label

The other name worth knowing is the one the ASPCA uses: Australian pine. The Norfolk Island pine's other common names, per NC State Extension, include Australian pine, house pine, living Christmas tree, Norfolk pine, Polynesian pine, star pine, and triangle tree. "Living Christmas tree" is the name that matters in November, because that is how the tree is sold — and the decorating section below treats it as one. But the tree is not a Christmas tree in the botanical sense, and the care that keeps a cut fir alive for a month will kill a Norfolk Island pine in a season. It is a tropical tree wearing a Christmas-tree shape, and how to care for norfolk island pine means caring for the tropical tree, not the shape — which is why every rule in this guide is written for the island tree, not for the pine it resembles.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a tree will be in how to care for norfolk island pine, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is symmetry: the tiers should be level and even all the way around, with five branches per whorl and no bare side. A tree that already leans, or has one thin side, will keep that shape — the tree grows from its top, and a lopsided young tree becomes a lopsided old tree. The second is the condition of the lower branches: they should be green to the tips, with no browning and no bare twigs at the bottom. Brown lower branches mean the tree has already been stressed — dry air, a missed watering, or too little light at the garden center — and those branches will not recover. The third is size: a tree 1 to 2 feet tall in a 6-inch pot is the usual shop size, and it is the right size to buy, because the tree grows only 3 to 6 inches a year indoors, per the University of Illinois Extension. A bigger tree costs more and gives you the same plant a year or two later. Buy the small, symmetrical, fully green one, and the rest of how to care for norfolk island pine is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for norfolk island pine, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the tree forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the tree shows it in the spring, tier by tier.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the tree survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. Iowa State University Extension says to place the Norfolk Island pine in a brightly lit location near an east, west, or south window. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County agrees and adds the detail that matters: although the tree will adapt to bright indirect light, it will look its best with a couple of hours of direct sunlight daily. NC State Extension says full sun is normally best for developing compact, symmetrical growth, but light afternoon shade may be needed to avoid possible foliage bleach — yellow tints on the needles. The planning figure this guide uses is bright light near an east, west, or south window, with a couple of hours of gentle direct sun, and shade from harsh midday sun through glass in summer. That range satisfies all three sources, and it is the light that keeps the tiers tight and level.

### The delayed warning: two to three years of dim light

The warning that matters most is the delayed one. NC State Extension says that indoors, the tree tolerates very low light for 2 to 3 years but may then display leaf drop and loss of lower branches. Read that again: two to three years of looking fine in a dim corner, and then the lower branches start to go. That is why so many Norfolk Island pines die in their third year in a dark living room — the owner did nothing different, and the tree had been quietly starving for light the whole time. Fix the light first, while the lower branches are still green, and the rest of how to care for norfolk island pine gets easier: a tree with its tiers intact is a tree you can keep, and a tree that has already lost them is a tree you are trying to save. If your tree sits more than a few feet from a window, or in a north-facing room with no direct sun at all, move it closer to the glass now. A full-spectrum grow light above the top of the tree is a reasonable stand-in for a bright window if the window is the problem. That light is the cheapest insurance in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Turn it every week

The second light habit is turning. Iowa State University Extension says to rotate the plant weekly to prevent it from growing toward the light and becoming lopsided. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County is more precise: rotate the plant a quarter turn weekly to keep it from tilting toward one side. UF/IFAS Extension's Gardening Solutions guide says the same — turn the plant every week or so to keep it growing straight and upright. A quarter turn a week is the habit: pick a day, turn the pot, and the tree grows straight. Skip it for a season and the trunk leans, the tiers go uneven, and the symmetry that makes the tree worth having is gone. No amount of turning afterward will straighten a trunk that has already set its lean. That weekly quarter turn is the smallest job in how to care for norfolk island pine, and the one most often skipped.

### Water

Watering is where most Norfolk Island pines are lost, and the loss usually comes from kindness: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain. The rule from Iowa State University Extension is the one to follow exactly — thoroughly water the Norfolk Island pine when the soil surface becomes dry to the touch, and discard the excess water which drains out the bottom of the pot. Two halves, both required: water thoroughly, and then remove the excess. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. Discarding the excess means emptying the saucer, so the pot never sits in water. A tree that sits in a saucer of water rots from the roots up, and the first sign is lower branches turning brown and dropping — the same symptom as drought, which is why so many owners respond to over-watering by watering more.

NC State Extension gives the seasonal version of the same rule: water regularly from spring to autumn, avoiding soil that is too wet, and in winter, mist in heated rooms and water sparingly. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County adds the judgment call — be sure to plant it in well-drained soil and err on the side of less water. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County is blunter still: it is very important to be careful not to over-water, because over-watering will cause potted Norfolk Island pines to drop the lower foliage.

### How often to water

The planning figure this guide uses is simple: check the top inch of soil with a finger, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — about once a week in a warm room in summer, stretching to every two weeks or longer in a cool room in winter. That range satisfies all four sources, and it keeps the soil evenly moist without ever staying soggy. That finger test is the daily habit in how to care for norfolk island pine: it takes five seconds, and it prevents both kinds of watering mistakes at once.

### The pot and the saucer A decorative cache pot with no drainage is a slow death for this tree — water collects at the bottom where you cannot see it, and the roots sit in it for weeks. If your tree came in a foil-wrapped pot or a cache pot with no holes, take it out, water it in the sink, let it drain fully, and put it back. The second is to water with room-temperature water rather than cold water straight from the tap, which is simply less of a shock to a tropical root ball. The thorough watering itself, and the empty saucer, are the rules in how to care for norfolk island pine, and they come straight from Iowa State University Extension.

### Temperature

Temperature is the easiest of the three to get right, because the range the tree wants is the range a house already has. The sources give a small spread, and it is worth showing honestly. NC State Extension says the tree indoors prefers a bright, cool room at 55 to 65 degrees Fahrenheit. Iowa State University Extension says a temperature of 55 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit is suitable. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County says the ideal indoor climate is bright and cool, with daytime temperatures ranging from 60 to 70 degrees and slightly cooler at night. The planning figure this guide uses is 55 to 70°F, in a bright, cool room — that range satisfies all three sources, and it is ordinary indoor temperature in a house that is not overheated. That is why temperature is the easiest of the three decisions in how to care for norfolk island pine: the house already provides it, and the job is simply not to put the tree somewhere hotter or colder than the room itself.

What matters more than the exact number is stability — and avoiding the two extremes, cold and heat.

### What to avoid: cold and heat

UF/IFAS Extension notes that the tree cannot tolerate temperatures below 35°F, and Nassau County says it is not a plant to recommend putting outside because of the chance of freeze damage. Keep it away from cold drafts, drafty windows, and exterior doors in winter — a chill that a real pine would ignore can damage this tropical tree, and the damage shows as browning and dropped branches. The second is heat: do not put the tree next to a radiator, a heating vent, or a fireplace. Hot, dry air is the combination that browns the tips fastest, and a tree that sits over a heating vent in January will lose its lower branches by March. Cool and steady is the temperature rule in how to care for norfolk island pine, and a room at 60 to 65°F away from heat sources is very nearly ideal.

### Humidity

Humidity is the winter problem, and the one most owners never think about. The Norfolk Island pine evolved on a mild, humid oceanic island, and the dry air of a centrally heated home in winter is genuinely hostile to it. Iowa State University Extension names the problem directly: winter is often a difficult time because of low relative humidity levels in most homes. Humidity is the quiet half of how to care for norfolk island pine in winter, and it is the half most owners miss entirely — because unlike a missed watering, dry air leaves no puddle and no smell, only brown tips weeks later. The fix it gives is practical: raise the humidity level around the Norfolk Island pine with a humidifier or place the plant on a pebble tray. NC State Extension gives the same advice in its own words: in winter, mist in heated rooms. Purdue University, as cited in corroborating sources, puts a number on it — the tree thrives around 50 percent humidity.

### The pebble tray and the humidifier

The planning figure this guide uses is about 50 percent relative humidity in winter, raised with a humidifier or a pebble tray, not by over-watering. That last point matters: UF/IFAS Extension's Gardening Solutions guide warns that over-watering is not the answer to dry air, and instead describes the pebble tray — fill a saucer with water and rocks or gravel, then place the potted plant on top, making sure the pot is not sitting directly in the water. A humidifier running near the plant is the most effective tool, and a pebble tray is a reasonable substitute in a small room. NC State Extension also recommends misting in heated rooms in winter, which helps briefly. Get the humidity right and the branch tips stay green through the heating season; get it wrong and the tips brown from the top of each tier downward, which is the most common winter complaint in how to care for norfolk island pine.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

The soil rule is short: loose, acid, and free-draining, in a pot with holes. NC State Extension says to use fertile, porous, sandy, peaty, slightly acidic, well-drained potting soil. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says an acid, fertile soil mixture is recommended as a potting medium, making sure that the pot has good drainage. Iowa State University Extension does not specify a mix, but its watering rule — water thoroughly and discard the excess — only works in a mix that drains. The planning figure this guide uses is a porous, slightly acidic potting mix: ordinary potting soil lightened with perlite, or a cactus and succulent mix with extra potting soil for moisture retention. Free drainage is the foundation of how to care for norfolk island pine in a pot, because every watering rule in this guide assumes the excess can leave. The comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite is the right lightener here: it drains and does not hold water the way vermiculite does, and this tree needs drainage more than it needs moisture retention.

### Repotting: rarely, gently, one size up

Repotting is the part of how to care for norfolk island pine that surprises people, because the answer is to do it rarely. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says the trees have weak root systems and do best on the pot-bound side, with repotting every four years or so. That is the planning figure: repot every three to four years, in spring, only when the roots circle the pot or poke out of the drainage holes, or when the soil dries out very fast after watering. When you do repot, go only one pot size up — an inch or two wider — and keep the tree at the same depth, with the root ball intact. Do not break the root ball apart or wash the old soil off: the weak, shallow roots resent disturbance, and a rough repotting can set the tree back a whole season. A stake may be needed for support if the tree becomes leggy or droopy, per UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County, so tie it loosely to a bamboo cane rather than letting it lean. A tree that is repotted gently, rarely, and one size up will keep growing steadily for years; a tree that is repotted often and roughly will drop branches every time. That restraint is the repotting rule in how to care for norfolk island pine.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. NC State Extension says to consider weekly or biweekly applications of dilute liquid fertilizer from spring through fall. Iowa State University Extension says that from spring to early fall, you should fertilize the plant with a dilute fertilizer solution every 2 to 4 weeks. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says containerized plants should be fed with most any liquid fertilizer at normal strength once every two weeks or according to label instructions during the active growing season. The planning figure this guide uses is a dilute liquid fertilizer every 2 to 4 weeks from spring to early fall, and nothing at all in autumn and winter. That range satisfies all three sources — it is the middle of the spread, it is dilute rather than full-strength, and it stops when growth stops. All three sources agree on the season, and on stopping when growth stops, which is the part of how to care for norfolk island pine that matters most: a tree that is fed only while it is growing never accumulates the salts that burn its roots.

The reason for the caution is in the Charlotte County guide: it is very important to be careful not to over-fertilize, because over-fertilizing, like over-watering, will cause potted Norfolk Island pines to drop their lower foliage.

### What over-feeding does

A slow-growing tree that puts on 3 to 6 inches a year does not need much food, and a tree that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half the label strength, or a balanced fertilizer diluted further if your tap water is hard. Feed only when the soil is already moist, never onto dry soil, and skip a feeding if the tree has just been repotted or is showing stress. That light hand is the feeding rule in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it is the cheapest of the five rules to follow: a single bottle of balanced liquid feed lasts years at this rate.

## Decorating it as a living Christmas tree

The reason most Norfolk Island pines are bought in November is the living Christmas tree, and the tree takes to the role well — with light decorations and a short season. Iowa State University Extension says that during the holiday season, many individuals turn their plants into living Christmas trees by decorating them with miniature lights, ribbons, and ornaments. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says the trees will also tolerate small bulbs, tinsel, and bows when decorated for the Christmas season. The key word in both sources is small: miniature lights, small bulbs, light ribbons. The branches are soft and flexible, and they are not built to carry weight. Heavy ornaments bend the tiers down, and a bent tier does not spring back — the branch stays drooped, and the symmetry is spoiled for the season. That weight limit is the first decorating fact in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it is the one most people learn the hard way.

### Four decorating rules

Four decorating rules keep the tree safe. First, use miniature LED lights only, and keep them cool — LEDs give off almost no heat, while old incandescent miniature bulbs warm the needles and dry them out. Second, hang ornaments from the branches, never wire them tightly around a branch or the trunk, and keep the total weight light: a few small baubles per tier, not a bauble on every branch. Third, do not spray the tree with artificial snow or flocking. Flocking coats the needles, blocks light, and is very hard to remove without damaging the soft foliage — and nothing in the extension sources recommends it. Fourth, keep the decorated tree in its bright, cool spot, not next to the fireplace or the television, and keep watering it on the same schedule. A tree that is moved to a dark corner for December, or left unwatered while the house is full of guests, will pay for it in January with brown tips and dropped lower branches. Decorate it where it lives, lightly, and the living Christmas tree is the easiest part of how to care for norfolk island pine.

### After the holidays: take it off promptly

After the holidays, take the decorations off promptly. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says that after the holidays, you should remove the decorations and use the tree as a floor plant for patios, pool cages, and Florida rooms year-round. In most of the United States that means back to the bright window, not the patio — but the point is the same: the tree goes back to being a houseplant in January, and the sooner the lights come off, the sooner it goes back to growing. A tree that wears lights until February is a tree that spent two months in the dark under a tangle of wire, and it will show it. Undecorate in the first week of January, give it a quarter turn, check the soil, and the tree starts the new year clean. That January reset is a small habit in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it is what separates the trees that last a decade from the trees that last one Christmas.

### A worked example: one tree, bought today

Here is the whole first year as a single story, for a tree bought on Tuesday, 17 November 2026. The tree is 2 feet tall in a 6-inch pot, symmetrical, with five branches per whorl and green tiers all the way to the bottom. You set it near an east-facing window, where it gets a couple of hours of morning sun, and you mark a day each week — Sunday, say — for the quarter turn. You water it thoroughly when the top inch of soil feels dry, empty the saucer every time, and you do not feed it at all: it is November, and feeding waits for spring. In early December you decorate it with miniature LED lights and a few small baubles, light enough that no tier bends, and you keep it in the same bright spot, watering on the same schedule. In the first week of January you take the decorations off, give it a quarter turn, and set it back to being a houseplant.

Spring comes, and the feeding starts: a dilute liquid fertilizer every two to four weeks from spring to early fall, per Iowa State University Extension, and nothing in winter. The tree grows 3 to 6 inches over the year, per the University of Illinois Extension, and by next November it is a little over 2 feet tall, a little wider, and ready to be a living Christmas tree again — this time with a year of your care behind it.

### Ten years on

You repot it in the spring of 2030, about four years on, one pot size up, keeping the root ball intact, per UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County. After ten years of that routine, the tree is five to eight feet tall, per the same source, and it has been a living Christmas tree ten times over. That is the whole story of how to care for norfolk island pine: a weekly turn, a finger in the soil, a cool room, a little food in summer, and no cuts at the top.

## Troubleshooting

Most Norfolk Island pine problems have one of three causes — water, light, or humidity — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the tree grows slowly and recovers slowly. A browned branch will not green up again, so troubleshooting in how to care for norfolk island pine is mostly about protecting the branches that are still green.

### Brown branch tips

Brown tips are the most common complaint, and the most useful symptom, because they point at the cause. Iowa State University Extension says low relative humidity, insufficient light, or excessively dry soil may induce browning of branch tips and lead to the loss of the lower branches. Check the three causes in order. First, the soil: push a finger an inch into the mix. If it is bone dry all the way down, the tree has been under-watered, and the fix is a thorough watering now, then the regular check. Second, the light: if the tree sits far from a window, move it closer to the glass. Third, the humidity: if the heat has been on for weeks and the air feels dry, set up a humidifier or a pebble tray. Do not cut the brown tips off the needles — the brown part is dead, but trimming it leaves a blunt end that looks worse and does not help the branch. Leave the tips alone, fix the cause, and judge the tree by its new growth, not by the browned tips. That patience is the troubleshooting habit in how to care for norfolk island pine.

### Dropping lower branches

A tree that drops its lower branches is a tree that has been stressed for a while, and the stress is usually one of the same three causes, left uncorrected. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says over-watering and excessive dry indoor air will cause potted Norfolk Island pines to drop the lower foliage. NC State Extension says the tree tolerates very low light for 2 to 3 years but may then display leaf drop and loss of lower branches. UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County adds the nuance: it is not unusual for leaves on the bottom to turn brown and drop off, especially when adjusting to a new site and light conditions — but if large numbers of brown leaves occur, it often means too much water or root decay. A few brown lower leaves in the first weeks after you bring the tree home is normal adjustment. A steady loss of whole lower tiers over months is not. Check the saucer first — if the pot has been sitting in water, the roots may be rotting, and the fix is to let the soil dry out, improve the drainage, and water only when the surface is dry. Then check the light and the humidity. A branch that has dropped is gone for good: the tree does not regrow needles from bare old wood, so the goal is to stop the loss, not to reverse it. Stopping the loss is the whole of the fix in how to care for norfolk island pine.

### Lopsided growth

A tree that leans toward the window, or grows fuller on one side, has not been turned. Iowa State University Extension says to rotate the plant weekly to prevent it from growing toward the light and becoming lopsided, and UF/IFAS Extension Nassau County says to rotate it a quarter turn weekly. Start the habit now, and the new growth will come in evenly — but the lean that is already there will not straighten on its own. If the trunk has already set a lean, turn the thin side toward the window and give it a season: the new tiers will grow toward the light and even the shape out over time, though the lower trunk will keep its bend. A loose tie to a bamboo cane can hold a leaning young tree upright while it corrects, per UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County, which says a stake may be needed for support if a tree becomes leggy or droopy. Tie it loosely, with soft twine, and remove the stake once the trunk holds itself straight. That weekly quarter turn is the prevention in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it costs nothing.

### Leggy or droopy growth

A tree that stretches upward with wide gaps between the tiers, or whose branches droop instead of holding level, is usually short of light — and sometimes over-fed. Move it closer to the window first, and check the feeding: if you have been feeding through the winter, stop, and feed only from spring to early fall at half strength. A leggy tree will not tighten up again — the gaps between the tiers are permanent — but new growth in good light will come in tighter, and the tree will look better with every new whorl. Do not try to fix legginess by cutting the top off: the New York Botanical Garden says that if you cut off the top, the tree stops growing from that point, and the result is a tree that never grows taller and never looks right. The fix for a leggy tree is light and time, not the secateurs, and that restraint is the hardest lesson in how to care for norfolk island pine.

### Pests

The Norfolk Island pine is generally a healthy tree indoors, and pest trouble is uncommon. NC State Extension says the tree is generally pest free but is susceptible to scale, sooty mold, and leaf spot. Scale shows as small brown bumps on the stems and the undersides of the needles, and it is the pest to watch for: check the undersides of the branches when you water, and wipe off any scale you find with a damp cloth. Sooty mold is a black, powdery coating that grows on the honeydew scale insects leave behind — it follows the scale, so controlling the scale controls the mold. Leaf spot shows as dark spots on the needles, and it follows wet foliage and poor air circulation, so water the soil, not the needles, and give the tree space from the wall. None of these is common on a tree kept in bright light with even watering, and none of them needs a spray for a houseplant: wipe, improve the air flow, and fix the watering. That light touch is the pest rule in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it keeps the tree safe for the room it lives in.

## Is norfolk island pine poisonous to pets?

No — and that is one of the easier safety answers in how to care for norfolk island pine. The ASPCA lists the Norfolk Island pine, under its other common name Australian pine, as non-toxic to cats, dogs, and horses. NC State Extension likewise lists no toxicity for the plant. That makes it one of the safer plants on the holiday windowsill, and the comparison with its neighbors is worth making: the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is non-toxic too, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, and the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) and [paperwhite bulbs](/blog/how-to-force-paperwhite-bulbs/) are toxic. The Norfolk Island pine sits on the safe end of that row, next to the Christmas cactus.

A pet that chews the needles may still get an upset stomach — the needles are sharp and fibrous, and any plant material in quantity can cause vomiting — so keep the tree where pets cannot make a habit of chewing it, and keep a small tree out of reach of a determined cat. But there is no poisoning risk of the kind amaryllis carries, and no emergency call to make if a dog takes one bite. If a pet eats a large amount of any plant, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. For a household with cats, dogs, and small children, the Norfolk Island pine is the living Christmas tree with the fewest safety worries — and that, along with the fact that it lives on after Christmas, is a genuine reason to choose it over a cut tree. That safety is a quiet advantage in how to care for norfolk island pine, and it is worth knowing before you buy one for a house with a curious cat.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Non-toxic does not mean chew-proof. A cat that treats the lower tiers as a toy will strip the needles and break the branches, and a branch that is broken off does not grow back. A dog that knocks the pot over will break the pot, the tree, or both. Keep a small tree on a sturdy stand or a table where pets cannot reach the lowest tier, and keep a floor plant in a heavy pot that cannot be tipped. The tree's own fragility is the reason to keep it out of reach, not its chemistry — and that is the one safety rule that applies to every plant on the holiday windowsill, toxic or not. A Norfolk Island pine that survives the pets survives the decade, and that survival is the whole point of how to care for norfolk island pine.

## The short version

How to care for norfolk island pine, in one paragraph: buy a small, symmetrical tree with green tiers all the way to the bottom; set it in a bright, cool room at 55 to 70°F near an east, west, or south window, with a couple of hours of gentle direct sun, and turn the pot a quarter turn every week so it grows straight; water thoroughly when the top inch of soil feels dry, let the excess drain away, and empty the saucer — about once a week in summer, every two weeks or longer in a cool room in winter; raise the humidity with a humidifier or a pebble tray when the heat is on, aiming for about 50 percent; feed a dilute liquid fertilizer every two to four weeks from spring to early fall, and nothing in autumn and winter; repot every three to four years, one pot size up, keeping the root ball intact; decorate it for Christmas with miniature LED lights and a few small baubles, then take the decorations off in the first week of January; never cut the top, never shorten a healthy branch, and remember that a browned branch never greens up again. Do that, and the two-foot tree you buy this November will be a five- to eight-foot floor plant after ten years — and a living Christmas tree ten times over. If you want a second holiday plant for the same windowsill, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) blooms in the same cool room, the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) flowers on the same short days with none of the watering worry, and the [cyclamen](/blog/how-to-care-for-cyclamen/) keeps blooming for up to three months in the same bright, cool spot.

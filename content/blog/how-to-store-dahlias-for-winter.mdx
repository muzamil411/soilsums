The first hard frost turns dahlia leaves black overnight, and that is the moment the question of how to store dahlias for winter stops being theoretical. The flowers are finished either way — frost ends the show — but the tubers underneath are still alive, and whether they see next summer depends on what happens in the next few weeks. Left in frozen ground in a cold climate, they rot or freeze. Lifted, dried, and kept cool, the same clump comes back, and one clump divided in spring becomes several plants. This guide to how to store dahlias for winter follows the extension services that publish the method: when to dig, how to lift without breaking the necks, how long to dry, what to pack them in, and the temperature that keeps them dormant until spring.

## Do you have to dig them up at all?

Before the shovel comes out, the zone question decides whether how to store dahlias for winter means digging at all. Dahlias are tender perennials — tropical in origin — and unlike tulips they have no built-in winter plan for cold ground. Washington State University Extension lays out the rule by zone, and it is the clearest version published: in zones 8 to 10, where winter temperatures rarely dip below 20 degrees Fahrenheit, cut the plants to the ground and leave the tubers where they are. In zone 7, dahlias may survive if the winter is temperate and water does not funnel into the tubers causing rot — but the safe play, WSU says, is to dig the ones you cannot bear to lose. In zones 3 to 6, digging and storing is required, because the tubers are thin-skinned and will be destroyed if a freeze reaches a depth of 6 inches in the garden.

Penn State Extension reaches the same conclusion from the cold end. Writing from zone 5b, Master Gardener Mary Niezelski says dahlias there must be dug and stored, and that heavy freezing and winter rains can promote rotting, especially in USDA Hardiness Zones 6 and lower. She adds the one exception that proves the rule: she has inadvertently left some tubers in the ground in protected areas, like close to the house, and they have survived — but that is luck with a microclimate, not a method. The University of Wisconsin Extension states the general case even more plainly: tender bulbs, dahlias included, do not survive Wisconsin's cold winters outdoors.

### Zone 7: the borderline

Zone 7 deserves a second look, because it is where the most tubers are lost to optimism. WSU's in-ground procedure for the gamblers is specific: after the first killing frost, or by mid-November, whichever comes first, cut the stalks to below ground level, inspect where the stalk connects with the tuber, and cut off any hollow stalk that remains until there are no tube-like protrusions that could catch and funnel water down into the plant. Then cover the area with plastic to keep the winter rains at bay, and add a few inches of mulch, soil, leaves, straw, or compost over the area for protection from severe cold, removing the extra layers in March. That is a real method, not a shrug — but WSU is equally clear that treating dahlias as annuals and planting a fresh bunch in spring is a legitimate choice many Pacific Northwest gardeners prefer. If you are in zone 7 and the variety matters to you, dig it — that is the cautious half of how to store dahlias for winter. ## When to dig: after the first killing frost

The digging signal is the frost, not the calendar — and the extension services agree on the shape of the window even though their dates differ. That window is the opening move of how to store dahlias for winter: everything after it assumes the tubers were dug at the right time. Iowa State University Extension says to cut back the plants to within 2 to 4 inches of the ground within three or four days of a killing frost, then leave the dahlias in the ground for an additional six or seven days to cure before digging. Washington State University Extension gives the same shape: after a hard frost, or in mid-November if frost has not come, cut the stalks to 6 inches above the ground and wait at least a week before digging, to force the eye of the plant to develop and make dividing easier. The University of Wisconsin Extension adds the deadline: if the foliage is killed by frost, dig the bulb within a few days, so rot-causing organisms do not enter through the damaged above-ground parts.

### Two timing mistakes to avoid

Two details in that timing are easy to get wrong. First, do not cut the stems down early and leave hollow tubes standing. The University of New Hampshire Extension warns to delay cutting dahlia stems until right before digging, because the stems are hollow and can collect water, which in turn promotes crown rot and tuber decay. Cut when you are ready to dig, not a week before. Second, do not dig too early in the season even if the plants look finished. Penn State Extension says dahlias need to be in the ground for at least 120 days to produce mature tubers for storage, and UNH Extension agrees the roots are not mature until late in the growing season: the longer they are kept in the ground, the more mature they will be, and the better their chances of surviving in storage. A dahlia planted late and dug on the frost schedule may still be immature — that is a planting-date problem, not a storage problem, and no packing method fixes it. It is the one part of how to store dahlias for winter that is decided in spring, not fall. Note the contrast with the spring bulbs in our [tulip planting guide](/blog/when-to-plant-tulip-bulbs/): tulips go into the ground in fall and stay there, while dahlias come out of the ground in fall and go into storage — the same frost ends one season and starts the other job.

## How to store dahlias for winter, step by step

The method is six moves — cut, wait, dig, wash, dry, pack — and each one has a reason the sources state plainly. Together they are how to store dahlias for winter in practice. Skipping one does not always kill the tubers, but the failures in storage almost always trace back to one of them.

### Cut the stalks, then wait

Cut the stalks down to a few inches once the killing frost has blackened the tops, then leave the tubers in the ground for about a week. Iowa State puts the cut at 2 to 4 inches, within three or four days of the killing frost, followed by six or seven days in the ground to cure. WSU puts the cut at 6 inches after a hard frost, or by mid-November, followed by at least a week before digging. The week matters for two reasons: the skins toughen, and the eyes — the growing points for next year's stems — develop and become easier to find when you divide. That week of waiting is not idle time — it is part of how to store dahlias for winter, and it is what makes the eyes findable when you divide. Label the plants before you cut if you grow more than one variety: WSU says to write the varietal name or code number on a tag, or directly on the tuber with a permanent marker, and UNH Extension says a felt-tipped pen can be used to write directly onto the tubers. Labels tied on after the tops are gone are guesses.

### Dig without breaking the necks

The neck is the weak point of the whole operation. The University of New Hampshire Extension warns that the neck on dahlia tubers is relatively delicate and can be easily damaged while digging, and that any damage lets disease organisms in, leading to rot and storage losses. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same thing in practical terms: loosen the roots gently by digging several inches back from the base of the plant on all sides before lifting the clump, avoid cutting or bruising the bulbs, and take special care not to rub off the skin of dahlia tubers, because pathogens can readily enter through injured areas. WSU is more specific about distance: dig down on all four sides about a foot away from the stalk and lift the clumps carefully so their necks do not break. A digging fork is less likely than a shovel to cause injury, per Wisconsin, and Iowa State says to dig carefully with a spade or shovel and gently shake off the soil. Never pull the clump up by the stem — the neck of each tuber breaks easily, and a tuber with a broken neck is a tuber with an open door for rot. Neck damage is the most common physical mistake in how to store dahlias for winter.

### Wash, or leave the soil on

Here the sources disagree about one step of how to store dahlias for winter, and the disagreement is worth stating rather than smoothing over. The Extension system's Ask Extension service recommends rinsing the tubers, since it removes clumps of soil that might be holding a lot of moisture and microorganisms that could potentially lead to rotting in storage — and rinsing makes the tubers easier to divide. Iowa State washes the clumps to remove any remaining soil, then lets them dry for 24 hours. UNH Extension swishes them in a tub of water or uses a garden hose, because soil contains microorganisms that can cause decay in storage. WSU hoses off the excess dirt and trims all feeder roots and long tapered roots.

The exception is Penn State Extension: if you are storing the clump whole, without dividing, it recommends not washing the soil off — though that needs more storage space. The logic is consistent once you see it: washing is part of dividing, and an undivided clump keeps its soil as a buffer. So the practical rule for how to store dahlias for winter is to wash when you divide and leave the soil on when you store whole clumps. Either way, discard any tubers that are obviously damaged — Wisconsin says so directly — because a damaged tuber in a shared box is a rot source for the healthy ones.

### Dry and cure before packing

Wet tubers in a closed box are the single fastest way to lose the whole batch, and every source gives a drying step before packing. The times differ, so treat them as a range with a shared test: the tubers should feel dry to the touch before they are packed. Iowa State allows the washed clumps to dry for 24 hours. WSU dries clumps overnight in a cool, dry location and warns not to leave them in the sun. The University of Wisconsin Extension cures most tender bulbs for 1 to 3 days, and dahlias specifically for several hours, out of direct sunlight in a dry, well-ventilated area at 60 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit. UNH Extension cures the tubers in a well-ventilated area at a constant 60 to 70 degrees, out of direct sunlight, for a few days.

Penn State's Master Gardener is the outlier on time, and her reason is worth hearing: she originally let her tubers dry for the recommended 24 to 48 hours, but found that they needed more time to dry or they were more prone to rot, and now lets them dry for four or five days out of the sun. That is one gardener's experience, not a new rule — but it is a reminder that how to store dahlias for winter depends on the drying spot as much as on the calendar. A dry garage in November cures tubers faster than a damp basement. Judge by feel, not by the clock: dry to the touch, skins set, cut surfaces sealed — that is the test that decides whether how to store dahlias for winter starts with sound tubers. WSU adds the same rule for divided tubers: cut surfaces should be dried overnight before storing.

### Divide now, or in spring

Dividing is the other honest split in how to store dahlias for winter, and both sides have an extension service behind them. Penn State divides in the fall: the tubers are more manageable to cut, but the eyes are more visible in the spring, so some eyes are lost this way. Ask Extension reports the same trade-off from the commercial side: most commercial dahlia growers divide in the fall, when the tubers are more tender and easier to cut, and it makes them easier to store and quicker to plant in the spring — but the eye is harder to find in the fall, so many people cut the big clumps into manageable smaller clumps of whole tubers and divide those in spring, when the eyes are easier to see and many have started to sprout. The University of Wisconsin Extension takes the spring side outright: wait until spring to divide dug bulbs if they have multiplied, as injury increases the chance of rot in storage.

### What a viable tuber needs

Whichever season you choose, the anatomy rule does not change. Penn State states it plainly: viable tubers are always attached to the old stalk, and each tuber must have a neck and a crown to get a viable eye — and necks that are less than the diameter of a pencil will probably dry out by spring. WSU describes the eye itself: the small white or pinkish swollen dot located around the base of the old stem, the growing point for next year's stem — and if you find a tuber without an eye, or one growing out of another tuber, discard it, because it is unlikely to produce a healthy new plant. A tuber without an eye is not a plant in waiting; it is compost. That is the test to apply before anything goes into the box: no eye, no storage — the first sorting rule of how to store dahlias for winter. Cut with a sharp paring knife or a hooked-blade knife, per WSU, and let the cut surfaces dry overnight before storing.

### Pack them and put them somewhere cool

Packing is moisture management, not just boxing — and it is the step of how to store dahlias for winter where most tubers are actually lost. Iowa State places the clumps upside down in cardboard boxes and covers them with vermiculite, peat moss, or wood shavings. The University of Wisconsin Extension stores the dried bulbs between 2- to 3-inch layers of peat moss, sand, vermiculite, sawdust, wood shavings, or coir, in a ventilated container such as a cardboard box — and warns not to pack the bulbs in air-tight containers, which can cause moisture build-up and promote decay, and to keep the individual bulbs from touching, to prevent the spread of rot between them. UNH Extension packs the cured tubers in moistened vermiculite, wood shavings, or sand, inside plastic bags or cardboard boxes, covered with more storage medium — a little moisture goes a long way, and if the medium is too wet, the tubers will likely rot. Ask Extension lists the same three materials — vermiculite, wood shavings, or sand — and says they all can work, and it usually comes down to personal preference, as long as the material conserves moisture within the tubers while preventing fungus and rot.

### The storage spot

The storage spot itself is the other half of packing. The number every dahlia-specific source agrees on is 40 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit: Iowa State, Penn State, and Ask Extension all give it. A root cellar, an unheated basement, a garage that does not freeze, or an attic are the spots Ask Extension names. Penn State adds two practical details: store the tubers off cement floors, and move the containers into a temporarily warmer area if air temperatures drop toward 0 degrees. If you are weighing the method against the other cool-storage job in the garden, our [potato storage guide](/blog/how-to-store-potatoes/) runs on the same principle as how to store dahlias for winter — cool, dark, ventilated, checked monthly — but potatoes want it colder and damper than dahlias do, so do not store the two crops by the same numbers.

## The temperature question, honestly

The 40 to 50 degree figure is the consensus, but it is not the only number an extension service publishes, and the spread is worth showing rather than hiding. Iowa State, Penn State, and the Extension system's Ask Extension service all say 40 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit for dahlias specifically. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives 35 to 45 degrees at 50 percent relative humidity — but that is its general figure for tender bulbs as a group, not a dahlia-specific number. Washington State University Extension gives 40 to 60 degrees for its dig-and-store method, the widest band of the lot. UNH Extension gives the range in words rather than numbers: kept chilled, below 50 degrees but above freezing.

So the practical answer for how to store dahlias for winter is 40 to 50 degrees, with the edges understood. Below freezing, the tubers are ruined — Ask Extension says freezing temperatures ruin tubers, and WSU says a frozen tuber is a dead tuber. Above 50 degrees, two things go wrong at once: Ask Extension warns that higher temperatures encourage microorganisms and fungus and prompt the tubers to develop sprouts too soon, and WSU says tubers will shrivel if too warm. The most common real-world failure is not a thermometer reading of 52 degrees — it is a heated basement at 65 to 70 degrees, where the tubers neither stay dormant nor stay dry. That warm basement is the single most common way how to store dahlias for winter goes wrong in practice. If the only cool spot you have runs warm, the tubers are better off in the coolest corner of it, checked more often, than in a warm room where they will sprout in January and shrivel by March. A warm spot checked often still beats a warm room ignored — that is the practical compromise inside how to store dahlias for winter.

## The container argument: cardboard or plastic bins

Ask five extension gardeners what to pack dahlias in and you get a small argument about moisture, and the argument is really about whose basement is damp. Iowa State uses cardboard boxes. The University of Wisconsin Extension uses a ventilated container such as a cardboard box, and warns against air-tight containers. Ask Extension says the tubers can go in Styrofoam, wood, or cardboard containers — and adds that if you use a wood or cardboard box, moisture could escape, so line all sides with newspaper at least eight sheets thick and keep the top closed. WSU stores in a cardboard box, or lines containers with 10 to 12 sheets of newspaper.

Penn State's Master Gardener takes the other side, and her reason is her own damp basement: she uses plastic bins with lids and drills holes in the sides for air flow, layers the tubers in cedar shavings, and does not click the lid shut — and she explicitly does not recommend cardboard boxes, newspapers, or paper bags, because they wick up too much moisture. After a few weeks she checks, and if the shavings are moist to the touch, she changes them; the first month is usually the too-moist month. The disagreement dissolves once you name the variable: cardboard wicks moisture away in a damp spot and dries tubers out in a dry spot, while a vented plastic bin holds moisture in. Pick the container for your storage spot, not for the article you read — and whichever you pick, the container must breathe. A sealed box is the one choice every source agrees is wrong. The container is a moisture decision, and getting it wrong is the quietest failure in how to store dahlias for winter.

## The numbers: one clump, three storage choices

The figures above give a concrete answer if you run them through one clump, and the comparison is the practical payoff of learning how to store dahlias for winter by the numbers rather than by habit. Take one healthy clump dug after the first killing frost in zone 5 — eight tubers, each with a neck, a crown, and an eye — and store it three ways through the same winter:

- **Cut to 2 to 4 inches, cured a week in the ground, washed, dried until dry to the touch, packed in vermiculite in a ventilated box at 40 to 50 degrees, and checked monthly:** the tubers stay dormant and firm, the medium holds just enough moisture to keep them from shrivelling, and in spring each tuber with an eye is a plant. That is the Iowa State, Penn State, and Wisconsin rules all followed at once — and the clump that went in as eight tubers comes out as eight plants, or more if you divide in spring. It is the control case: how to store dahlias for winter done by the book.
- **The same clump packed the same way but stored in a heated basement at 65 to 70 degrees:** Ask Extension's warning is the outcome — the warmth encourages microorganisms and fungus and prompts the tubers to develop sprouts too soon. The tubers sprout in January, burn their stored energy on pale shoots in the dark, and shrivel before spring. WSU says the same thing in fewer words: tubers will shrivel if too warm.
- **The same clump left in the ground through a zone 5 winter:** the freeze reaches 6 inches deep and destroys the thin-skinned tubers, per Washington State University Extension — and Penn State adds that heavy freezing and winter rains promote rotting, especially in zones 6 and lower. The clump does not come back in spring, and there is nothing to divide, because the tubers are mush — the cost of skipping how to store dahlias for winter entirely.

### The moisture math

And the moisture math, since the sources give three numbers for the same job: the storage medium must hold moisture around the tubers but still allow air flow, per UNH Extension — moistened vermiculite, wood shavings, or sand, with a little moisture going a long way. Too wet, and the tubers rot; too dry, and they shrivel. The check schedule is the control: Ask Extension says to check every three weeks, Wisconsin and WSU say monthly, and Penn State checks after a few weeks and then finds them usually good for the rest of the season. Pick a schedule you will actually keep — monthly at minimum — because the check is the control that makes how to store dahlias for winter work: a rotting tuber left in the box spreads decay to healthy tubers, per UNH Extension, and a shrivelling tuber caught early can be saved with a light misting, per Wisconsin and WSU.

## Monthly checks: rot or shrivel

Storage is not a job you finish in November; it is a job you check — and the check is part of how to store dahlias for winter, not an afterthought. Every source gives a check schedule, and they cluster around the same interval: the University of Wisconsin Extension says to check the bulbs periodically during the winter, and monthly for dehydration; WSU says to check them once a month throughout the winter; Ask Extension says to check every three weeks; UNH Extension says to check occasionally over the winter. Penn State checks after a few weeks, changes the shavings if they are moist to the touch, and then usually finds them good for the rest of the season.

What you are looking for is one of two failures, and they look opposite. Rot is soft, dark, and spreading: remove any tubers that have started to rot before the decay spreads to healthy tubers, per UNH Extension, and cut out any damaged tissue of larger tubers, per Wisconsin. Shrivel is the other failure: tubers that have dehydrated show a shrunken or wrinkled appearance, per Wisconsin — moisten the layering medium a little, with a spray bottle, and re-pack them. WSU gives the same fix: moisten lightly with a spray bottle if they appear to be dehydrating. If the medium is very moist, Wisconsin says to remove the tubers from the packing material and air dry them for a few days before repacking in dry material. If the medium seems too dry, Penn State says a spritz of water may be necessary — though she adds she has never needed to do it. Either failure caught in December costs one tuber; either failure found in March costs the box. That is why the monthly check is the cheapest insurance in how to store dahlias for winter.

## Spring: waking the tubers up

How to store dahlias for winter ends in spring, and the wake-up has its own numbers. Washington State University Extension starts the tubers in potting soil or sand, 2 to 3 inches deep, about six weeks before transplanting outside, in a warm area over 60 degrees Fahrenheit, in soil that is slightly damp but not wet — then transplants outside after all danger of frost has passed. If the start grows over 12 inches tall before transplanting, pinch it back. In the garden, dig a hole that places the tuber 6 inches deep, add a handful of bone meal in each hole, followed by the tuber, and place the stakes at planting time, about 2 inches from the tuber, so you do not pierce the tubers by staking later. Keep the plants watered until the roots are established, approximately one week.

Two spring details from WSU are easy to skip and worth keeping — they are the last steps of how to store dahlias for winter, not the first steps of growing dahlias. First, once you have carefully divided the tubers, let the cuts seal overnight before planting in containers. Second, do not wait too long to replant, or the tubers will become dehydrated — the same drying that storage was managing all winter continues in spring, and a tuber left on a shelf in April is a tuber losing moisture it cannot replace. If you divided in fall, the spring job is just planting; if you stored whole clumps, spring is when you divide, when the eyes are easiest to see and many have started to sprout, per Ask Extension. Either way, the plant that goes in the ground in spring is the tuber you kept at 40 to 50 degrees all winter — the storage was the growing season, just underground, and that is the payoff of how to store dahlias for winter.

## Mistakes that lose tubers

The short list of what goes wrong when people learn how to store dahlias for winter — all of it avoidable:

### Timing and digging mistakes

- **Digging before the tubers are mature.** Dahlias need at least 120 days in the ground, per Penn State — a late-planted dahlia dug on schedule may still be immature.
- **Cutting the stems early and leaving hollow tubes.** Hollow stems collect water and promote crown rot, per UNH Extension — cut right before digging.
- **Digging too close to the stalk.** Dig several inches back, or a foot away on all four sides, per Wisconsin and WSU — the necks break easily and a broken neck is an open door for rot.
- **Rubbing the skin off the tubers.** Pathogens enter through injured areas, per Wisconsin — handle them like eggs, not potatoes.
- **Dividing without an eye.** Each tuber needs a neck, a crown, and an eye, per Penn State — a tuber without an eye is compost, not a plant.
- **Leaving them in the ground in a cold zone.** A freeze reaching 6 inches deep destroys the thin-skinned tubers, per WSU — zones 3 to 6 must dig.

### Storage mistakes

- **Packing wet tubers.** Dry until dry to the touch first — from 24 hours (Iowa State) to four or five days (Penn State's experience) depending on your drying spot.
- **Storing them warm.** A heated basement at 65 to 70 degrees grows fungus and early sprouts, per Ask Extension — 40 to 50 degrees is the number.
- **Sealing the container air-tight.** Air-tight containers cause moisture build-up and promote decay, per Wisconsin — the box must breathe.
- **Storing a damaged tuber with healthy ones.** Rot spreads in the box — remove rotting tubers the moment you find them, per UNH Extension and Ask Extension.
- **Using potting soil as the storage medium.** WSU says not to use it — vermiculite, peat moss, sand, or wood shavings are the media the sources name.
- **Never checking the box.** Monthly checks, at minimum, per Wisconsin and WSU — a rotting tuber found in December costs one tuber; found in March, it costs the box.

Get the timing right — after the killing frost, before the hard freeze — the tubers dry to the touch, packed in vermiculite at 40 to 50 degrees in a container that breathes, and checked once a month, and that is how to store dahlias for winter the extension-backed way: a clump dug up in November is a garden full of flowers next summer.

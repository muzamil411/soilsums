A rubber plant is the houseplant that looks like it needs a gardener and behaves like it needs almost nothing, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for rubber plant. It stands in a bright corner with glossy leaves a foot long, it grows into a tree six to ten feet tall indoors, and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: a bright window, a mix that drains, and a watering only when the soil has dried slightly. Then the same plant sits for a year dropping its lower leaves in the hands of an owner who waters it every Sunday, because how to care for rubber plant is not the same as keeping a plant watered. A rubber plant is a tropical tree from South and Southeast Asia that starts life growing on another plant and grows into a tree a hundred feet tall in the wild, and it wants bright light, a pot it can drain from, and to be left in one place. Give it those, and it grows into a tree with glossy, leathery leaves eight to twelve inches long, dark green or deep maroon or marked with cream, pink and gold. Give it a dark corner, a pot without holes, and a weekly watering, and it drops its leaves one by one until only a bare stem is left.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A rubber plant that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go limp and droop, the edges curl slightly inward, and the pot feels light when you lift it. A rubber plant that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and then they drop, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its stems grow tall and bare between leaves, and the new leaves come out small and pale. A plant moved suddenly into strong afternoon sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much drops its leaves in protest. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, yellowing leaves, leaf drop, stretch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for rubber plant, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are Clemson Cooperative Extension and NC State Extension, with the ASPCA covering the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the leaf-drop section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most. If you bought it for the glossy leaves and got small, pale ones instead, read the light section first — because the leaf size answers to light, not to fertilizer.

## How to care for rubber plant: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright light, and keep it out of harsh afternoon sun.** The brightest spot you can spare, a few feet from an east-facing window, with gentle morning sun and no harsh afternoon sun on the leaves, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for rubber plant, because the plant is a tropical tree that grows in bright, filtered light — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and loses its variegation, while a plant moved into harsh afternoon sun in one day scorches.

**Let the soil dry slightly between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the top inch or two feels dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for rubber plant.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room by day and night, between 60 and 80°F if you can manage it, and inside long before the first cold snap. Cold is the third rule of how to care for rubber plant — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single cold night can make it drop its leaves in a day.

### The last two rules in how to care for rubber plant

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed every two weeks from spring through summer, less often in lower light, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding does not make a rubber plant grow faster; it makes it drop its leaves. Feeding is the fourth rule of how to care for rubber plant.

**Keep it in a well-drained mix, in a pot with holes, and leave it in one place.** A well-drained houseplant mix in a pot with drainage holes, repotted only when the roots fill the pot — and the same spot for years, because a rubber plant that is moved often drops its leaves in protest. Free drainage and a settled spot are the fifth rule of how to care for rubber plant, and they protect the roots and the leaves for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for rubber plant does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays full and glossy: stiff, leathery leaves eight to twelve inches long, dark green with a pale, raised midrib, per NC State Extension — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it grows into a tree: a single upright stem with a crown of big leaves, six to ten feet tall indoors, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — and that tree habit is the reason the plant is worth keeping for years. Third, it keeps its leaves: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and left in one place holds its lower leaves for years, per NC State Extension, instead of dropping them one by one until only a bare stem is left. The five rules in how to care for rubber plant are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the tropical tree, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a tree in a pot

The first thing to get straight in how to care for rubber plant is that the name covers a tree, not a small houseplant, because the care splits along that line. NC State Extension says the rubber plant is a broadleaf evergreen, flowering tree in the mulberry family, typically grown as a popular ornamental house plant in temperate climates — and that in the wild, left to its own devices, the tree can grow to 100 feet high. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants are easy to grow and care for and do well in a variety of conditions, and that they are excellent plants for beginners. That background explains almost everything about how to care for rubber plant. It is a plant from a warm place where the rain comes often and the soil drains instantly, and it wants bright light, warm air, and a soil that dries out between waterings. It is not a desert succulent and it is not a shade plant — it is a tree that starts life growing on another plant and grows into a spreading crown with aerial roots, which is why it wants light from above and a spot it can stay in for years.

### A tree that starts on another tree

NC State Extension says rubber plants in the wild are erect with a spreading crown, aerial roots, and initially epiphytic — a plant that begins as a growth on the surface of another plant. The species name refers to the milky sap that once was used to produce rubber, per NC State Extension, and the genus name comes from the Latin name for the edible fig. That history is not trivia: it is why the plant bleeds white sap from every cut, why the sap is sticky and hard to wash off, and why the plant is so sensitive to being moved. A tree that starts on another tree expects to stay where it lands, and a rubber plant that is moved from room to room every few weeks answers by dropping its leaves. Pick the spot with care, and then leave the plant there — that is the first practical lesson in how to care for rubber plant, and it costs nothing.

### The size that matters before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for rubber plant, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the height, because the plant grows fast and the tree decides how much room it needs. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants can easily grow 6 to 10 feet tall or more indoors if there is enough space, and that they can be pruned to reduce their size. NC State Extension says that when grown indoors, the plant is usually kept to 2 to 10 feet with training or support as needed, and that it can become a heavy plant that is best for large spaces. Buy the height for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a bright corner, a standard plant for a floor spot by the window, and a tall plant only if you have a ceiling it can grow toward — because a plant that reaches 10 feet needs room, and a plant without headroom bends toward the window and goes crooked.

### The leaves that matter: big, glossy, and leathery

The second is the leaves, because the leaves are the whole point of the plant in how to care for rubber plant. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants are grown for their glossy, leathery, large leaves, and that the leaves are oblong to oval, 8 to 12 inches long and in various colors of dark green, deep maroon or marked with yellow, cream, pink or white. NC State Extension says the plant is noted for its foot-long, shiny, attractive leaves, dark green with pink or purple stipules, and that variegated forms are available. The leaves should be thick, stiff and glossy, bright green with a pale midrib that is raised underneath, per NC State Extension — and on a variegated plant the markings should be clear and bright, not faded to green. A plant with small, pale, thin leaves in the shop has been kept in too little light, and a plant with brown, curled leaf edges has been over-fed or chilled. Buy the glossy, firm, right-sized plant, and the rest of how to care for rubber plant is straightforward.

### The varieties in the shop

The shop will show you more than the plain green rubber plant, and the varieties differ mostly in color, not in care, in how to care for rubber plant. Clemson Cooperative Extension lists the common forms. 'Decora' is a widely available cultivar with dark leaves with creamy white midribs. 'Doescheri' has cream and gray variegated leaves with pink ribs. 'Foliis Aureo-marginata' has gold margined leaves. 'Robusta' has very large leaves up to 18 inches long, and it is good in low light. 'Rubra' has leaves that are reddish when young and mature deep green with red edges. 'Tricolor' has green leaves with cream and pink patches. NC State Extension lists the same spread, including 'Burgundy', 'Tineke', 'Variegata' and 'Red Ruby' among the variegated forms. The rule for all of them is the same: the more color in the leaf, the more light the plant needs to hold it, because variegated forms have less green leaf area doing the work — and 'Robusta', the one Clemson Cooperative Extension calls good in low light, is the exception that proves it, because its leaves are plain dark green and very large.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, glossy, and firm, bright green with clear markings on a variegated plant, with no yellow patches, no brown edges, and no bare stem where leaves have already dropped. Yellow leaves on a plant in the shop mean the plant has already been over-watered, and those leaves will not turn green again. A plant that is already dropping leaves in the shop has been moved, chilled, or over-watered, and a plant with clean green leaves is a better buy. The soil should be damp, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes and enough weight that the plant cannot tip over. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a light plastic pot with a heavy tree on top will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. Buy the glossy, firm, right-sized plant in damp soil in a heavy pot, and the rest of how to care for rubber plant is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the decision that shapes the plant more than any other in how to care for rubber plant, because the plant answers to it in its leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants prefer bright light but are adaptable to low light, and that they grow best with the morning light from an east window. NC State Extension says to grow the plant indoors under bright indirect light or partial shade, with protection from the afternoon sun, and that the plant does best in partial shade or low light in dry soil and medium relative humidity. The spread is narrow and the direction is the same: bright light is best, low light is survivable, harsh afternoon sun burns. The planning figure is the brightest spot you can spare within a few feet of an east-facing window, with gentle morning sun and no harsh afternoon sun on the leaves. A rubber plant in a north-facing window will survive and grow slowly; a 'Tineke' or a 'Tricolor' in the same window will lose its color, because the variegated kinds need more light than the plain green one — and 'Robusta', which Clemson Cooperative Extension calls good in low light, is the one variety that holds its leaves in a dim room.

### The yellowing warning

The first warning that the light is wrong is yellow, and it comes in two forms in how to care for rubber plant. A plant moved suddenly into strong afternoon sun scorches across the leaves that caught the sun, and the patches do not heal — move it back a few feet from the window and leave the marked leaves alone, because they still feed the plant. A plant in too little light yellows from the bottom up, the lower leaves going yellow and dropping while the growing tip stretches toward the window — and that is the plant telling you it cannot make enough food where it stands. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf yellowing may occur if the soil stays too wet, and that too little light, dry air or cold drafts may cause leaf loss. The two causes look alike, which is why you check the soil before you move the plant: if the soil is wet and the leaves are yellow, it is water; if the soil is dry and the leaves are yellow, it is light.

### The delayed warning

The second warning is slower, and it is the one owners miss in how to care for rubber plant. A plant in too little light does not die — it stretches. The stems grow tall and bare between the leaves, the new leaves come out small, and the variegated kinds go plain green. NC State Extension says the plant does best in partial shade or low light, and that is the trap: the plant survives, so the owner assumes the spot is fine, and a year later the plant is a bare stem with a tuft of small leaves at the top. The fix is the same as the fix for yellow leaves: move it to a brighter spot, with no harsh afternoon sun, and prune the bare stem back in spring, because the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length. The new growth will show whether the light is right — full, glossy leaves, close together on the stem — and the old bare stem is the record of the year in the dark.

### Grow lights when there is no bright window

A grow light is a fair answer when there is no bright window in how to care for rubber plant, and the plant is honest about whether it is working. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the plant is adaptable to low light, and that is the most shade-tolerant reading the sources give — but adaptable is not the same as thriving, and a plant under a weak lamp grows slowly with small leaves. A plain white LED or fluorescent tube a foot or two above the plant, on for twelve to fourteen hours a day as a planning figure, is enough for a plain green rubber plant — and the test is the same as the test for a window: new leaves close together on the stem, full-sized, and glossy. If the new leaves are still small and far apart, the light is still too weak, and the plant needs to be closer to the lamp or under a brighter one. A variegated rubber plant wants more light than a plain green one, and a grow light that suits a 'Robusta' may not suit a 'Tricolor' — give the colorful kinds the brightest spot in the room, and the plain kinds the lamp.

### Water

Water is the decision that kills the most rubber plants in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources are blunt about it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to water rubber plants thoroughly, but to let the soil dry slightly to the touch between watering times, and to be sure the pot has proper drainage holes — and if the pot is sitting in a saucer, to empty out the excess water after watering the plant. NC State Extension says to water regularly, but to avoid overwatering, and to reduce watering altogether when the plant is dormant, from fall to late winter. The spread is honest: "let the soil dry slightly" and "water regularly, but avoid overwatering" describe the same soil from two directions, and the finger test settles it. Push a finger an inch or two into the mix. If it feels damp, wait. If it feels dry, water thoroughly until it runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for rubber plant

The rule is dry, then soak, and the order matters in how to care for rubber plant. A little water every day keeps the top of the soil wet and the bottom dry, and the roots in the middle rot while the top looks fine. A thorough soak, only when the top inch or two is dry, wets the whole root ball and then lets it drain, and the roots get water and air in turn. That is why the sources all say the same thing in different words: water thoroughly, let it drain, and never let the pot stand in water. A pot without a drainage hole breaks the rule at the start, because the water has nowhere to go — and a saucer left full after watering is the same mistake in slow motion. If the plant is in a pot without holes, repot it into one with holes before you change anything else, because no watering schedule fixes a pot that cannot drain.

### How often to water

The honest answer to how often is that the calendar does not know, and in how to care for rubber plant the soil does. In a warm, bright room in summer, a rubber plant in a 10-inch pot will want water roughly every one to two weeks; in a cool room in winter, the same plant may go three weeks or more between waterings — and those are planning figures, not schedules, because the pot size, the light, the mix, and the season all change the answer. NC State Extension says to reduce watering altogether when the plant is dormant, from fall to late winter, and that is the only seasonal rule the sources give. The finger test is the schedule: check the top inch or two every few days, water only when it is dry, and write down what you find for the first month, because after a month you will know your plant's rhythm in your room. A plant that is watered on the finger test grows steadily; a plant that is watered every Sunday because it is Sunday rots by spring.

### The drooping leaves

Drooping leaves are the plant's own watering signal in how to care for rubber plant, and they are worth learning to read. A rubber plant that is thirsty goes limp: the leaves droop, the edges curl slightly inward, and the pot feels light when you lift it. Water it thoroughly and the leaves lift again within hours. A rubber plant that is over-watered looks different: the leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and then they drop, and the pot feels heavy, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. The two kinds of droop need opposite fixes, and the soil test tells them apart: dry soil and drooping leaves means water; wet soil and yellow leaves means stop watering and let it dry. If the soil is wet and the plant is dropping leaves, take the plant out of the pot and look at the roots — white and firm is healthy, brown and mushy is rot, and the fix is to cut the rot away and repot in fresh, dry mix.

### Temperature

Temperature is the quiet rule in how to care for rubber plant, because the plant likes the same air you do. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the ideal temperatures are 60 to 65°F at night and 75 to 80°F during the day, and to avoid temperatures lower than 55°F, sudden drops in temperatures, and cold drafts. NC State Extension says the plant does best with a temperature of greater than 55 degrees F, and that it does not do well with drafts or cold temperatures. The spread is narrow: 60 to 80°F is the comfort zone, nights no colder than 60°F, and below 55°F is damage territory. The planning figure is 60 to 80°F, nights no colder than 60°F, and the plant inside long before the first cold snap — because a rubber plant left on a cold windowsill in November will drop its leaves in a day, and the leaves do not grow back.

### What to avoid: cold and drafts

Cold is not a slow decline in how to care for rubber plant — it is a sudden injury. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to avoid temperatures lower than 55°F, sudden drops in temperatures, and cold drafts, and NC State Extension says the plant prefers to remain in one location and does not do well with drafts or cold temperatures. That warning covers the winter windowsill, the door that opens onto a cold porch, and the air-conditioning vent that blows cold air across the leaves in summer. A plant that sits in a cold draft goes limp and then drops its leaves, and the dropped leaves do not grow back. Keep the plant away from cold windows in winter, away from exterior doors, and away from heating and air-conditioning vents in both seasons, because hot, dry air from a vent dries the leaves just as surely as cold air chills them. If the plant has to spend a night near a cold window, move it a few feet into the room for the winter — a few feet is often the whole difference.

### Humidity

Humidity is the rule the plant is most forgiving about in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources say so plainly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants prefer humid conditions, but tolerate the dry air common in homes. NC State Extension says the plant does best in dry soil and medium relative humidity. The planning figure is average household air, which the plant tolerates, and medium humidity if you can manage it, which the plant prefers — a pebble tray under the pot, a humidifier in a dry room in winter, or a bathroom window for a small plant. Misting helps for an hour and then the air is dry again, so misting is a supplement, not a solution, and a plant with brown, crispy leaf edges in a dry winter room wants a humidifier, not a spray bottle. What the plant does not forgive is dry air combined with cold drafts: Clemson Cooperative Extension lists dry air beside too little light and cold drafts as a cause of leaf loss, so fix the drafts first and the humidity second.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once and live with for a year in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources agree on what it should do. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the soil mix should be a well-drained houseplant mix. NC State Extension says to plant in a soil-based potting mix indoors. The mix should hold moisture without staying wet: a bagged indoor [potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) cut with roughly a third [perlite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) or orchid bark, or a general-purpose potting soil on its own in a pot with good drainage, both work — and the test is what happens after you water. If the mix is still soggy three days after watering, it is too dense, and the plant will rot; if it dries out in two days in a bright room, it is too light, and the plant will want water constantly. A mix that is damp on the second day and dry on the seventh is right, and that is the mix to keep.

### An airy mix that drains

The air in the mix matters as much as the water in how to care for rubber plant, because the roots breathe. A dense, peaty mix that stays wet for a week starves the roots of air, and the roots rot — and that is the root rot every source names. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot usually results from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or overly frequent watering. NC State Extension says overwatering can cause loss of leaves. The fix is in the mix, not in the watering can: perlite, orchid bark, or coarse pumice mixed into the potting soil opens air pockets that stay open after watering, and a pot with drainage holes lets the excess water out. Terracotta is a good choice for a rubber plant that is watered too often, because the clay breathes and the mix dries faster; plastic is a good choice for a plant in a dry, bright room, because it holds moisture longer. Either way, the drainage hole is non-negotiable — a pot without one is a pot that will rot the plant.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the second half of the soil decision in how to care for rubber plant, and the rule is simple: one size up, never three. A pot much bigger than the root ball holds a lot of wet mix that the small root system cannot drink, and that wet mix rots the roots — which is why a plant repotted into a pot three sizes too big often dies faster than a plant left pot-bound. NC State Extension says the plant can become a heavy plant that is best for large spaces, and that matters for the pot: a six-foot tree in a light plastic pot will tip over, so choose a heavy pot — terracotta or ceramic — once the plant is taller than two feet. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to repot in late winter or early spring if needed. The sign to act is unmistakable: roots circling the drainage holes, water running straight through the pot without wetting the mix, or a plant that needs water every few days because the pot is all roots and no soil. Choose a pot one size larger than the last, with drainage holes, and fresh mix — and if the plant is tall, choose a heavy pot, because a six-foot tree with big leaves in a light plastic pot will tip over.

### Repotting: when the roots fill the pot

Repotting is rare work for a rubber plant in how to care for rubber plant, and the plant tells you when it is time. A young plant in a small pot may need a new pot every year or two; a mature tree in a big pot may go three years in the same pot. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to repot in late winter or early spring if needed, and that is the window to use: the plant is about to grow, and it recovers fastest then. The method is the same either way: water the plant the day before, so the mix holds together; tip the plant out and shake off the loose mix; trim any brown, mushy roots with clean scissors; set the plant at the same depth in the new pot, with fresh mix around the roots; and water thoroughly, then leave it alone for a week. Do not feed a newly repotted plant for a month — the fresh mix already has enough food in it, and a stressed plant does not want fertilizer. A plant that drops a leaf or two after repotting is normal; a plant that drops many leaves was repotted too roughly, or into a pot too big, and the fix is to leave it alone in a bright, warm spot and let it settle.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the rule owners overdo in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources are restrained about it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to fertilize regularly with a water-soluble houseplant fertilizer during active growth in the spring and summer, and that this should be done every two weeks — and that plants in lower light should be fertilized less often. The spread is narrow — every two weeks at the bright end, less often in lower light — and the reason is that the plant is not a heavy feeder. A rubber plant in fresh mix, watered well and kept in bright light, grows steadily on very little food, and the signs of too much food are worse than the signs of too little: leaf drop, brown leaf edges, and a white crust on the soil surface.

### The planning figure: every two weeks in summer, less in low light

The planning figure this guide uses in how to care for rubber plant is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half strength, every two weeks from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. That figure follows Clemson Cooperative Extension's every two weeks, cut to half strength, because a full-strength feed on a dry plant burns the roots, so water the plant first, then feed, and never feed a plant that is wilted, newly repotted, or sitting in the dark in winter. A plant in lower light gets fed half as often — once a month at most — because it is growing half as fast, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that brown the leaf edges. If the leaves drop after a feed, stop feeding and flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear.

### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding does not make a rubber plant grow faster in how to care for rubber plant — it makes it drop its leaves. The salts in fertilizer build up in the mix, the roots cannot take up water against the salt, and the leaves drop while the plant sits in damp soil. The fix is to stop feeding, flush the mix with plain water, and wait: a plant that has been over-fed for a month needs a month of plain water before it starts growing again. Fresh mix has food in it already, and a plant repotted in late winter needs no feed until summer — which is why the planning figure starts feeding in spring, not at repotting. A rubber plant that is fed lightly and watered well will outgrow a rubber plant that is fed heavily and watered badly, every time.

## Pruning a tree that never stops

Pruning is the maintenance that keeps a rubber plant full in how to care for rubber plant, because a tree that is never pruned goes bare at the base. Clemson Cooperative Extension says rubber plants may grow tall and lanky indoors, and that you can support them with a stake or prune them in spring to rejuvenate. NC State Extension says to prune the main branches to encourage a bushier habit, or to leave them unpruned to encourage a tall, narrow, more tree-like form. A rubber plant that grows for two years without pruning becomes a tall, bare stem with a tuft of leaves at the top — and the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length, because the plant only grows from the tip and from the nodes. The fix is to cut the stem back, and the cuttings are not waste: they are new plants, and the pruned stem branches from the node below the cut.

### Prune in spring, back to a node

Prune in spring, when the plant is growing, and cut just above a node in how to care for rubber plant — the knuckle on the stem where a leaf grows. A clean cut with sharp scissors, just above the node, and the stem below the cut will branch, often with two new shoots where there was one. Cut a tall, bare stem back by a third, or by half if it is very bare, and the plant will fill out from the top over the summer. Wear gloves, because the cut drips the milky latex sap that Clemson Cooperative Extension says may irritate skin — and let the cut end dry before you handle it, because the sap keeps bleeding until it skins over. Do not prune in autumn or winter, when the plant is barely growing, because a cut made then heals slowly and the plant drops leaves instead of branching.

### Staking a tall, heavy tree

Staking is the other half of pruning in how to care for rubber plant, because a six-foot tree in a pot is top-heavy. Clemson Cooperative Extension says you can support a tall, lanky rubber plant with a stake. A bamboo cane or a wooden stake, pushed into the pot beside the stem and tied loosely with soft twine, holds the tree upright — and the tie should be loose, because a tight tie cuts into the stem as it grows. A plant that leans toward the window is asking for more light, not for a stronger stake, so turn the pot a quarter turn every month to keep the growth even, and move the plant closer to the window if it keeps leaning. A staked plant in a heavy pot, turned monthly, grows straight — and a plant that is never turned grows crooked, and no stake fixes a crooked tree.

### What the cuttings become

The cuttings from a spring pruning are the next plant in how to care for rubber plant, and they are not waste. A stem tip a few inches long, cut just below a node, roots in damp mix or water in a few weeks — and a plant that is pruned every spring gives you a cutting every spring, which is how one rubber plant becomes three. Keep one or two leaves on the cutting, and root it in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, because a cutting in a dark corner rots before it roots. A cutting taken in spring or summer roots fastest, when the plant is growing — and a cutting taken in winter sits in the mix for months and often rots, because the plant is dormant and the mix stays wet.

## Propagation: cuttings and air layering

Propagation is the reward for a healthy plant in how to care for rubber plant, and Clemson Cooperative Extension lists two methods: stem or tip cuttings, or air layering. Cuttings suit a young plant with plenty of stem tips; air layering suits a tall, leggy plant that has only one long stem and no tips to spare. Both work, and both are done in spring or summer, when the plant is growing — because a cutting taken in winter roots slowly, and an air layer started in winter sits for months before it roots.

### Stem and tip cuttings

Take a stem tip a few inches long, cut just below a node, and root it in damp mix or water before potting it up in how to care for rubber plant. Keep one or two leaves attached, and remove the lower leaves, because a leaf sitting in the mix rots and takes the cutting with it. Wear gloves — the cut end drips the milky latex sap that Clemson Cooperative Extension says may irritate skin, and the sap keeps bleeding until the cut skins over, so let the cutting sit for an hour before you put it in the mix. Set the cutting in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, keep the mix damp but not wet, and wait: roots form in a few weeks, and the first new leaf is the sign that the cutting has taken. A leaf on its own, without a piece of stem and a node, will not make a new plant.

### Air layering a tall, leggy plant

Air layering is the method for a tall, leggy plant in how to care for rubber plant, because it makes a new plant from the top of the old one without cutting the stem first. Clemson Cooperative Extension lists air layering beside stem cuttings as a way to propagate new plants. Wound a stem a foot or two below the top, pack damp moss around the wound, wrap it in plastic to hold the moisture in, and wait for roots to form inside the moss — then cut the stem below the rooted section and pot the new plant up, and the old stem branches from the node below the cut. Air layering takes longer than a cutting — weeks to a couple of months — but it gives you a bigger plant at the start, because the new plant already has a foot of stem and a crown of leaves when you cut it free. Do it in spring, when the plant is growing, and keep the moss damp, because moss that dries out stops the roots from forming.

## Problems: leaf drop, yellow leaves, and pests

Problems in how to care for rubber plant are almost always water, light, or cold, and the sources name the same three causes for nearly every symptom. Work through them in order — soil first, then light, then cold — and the plant usually answers within a week. A plant that is checked weekly never gets a bad problem; a plant that is never checked gets one that takes a month to clear.

### Leaf drop: the plant's loudest complaint

Leaf drop is the first problem in how to care for rubber plant, and it is the one owners panic about most. Clemson Cooperative Extension says too little light, dry air or cold drafts may cause leaf loss. NC State Extension says overwatering can cause loss of leaves, as can temperature drops or cold drafts, and that the plant prefers to remain in one location and does not do well with drafts or cold temperatures. If the plant drops a leaf or two after a move, that is the plant protesting the move — give it a few weeks in its new spot and it settles. If it drops many leaves at once, check the soil: wet soil means stop watering and let it dry; dry soil means water it; a dark corner means move it to a brighter spot; a cold draft means move it away from the door or window. A leaf that has dropped will not grow back, but the plant branches from the node below the cut, and a pruned stem fills out again over the summer.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the second problem in how to care for rubber plant, and they are the overwatering signal. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf yellowing may occur if the soil stays too wet. NC State Extension says overwatering can cause loss of leaves, and that it is normal for some bottom leaves to turn yellow and drop. If only the oldest leaves at the base are yellowing, that is the plant aging, and no action is needed — the plant sheds its oldest leaves as it grows taller, and a leaf that has already turned yellow will not turn green again. If many leaves yellow at once, check the soil first: if it is wet, stop watering and let it dry out, and check that the pot has drainage holes and the saucer is empty; if the soil is dry and the leaves are yellow, the light is too low or the plant is catching a cold draft, so move it to a brighter, warmer spot.

### Brown edges and cold brown patches

Brown edges are the third problem in how to care for rubber plant, and they are the dry-air and over-feeding signal. A plant in a dry winter room gets brown, crispy leaf edges, and a plant that has been fed too much gets brown leaf tips while the rest of the leaf stays green. The fix for dry air is a humidifier, not a spray bottle, because misting helps for an hour and then the air is dry again. The fix for over-feeding is to stop feeding and flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear. Cold brown patches are the winter injury: a plant left on a cold windowsill, or in the path of a cold draft, develops brown patches in the middle of the leaves, and the patches do not heal. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to avoid temperatures lower than 55°F, sudden drops in temperatures, and cold drafts. Move the plant a few feet into the room for the winter, away from cold windows and exterior doors, and leave the marked leaves alone — they still feed the plant.

### Mealybugs, scale, and spider mites

Mealybugs are the first pest in how to care for rubber plant, and they are easy to see once you know what to look for: white, cottony masses in the leaf axils and on the undersides of the leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says mealybugs may infest rubber plants. NC State Extension says mealybugs, scales, and spider mites can be problematic. The fix is to check the undersides of the leaves every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the leaf you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back. Wipe the visible mealybugs off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later, because the eggs hatch after the first treatment. Scale looks like small brown bumps on the leaves and stems, and spider mites are tiny, with the first sign fine webbing on the undersides of the leaves. The fix is the same: wipe the scale off with a cotton bud and rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later. Spider mites like dry air, so a plant in a dry winter room is more likely to get them — and a humidifier helps the plant and hurts the mites at the same time.

### Keep the leaves clean

Washing the leaves is the maintenance that keeps a rubber plant healthy in how to care for rubber plant, and the sources both name it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to wash the leaves with water when they get dusty, to keep them attractive and help your plant stay healthy. NC State Extension says to use a damp soft cloth or sponge to clean dust off of leaves. Dust blocks the light the leaves need, and a dusty plant grows slower than a clean one — so wipe the big leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks, and wash the plant with plain water when it gets dusty. Do not use leaf-shine sprays, because they clog the pores on the leaves, and do not wipe the leaves when they are wet from watering, because a wet leaf tears more easily than a dry one. A clean plant is a plant that can use its light, and a plant that can use its light grows.

## Is a rubber plant poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for rubber plant, because the plant is so common that its big leaves hang within reach of pets in millions of homes. NC State Extension flags the rubber plant as a problem for cats, dogs and horses. The ASPCA has no separate entry for *Ficus elastica* itself: its entry named [Indian Rubber Plant](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/indian-rubber-plant) is the weeping fig, *Ficus benjamina*, which it lists as toxic to dogs and cats, with the toxic principles given as the proteolytic enzyme ficin and psoralen, and the clinical signs given as gastrointestinal and dermal irritation. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the sticky white sap may irritate skin or the stomach if eaten, and to keep these plants away from any pets or young children that tend to eat plants. The honest reading is that the rubber plant's own entry in the extension sources flags it for pets, the ASPCA's fig listing covers the genus with the same signs, and the sap is the part that does the harm.

### The riskiest plant on the floor

The riskiest rubber plant in the house is the tall one on the floor in how to care for rubber plant, because its lower leaves hang where a dog can reach them. A rubber plant in a floor pot has leaves at nose height for a dog and at paw height for a cat, and a dog that finds a fallen leaf on the floor will eat it — and a cat that bats at a low leaf will chew it. The plant is not deadly — no source lists it as lethal, and Clemson Cooperative Extension calls the effect irritation rather than poisoning — but a pet that chews it will drool and vomit, and a pet that swallows a large piece can have a sore mouth and an upset stomach. The fix is placement: keep the plant on a stand where the lowest leaves are above the dog's reach, or keep it in a room the pets do not enter. A plant on a tall stand with its leaves above the cat's reach is a safe plant; a plant on the floor with leaves trailing onto the carpet is not.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Keep it out of reach anyway in how to care for rubber plant, because the risk is not worth the test. A pet that has never chewed a plant before will chew one eventually, and a child that has never eaten a leaf before will eat one eventually — and the plant is flagged for both, per NC State Extension for pets and per Clemson Cooperative Extension for young children. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any rubber plant and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. If a child eats a piece, call your pediatrician or your local poison control center. The plant is worth keeping, and the risk is worth managing — but only if the plant is placed where it cannot be eaten.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap can irritate skin in how to care for rubber plant, and that is the second safety fact. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the sticky white sap may irritate skin or the stomach if eaten. NC State Extension says that if the leaves are cut, the surface will drip sap. Wear gloves when you prune or repot a rubber plant, wash your hands afterwards, and keep the sap out of your eyes. A person who handles the plant every day without gloves may develop a rash over time, and a person who gets sap in a cut will feel it sting. The sap is not dangerous to healthy skin in small amounts, but it is an irritant, and it is sticky and hard to wash off — so the simplest habit, gloves on and hands washed, is enough. The sap is the latex the species name refers to — NC State Extension says the species name refers to the milky sap that once was used to produce rubber — so treat it as latex: gloves on, hands washed, and out of eyes, and anyone with a latex allergy should take extra care around it.

## The short version

The short version of how to care for rubber plant is five rules, and they fit on a card. Bright light, a few feet from an east-facing window, with morning sun and no harsh afternoon sun. Water when the top inch or two of soil feels dry, soak thoroughly, and empty the saucer. Keep it warm, between 60 and 80°F, nights no colder than 60°F, and inside before the first cold snap. Feed lightly at half strength every two weeks from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Keep it in a well-drained mix in a pot with holes, and leave it in one place. Do those five, and the plant grows into a tree six to ten feet tall, with glossy leaves eight to twelve inches long that get bigger every year — and the cuttings from one pruning become the next plant.

### And a second plant for the same bright room

A second plant for the same bright room in how to care for rubber plant is the [philodendron](/blog/how-to-care-for-philodendron/), and the two belong together because they are the pair the shop confuses least and the room suits best. A philodendron wants bright indirect light and a warmer room than a rubber plant, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and it climbs or trails where a rubber plant stands upright. A [pothos](/blog/how-to-care-for-pothos/) is the third plant for the same room, and it wants brighter light still, and it trails where the rubber plant stands. A [monstera](/blog/how-to-care-for-monstera/) is the fourth, and it wants the brightest spot of the four, and a pole to climb, and it will make split leaves with holes if the light is right. All four are tropical, all four want bright light and a pot that drains, and all four are flagged for pets — and all four reward the same five rules, with the light turned up a notch for each one in turn. Start with the rubber plant, add the philodendron, and then the pothos, and the bright room fills itself.

A spider plant is the houseplant that forgives almost everything except two things: soggy soil and hard tap water. It survives missed waterings, dim corners, and months of neglect, and it pays you back by making babies — small plantlets on long arching stems that you can pot up for free. That forgiveness is the first lesson in how to care for spider plant, and it is why the plant is one of the most common houseplants in the United States — and why so many owners are surprised when the leaf tips turn brown, the plant stops making babies, or the whole plant looks tired despite being watered every week. The truth is that how to care for spider plant is simple, but it is not the same as caring for the succulents and cacti on the same windowsill. A spider plant is a plant with thick, fleshy roots that store water, and it wants bright light without direct sun, a soil that dries a little between waterings, and water without fluoride or salts. Give it those, and it makes babies for years.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A spider plant that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go pale and dull, and the whole plant looks limp, and then it perks up soon after watering. A plant that is over-watered looks the same — but its soil is wet, and no amount of extra water helps it. A plant in too little light stays green and alive but never makes babies. A plant in direct sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much burns at the leaf tips and stops making babies. Learning to read those five signals — pale leaves, no babies, scorch, brown tips, a pot that cracks — is the whole of how to care for spider plant, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are Clemson Cooperative Extension, NC State Extension, UF/IFAS Extension, the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, and South Dakota State University Extension, with the ASPCA corroborating the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant for its babies, the propagation section covers that. If you bought it because someone told you it cleans the air, read the honest note in the babies section first.

## How to care for spider plant: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright indirect light, and never direct sun.** A few feet back from an east-facing window, or near a north-facing window with good ambient light, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for spider plant, because the plant tolerates low light but only makes babies in bright light — and a plant that never makes babies is a plant in too little light.

**Let the soil dry a little between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water when the top inch of soil feels dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates both extremes: soggy soil rots the thick roots, and soil that stays dry too long browns the leaf tips. Drying a little between waterings is the second rule of how to care for spider plant.

**Keep it warm, and away from cold drafts.** A room at 65 to 75°F during the day, 50 to 55°F at night, and never below 55°F if you can help it. Cold air is the third rule of how to care for spider plant — the plant is tropical, and a cold windowsill in winter slows it and can damage it.

### The last two rules in how to care for spider plant

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A balanced liquid fertilizer at label strength every three to four months from spring to summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Over-feeding burns the leaf tips and, worse, stops the plant making babies, and a plant that is fed too much looks worse than a plant that is never fed at all.

**Keep it slightly pot-bound, and repot only when the roots demand it.** The plant makes the most babies when it is slightly pot-bound, so repot only every one to two years, when the roots crowd the pot or threaten to crack it, into a pot just one or two inches wider. A pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots, and a plant in a pot that is too roomy makes leaves instead of babies. Repotting restraint is the fifth rule of how to care for spider plant, and it protects the roots for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for spider plant does three things a neglected one does not. First, it makes babies: bright indirect light, a slightly crowded pot, and the short days of autumn are what make a spider plant send out its long stems with plantlets, and a plant that gets all three can make a dozen new plants a year, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. A plant in a dim corner with a roomy pot stays green and never makes a single baby, which is the most common complaint about this plant — and the fix is light and a snug pot, not fertilizer. Second, it stays green to the tips: soft water, the right light, and light feeding stop the brown tips that are the second most common complaint. A plant whose leaves are green edge to edge is a plant whose owner is watering and feeding correctly, and the leaves show it. Third, it grows steadily without drama: a plant that is watered on the finger test, kept warm, and repotted rarely puts on a few inches a year and reaches 2 to 2 and a half feet wide in a few years, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — a hanging basket you can keep, not a plant you have to replace every spring. The five rules in how to care for spider plant are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the South African coast, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a plant that makes babies

The first thing to get straight in how to care for spider plant is what the plant actually is, because the name tells you the whole story. It is a spider plant — *Chlorophytum comosum* — a member of the asparagus family (Asparagaceae), per NC State Extension, and it gets its common name from the small plantlets produced on long trailing stems that vaguely resemble spiders, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension. The babies are not a bonus feature of this plant. They are the plant's whole way of reproducing in your home, and they are what you are actually growing when you grow a spider plant well. A plant that makes no babies is a plant that is missing something — usually light, or a snug pot, or the short days of autumn — and the rest of this guide is about finding which one.

### A plant from the South African coast

In the wild the plant grows in the coastal areas of South Africa, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, and South Dakota State University Extension says it originated in the jungles of South and West Africa. NC State Extension gives the range precisely: its native region is tropical West Africa to Cameroon and Ethiopia to South Africa. That background explains almost everything about how to care for spider plant. It is a tropical, clump-forming, perennial, herbaceous plant, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, with thick, fleshy roots and rhizomes that evolved to store water, allowing it to survive inconsistent watering. It is not a desert plant and it is not a rainforest plant — it is a plant from a warm coast where the rain comes and goes, and it wants bright light, warm air, and a soil that dries a little between rains. South Dakota State University Extension adds that it was introduced into Europe in the 18th century, and that in some regions the variegated varieties were called the ribbon plant — which is still one of its common names, alongside spider ivy and airplane plant, per the ASPCA.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a clump of narrow, strap-shaped leaves arising from a central point, arching outward and downward, with long wiry stems carrying small white flowers and miniature plantlets at their ends. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension describes the leaves as narrow and strap-shaped, arising from a central point, solid green or variegated with lengthwise stripes of white or yellow, and not flat but channeled or folded down the middle. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the long, grassy leaves are available in green or striped yellow or white, and that long, wiry stems appear on healthy plants with many small white flowers and miniature plantlets. NC State Extension says the green linear leaves are often striped white, and that long stolons produce plantlets and flowers. The small white flowers are star-shaped, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, and they appear at the ends of the stems. After flowering, more leaves are produced at the end of the stems, forming small plantlets — and if a flower is pollinated, a leathery, three-angled capsule-type fruit is produced that contains flat black seeds, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension. That seed capsule is a curiosity, not a propagation method: in practice, nobody grows spider plants from seed at home, because seedlings will not necessarily have the same leaf coloration as the parent, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension. The plantlets are the propagation, and they are the point.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for spider plant, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the cultivar, because size and leaf pattern are decided at the garden center, not by your care. Clemson Cooperative Extension gives the range: spider plants grow quickly to 2 to 2 and a half feet wide and 2 to 3 feet long when grown in a hanging basket. Its dwarf 'Mandaianum' has leaves only 4 to 6 inches long, dark green, with a bright yellow stripe. Its most common 'Vittatum' has pale green leaves 4 to 8 inches long with a white central stripe. Its 'Variegatum' has white-edged leaves 10 to 16 inches long and up to an inch wide. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension adds two more: 'Milky Way', with green margins and a broad white to cream center, and 'White Stripe', with a narrow white to cream line down the central leaf vein that is lost as the leaf ages. It also notes that 'Vittatum' tends to be slower growing than the green types, and that 'Variegatum' tends to produce fewer plantlets than other types — which matters if you are buying the plant for its babies. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says the plants grow 12 to 15 inches tall, with long, wiry stems up to 2 feet long. Buy the size for the spot: a dwarf 'Mandaianum' for a shelf, a standard 'Vittatum' for a hanging basket, and a green, unvariegated plant only if you want the fastest growth and the most babies — because the variegated types grow more slowly, and the plain green plant grows fastest of all.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The second is the condition of the leaves: they should be green to the tips, with crisp stripes and no browning, no yellow edges, and no droop. Brown tips in the shop mean the plant has already been stressed — over-fed, under-watered, or watered with hard tap water — and those tips will not recover. The third is the soil: it should be moist, not wet and not dry, and the pot should have drainage holes. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in bone-dry soil has already wilted once. Buy the green, right-sized plant in moist soil, and the rest of how to care for spider plant is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for spider plant, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. Clemson Cooperative Extension says spider plants grow best with bright indirect light, that they can tolerate some direct sunlight, but that midday light may scorch leaves. NC State Extension says the same in fewer words, and more strictly: spider plants prefer medium light levels, yet can tolerate deep shade, and are intolerant of direct sunlight. UF/IFAS Extension agrees: spider plants prefer bright to moderate indirect light year round. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says spider plants are very easy to grow indoors in medium to bright light throughout the year. South Dakota State University Extension says indirect to moderate light. The planning figure this guide uses is bright indirect light — a few feet back from an east-facing window, or near a north-facing window with good ambient light, or a south or west window with a sheer curtain — and no direct sun at any time of day. That range satisfies all five sources, and it is the light that makes the plant make babies.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters most is the delayed one. Clemson Cooperative Extension says spider plants are one of the most common houseplants because they are easily grown — but "easily grown" is not "grows well." A plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never makes a baby, and the owner concludes that spider plants simply do not make babies indoors, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. NC State Extension says the plant prefers medium light levels yet can tolerate deep shade — and that tolerance is the trap. A plant that survives in deep shade is not a plant that thrives in it — and thriving, not surviving, is the point of how to care for spider plant. The variegation fades in low light too: plants grown in low light may lose their variegation, and the striped leaves turn plain green, because a leaf that needs to photosynthesize harder produces more green to do it. If your plant has not made a baby in a year, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for spider plant, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Water

Watering is where most spider plants are lost, and the loss usually comes from one of two opposite mistakes: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain — or too little water, given too rarely, until the leaf tips brown. The watering rule from Clemson Cooperative Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for spider plant: spider plants should be allowed to dry out briefly between waterings, and to check their soil daily until you learn the plant's rhythm. Two halves, both required: let the soil dry a little, and then water thoroughly. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. And then remove the excess, so the pot never sits in water.

NC State Extension gives the same rule with a useful extra detail: keep the soil evenly moist through the growing season and reduce watering in winter, letting the surface dry slightly between waterings rather than keeping it constantly wet. UF/IFAS Extension says the same in its own words: the upper inch of soil should be allowed to dry out briefly between waterings. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says to allow the soil to dry slightly between thorough waterings. South Dakota State University Extension says to water once a week, allowing the soil to dry out between waterings. All five sources agree on the shape of the rule — dry a little, then soak — and they disagree only on the interval, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season.

### How often to water

The planning figure this guide uses is simple: check the top inch of soil with a finger, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — roughly once a week in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to every two weeks or longer in a cool room in winter. That range satisfies all the sources, and it keeps the soil evenly moist without ever staying soggy. It is a planning figure, not a schedule from any source: no extension source gives a fixed watering calendar for spider plants, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season. A small pot in a warm, bright room dries faster than a large pot in a cool, dim one, and a plant in summer drinks faster than the same plant in winter. The thick, fleshy roots store water, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, which is why a missed watering is forgiven — and why a plant that is watered every day, whether it needs it or not, rots. That finger test is the schedule in how to care for spider plant, and it is the only one that works in every house.

### The water itself matters

There is one more watering rule in how to care for spider plant, and it is the one that separates this plant from almost every other houseplant: the water itself matters, not just the amount. UF/IFAS Extension says the plant is particularly sensitive to fluoridated water, and that if your water is treated with fluoride, watering with rainwater or distilled water is best for the health of your plant. NC State Extension says the same: avoid watering with tap water, as the chlorine and fluorides can cause leaf tip burn. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says that salt accumulation and chemicals, particularly fluoride or chlorine, in tap water may cause brown leaf tips, and that using distilled water or rainwater will help prevent tip browning. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf tip burn is caused by water that is high in soluble salts. South Dakota State University Extension says browning of leaf tips is caused by chemical build-up from tap water, and gives the fix: set out your water for 24 hours before using it, so the chemicals can evaporate, and then remember to water the plant regularly. If your tap water is fluoridated — and most municipal water in the United States is — the cheapest fix in how to care for spider plant is a jug of rainwater or distilled water, or a jug of tap water left out overnight. That one change stops more brown tips than any other single habit.

### Temperature

Temperature is the easiest of the three to get right, because the range the plant wants is the range a house already has. The sources give a small spread, and it is worth showing honestly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says temperatures between 65°F and 75°F during the day and 50°F to 55°F at night are ideal. South Dakota State University Extension says the same: temperatures that are 65 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit during the day and 50 to 55 degrees at night, with average humidity. UF/IFAS Extension says temperatures below 55°F are too cool for spider plants, while those above 80°F are too warm, and that the plant prefers average temperatures. NC State Extension says to keep temperatures above 45 degrees F. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says the plant does well with average humidity and cool to average temperature, although it will tolerate warmer conditions.

The planning figure this guide uses is 65 to 75°F during the day, with nights at 50 to 55°F, and never below 55°F indoors if you can help it, with 45°F as the hard floor below which damage begins. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is ordinary indoor temperature in a house that is not overheated. That is why temperature is the easiest of the three decisions in how to care for spider plant: the house already provides it, and the job is simply not to put the plant somewhere colder than the room itself — or hotter, because a room that sits above 80°F for days is outside what this plant prefers, per UF/IFAS Extension, and that is the one heat weakness of a plant that is otherwise famously hard to kill.

### What to avoid: cold and heat

Two things matter more than the exact number. The first is cold: do not put the plant next to a drafty window, an exterior door, or an air-conditioning vent that blows directly on it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to move spider plants a few feet from windows to protect them from drafts during the winter. A chill that a hardy houseplant would ignore can slow this tropical plant, and the damage shows as browning and stunted growth. The second is heat: do not put the plant next to a radiator or a heating vent, and do not leave it in a room that sits above 80°F for days. Hot, dry air is the combination that browns the leaf tips fastest, and a plant that sits over a heating vent in January will show it by February. Warm and steady is the temperature rule in how to care for spider plant, and a room at 65 to 75°F away from vents and drafts is very nearly ideal.

### Humidity

Humidity is the winter problem, and the one most owners never think about. The spider plant comes from a humid coast, and the dry air of a centrally heated home in winter is genuinely hostile to it. NC State Extension says the plant grows best in moist soil and medium air humidity, and tells you to use a humidifier or place the plant container on a tray of pebbles with water to increase the humidity. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says the plant does well with average humidity, and names low humidity as one of the causes of brown leaf tips. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same: low humidity may cause brown leaf tips. South Dakota State University Extension says average humidity is enough, and names low humidity as a cause of browning. No extension source gives a humidity percentage for spider plants, so this guide gives none — the honest statement is that average household humidity is usually enough, and that brown tips in a dry heated room point to low humidity among the other causes. A humidifier running near the plant, or a pebble tray under the pot — a saucer filled with water and pebbles, with the pot sitting on the pebbles above the water, not in it — helps in a dry room. Misting the leaves helps briefly and is not a substitute. Get the humidity right and the leaf tips stay green through the heating season; get it wrong and the tips brown from the edges inward, which is the most common winter complaint in how to care for spider plant.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

The soil rule in how to care for spider plant is short: ordinary, well-drained, in a pot with holes. Clemson Cooperative Extension says a general-purpose potting soil is suitable for spider plants. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says to use a general-purpose potting soil or soilless medium. South Dakota State University Extension says general purpose potting soil. All three sources agree, and none of them asks for anything special — which is the point. This plant does not need a rich mix or a special recipe. It needs a mix that drains.

### A simple mix that drains

The planning figure this guide uses is ordinary potting soil with extra perlite for drainage, in a pot with drainage holes. Free drainage is the foundation of how to care for spider plant in a pot, because every watering rule in this guide assumes the excess can leave. Our [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs, and our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself — a recipe of potting soil, perlite, and compost that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy. The comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite is the right lightener here: it drains and keeps air in the mix, and this plant needs drainage more than it needs moisture retention — the mix should hold water like a wrung-out sponge, not like a swamp. The thick, fleshy roots store water already, so a mix that holds too much water on top of that is a mix that rots.

### Repotting: when the roots crack the pot

Repotting is the part of how to care for spider plant that surprises people, because the answer is to do it rarely — and because the plant tells you when it is time in a way no other houseplant does. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says plants grow and produce plantlets best when slightly pot bound, and that since spider plants grow quickly and roots can easily become too crowded, they need frequent repotting to do their best. South Dakota State University Extension says the same from the other side: the plant likes to be root bound and will produce babies when root bound — but if left too long, it can crack the pot or container and would need to be repotted in general potting soil. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same: spider plants form thick, fleshy tuberous roots, and to divide and repot plants before the roots expand enough to crack the container. That cracking pot is the signal: when the roots push the plant up out of the soil, or circle the inside of the pot, or threaten to split it, the plant has outgrown its home. UF/IFAS Extension gives the rhythm: a potted spider plant will thrive with repotting once a year while the plant is young and then every other year once it matures.

### A snug pot makes more babies in how to care for spider plant

The planning figure this guide uses is every one to two years, in spring — only when the roots crowd the pot, push the plant upward, or poke out of the drainage holes. A plant that is repotted into a pot that is too large stops making babies and makes leaves instead, because it has room to grow roots before it needs to reproduce. A plant that is kept slightly pot-bound makes babies freely, because it has run out of room and its answer is to make new plants. That snug pot is the repotting rule in how to care for spider plant, and it is the opposite of what most owners assume.

### Go one size up, and keep the root ball intact

When you do repot, go only one pot size up — an inch or two wider — and keep the plant at the same depth, with the root ball mostly intact. Do not break the root ball apart or wash the old soil off: the thick roots resent disturbance, and a rough repotting can set the plant back a whole season. Clemson Cooperative Extension says repotting can be done at any time of the year, and to divide and repot before the roots expand enough to crack the container. And then wait before feeding: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to wait four to six months before feeding newly potted plants, and South Dakota State University Extension says the same — it is advisable to wait four to six months before you feed a newly potted plant. A plant that is repotted gently, rarely, and one size up will keep growing steadily for years; a plant that is repotted often and roughly will droop and yellow every time. That restraint is the repotting rule in how to care for spider plant.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to feed plants during periods of active growth with a water-soluble or time-release houseplant fertilizer, and to follow the label recommendations. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says fertilizing every three to four months is usually sufficient, or to feed more frequently using a half-strength fertilizer solution — and warns that heavily fertilized plants may not form as many plantlets, and that excessive fertilizer may lead to tip browning. South Dakota State University Extension says to fertilize the plant every three to four months. NC State Extension says not to over-fertilize, as this decreases plantlet formation.

The planning figure this guide uses is a balanced liquid fertilizer at label strength every three to four months from spring to summer, or at half strength once a month in spring and summer, and nothing at all in autumn and winter, with an occasional flush — watering thoroughly with plain water and letting it drain — between feedings. And nothing for four to six months after repotting, per Clemson Cooperative Extension and South Dakota State University Extension. That range satisfies all the sources: it is the middle of the spread, it is dilute rather than full-strength, and it stops when growth stops. All the sources agree on the season, and on stopping when growth stops, which is the part of how to care for spider plant that matters most: a plant that is fed only while it is growing never accumulates the salts that burn its leaf tips — and never gets so well fed that it forgets to make babies.

### What over-feeding does

A spider plant that puts on a few inches a year does not need much food, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots and the leaf tips. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf tip burn is caused by too much fertilizer or water that is high in soluble salts. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says excessive fertilizer may lead to tip browning, and that heavily fertilized plants may not form as many plantlets. NC State Extension says the same: do not over-fertilize, as this decreases plantlet formation. That is the double cost of over-feeding in how to care for spider plant: brown tips and no babies, at the same time, from the same mistake. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at label strength every three to four months, feed only when the soil is already moist, never onto dry soil, and skip a feeding if the plant has just been repotted or is showing stress. If the tips are already brown from over-feeding, flush the soil with plain water — a thorough watering that runs out of the drainage holes, repeated once — and stop feeding until spring. That light hand is the feeding rule in how to care for spider plant, and it is the cheapest of the five rules to follow: a single bottle of balanced liquid feed lasts years at this rate.

## Getting it to make babies

Making babies is the reason most people buy a spider plant, and it is also the most misunderstood part of how to care for spider plant — because the plant makes babies on its own schedule, not yours, and because the shop plant you bought was probably already carrying them before you ever saw it. Understanding that is the key to not being disappointed.

### Why a young plant makes no babies

The first reason is youth. A plant under a year old simply has not matured enough to make babies, and no amount of fertilizer will change that — and fertilizer, as the last section showed, makes it worse, not better. The second reason is light, and it is the common one: a plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never makes a baby, because making babies takes energy the plant is not making. Move it to bright indirect light, give it a season, and the stems usually follow.

### The short-days trigger in how to care for spider plant

The third reason is the season, and it is the one most owners never hear about. NC State Extension says the stolons and plantlets develop when the mother plant receives short days and long, uninterrupted nights — less than 12 hours of light a day — for at least three weeks. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says the same: the long stems are produced in response to short days and long, uninterrupted nights for at least three weeks, although when grown indoors they may appear at any time of year. Clemson Cooperative Extension says spider plants produce most of their offshoots when the days shorten in the fall.

That is why the plant you bought in the shop was covered in babies in November: it had just lived through the short days of autumn. Your plant at home will not be on a grower's schedule, so it will make babies on the natural cycle — short days and long nights in autumn, with plantlets through winter and spring — and it will make them less reliably than the shop plant did. That is normal, not a failure, and it is worth knowing before you decide the plant is broken. Give it bright light, a snug pot, a cool autumn with short days, light feeding in spring, and a season of waiting, and the babies will come.

### What to do when it will not make babies

If the plant is over a year old, in bright indirect light, watered evenly, and still has not made a baby, check the pot first: a plant that has just been repotted into a pot that is too large will make roots and leaves before it makes babies, and it may take a season to settle. Then check the feeding: a plant that has been fed heavily may be too comfortable to reproduce, and the fix is to stop feeding until spring. Then check the light at night: a plant that sits under a lamp that stays on all evening never gets the long, uninterrupted nights that trigger the stolons, per NC State Extension — and a plant in a room where the lights burn late is a plant that never feels autumn. And then wait. A spider plant that is healthy and green but not making babies is a plant that needs time, not a rescue. Do not buy "bloom booster" fertilizer or move the plant into direct sun to force babies — direct sun scorches the leaves, and heavy feeding burns the tips and stops the babies, and a scorched, burned plant makes fewer babies, not more. The patience is the babies rule in how to care for spider plant: bright light, a snug pot, short days, light feeding, and a season of waiting.

### A note on the air-cleaning claim

You may have bought the plant because someone told you it cleans the air. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says studies have shown that spider plant is quite effective in cleaning indoor air by absorbing chemicals including formaldehyde, xylene, benzene, and carbon monoxide in homes or offices. UF/IFAS Extension says the same in its own words: like many indoor plants, spider plant removes airborne pollutants from the air. That study is real — and it was done in small sealed chambers, not in a lived-in room with doors, windows, and ventilation. A pot or two on a windowsill does not meaningfully clean a room's air, and no houseplant does. Buy the spider plant for its arching leaves and its free babies, which are real, and not for air cleaning, which is not. That honesty is part of how to care for spider plant: the plant is worth having for what it actually does, and it does that very well.

### Rooting the babies to make more plants

A spider plant makes new plants the easy way, and there are three ways to root the babies — which is also the only pruning this plant ever needs. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says spider plants are extremely easy to propagate by planting the plantlets that succeed the flowers in their own pots: set the plantlet, still attached to the mother plant, on the surface of a pot filled with soilless potting medium and allow it to root before severing the stem connecting it to the mother plant, and a bent paper clip or piece of wire can be used to hold the plantlet in contact with the soil until it develops roots. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same in fewer words: start new plants at any time of year from the plantlets, detach them once roots have formed, and pot directly into moist growing mix. South Dakota State University Extension gives the third way: remove one of the offshoots and directly plant it in potting soil, or place the offshoot in water for a couple of weeks to encourage more root growth before planting it in a soil medium. Larger plants can also be divided, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension.

### When to root the babies in how to care for spider plant

The time to root babies is any time of year, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — but the babies themselves appear most freely in fall, when the days shorten, so spring is when most owners find themselves with a pot full of rooted plantlets. Keep the new plants warm and out of direct sun, water them on the finger test, and do not feed them for four to six months after potting, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. A rooted plantlet will not make babies in its first year, so do not root a baby you want babies from this autumn. Root the babies you want more plants from, and that timing is the propagation rule in how to care for spider plant.

### A worked example: one plant, bought today

Here is the whole first year as a single story, for a plant bought on Thursday, 19 November 2026. The plant is a standard 'Vittatum' spider plant, 12 inches tall in a 6-inch pot, green to the tips with a white central stripe, with two long stems already carrying small plantlets. You hang it near an east-facing window, where it gets bright indirect light and no direct sun, and you mark Sundays for the finger test: push a finger an inch into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry, emptying the saucer every time. You use rainwater or tap water left out overnight, because the fluoride in your tap water browns the tips. You do not feed it at all: it is November, and the plant was just potted at the shop, so feeding waits four to six months, per Clemson Cooperative Extension.

The days are already short, so the plant makes more babies through the winter — the short-days trigger NC State Extension describes, less than 12 hours of light a day for at least three weeks. In spring you pin one plantlet, still attached to the mother plant, onto a small pot of moist mix with a bent paper clip, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, and sever the stem once it has rooted.

### Spring: the first feeding and the first baby in how to care for spider plant

The feeding starts in spring: a balanced liquid fertilizer at label strength every three to four months, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, and nothing in winter. The plant grows a few inches over the year, puts out new leaves from the center of the clump, and by next autumn it has had another season of short days — and a dozen new plantlets appear. You repot it in spring 2028, one pot size up, keeping the root ball mostly intact, when the roots crowd the pot. After a few years of that routine, the plant is 2 to 2 and a half feet wide, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, with stems trailing 2 to 3 feet in its hanging basket, and it has made a dozen new plants you gave away. That is the whole story of how to care for spider plant: a finger in the soil, a bright spot without sun, a warm room, soft water, a little food in summer, a snug pot, and patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most spider plant problems have one of four causes — water, water quality, light, or feeding — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a damaged leaf never heals. A browned leaf tip will not turn green again, so troubleshooting in how to care for spider plant is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Brown leaf tips

Brown tips are the most common complaint, and they have five possible causes — which is why they are so often misdiagnosed. Clemson Cooperative Extension says leaf tip burn is caused by too much fertilizer or water that is high in soluble salts, and that low humidity and excessively dry soil may also cause brown leaf tips. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says the same, and adds fluoride and chlorine in tap water: low humidity, excessively dry soil, salt accumulation, and chemicals in tap water may cause brown leaf tips. UF/IFAS Extension says the plant is particularly sensitive to fluoridated water. NC State Extension says to avoid watering with tap water, as the chlorine and fluorides can cause leaf tip burn. South Dakota State University Extension says browning of leaf tips is caused by low humidity, dry soil, or chemical build-up from tap water.

### Check the five causes in order in how to care for spider plant

Check the five causes in order. First, the water: if you water straight from the tap, switch to rainwater or distilled water, or leave tap water out for 24 hours so the chemicals evaporate, per South Dakota State University Extension. Second, the feeding: if you have been feeding every month at full strength, stop, flush the soil with plain water, and feed only at label strength every three to four months in spring and summer. Third, the watering: if the soil is dry, water thoroughly and start the finger test. Fourth, the light: if the plant sits in direct sun, move it back from the window. Fifth, the humidity: if the heat has been on for weeks and the air feels dry, set up a humidifier or a pebble tray. Do not cut the brown tips off the leaves — the brown part is dead, but trimming it leaves a blunt end that looks worse and does not help the leaf. Leave the tips alone, fix the cause, and judge the plant by its new growth, not by the browned tips. That patience is the troubleshooting habit in how to care for spider plant.

### No babies

A plant that stays green and never makes a baby is almost always short of light, too young, too comfortable, or too well fed — and the fix is time and a brighter spot, not fertilizer. NC State Extension says the stolons and plantlets develop when the mother plant receives short days and long, uninterrupted nights — less than 12 hours of light a day — for at least three weeks. Move the plant to bright indirect light, give it a cool autumn with short days, stop feeding it so much, check that the pot is snug rather than roomy, and wait a season. A plant that was carrying babies at the shop may take a year to make more on its own cycle — that is normal, and it is not a sign that anything is wrong. Do not move the plant into direct sun to force babies, and do not feed it heavily: both damage the leaves and make babies less likely, not more. The babies section above covers this in full, and the short version is the same: bright light, a snug pot, short days, light feeding, and patience. That waiting is the hardest lesson in how to care for spider plant, and it is the one most owners need to hear.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the most ambiguous symptom, because they point in two opposite directions. A plant that is over-watered yellows, and a plant that is under-watered yellows too. Check the soil first: if it is wet, the plant is over-watered — let it dry out and empty the saucer. If it is dry, the plant is under-watered — water thoroughly. If the soil is fine and only the oldest, lowest leaves are yellowing, the plant may simply be renewing itself as the clump grows, or the old soil may be exhausted and the plant may need repotting. A few yellow lower leaves on an otherwise healthy plant are normal; a whole plant turning yellow at once is a watering problem, and the soil tells you which one. That soil check is the yellow-leaf rule in how to care for spider plant.

### Drooping

Drooping is the most useful symptom, because it points at the cause — if you check the soil first. The thick, fleshy roots store water, per the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, so a spider plant forgives a missed watering — but it still droops when the soil dries out badly, and the leaves go pale and limp. If the soil is dry, the fix is a thorough watering now, and the plant usually recovers within hours — that fast recovery is the sign you read it right. If the soil is wet and the plant still droops, do not water again: the roots may be rotting, and the fix is to let the soil dry out, improve the drainage, and water only when the top inch is dry. A plant that droops every few days in dry soil needs a bigger pot or more frequent checks; a plant that droops in wet soil needs less water, not more. That soil check is the first step in every drooping diagnosis in how to care for spider plant.

### Pests

The spider plant is generally a healthy plant indoors, and pest trouble is uncommon — but there are four pests to watch for. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the most common insect pest problems include whiteflies, spider mites, scales, and aphids. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension says spider plants generally have few pest problems other than scale insects and mealybugs. South Dakota State University Extension lists the same pests: scales or mealybugs may show up. NC State Extension says there are no significant problems. Clemson Cooperative Extension also says that in the home, plant diseases are very rarely a problem, and that too much or too little water, along with insects and mites, are the primary problems. That wipe is the pest rule in how to care for spider plant: check the undersides of the leaves when you water, wipe off any mealybugs or scale you find with a damp cloth, and let the soil dry slightly between waterings to discourage fungus gnats — our guide to [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers that pest in full. Root rot follows over-watering, per Clemson Cooperative Extension and the University of Wisconsin-Madison Division of Extension, so the watering rule is the disease rule too: moist, never soggy, and never sitting in water.

## Is spider plant poisonous to pets?

No — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for spider plant, because the plant is so common that it sits within reach of pets in millions of homes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/spider-plant) lists the spider plant (*Chlorophytum comosum*) as non-toxic to dogs and non-toxic to cats, and NC State Extension confirms the same non-toxic status. That does not mean a pet should eat the plant: the ASPCA notes that eating any plant material can cause mild stomach upset, and cats in particular love to chew the arching leaves, so a plant in a cat's reach will get nibbled. The nibbling is not poisoning, and it will not cause the kidney failure that true lilies cause in cats — a spider plant is not a lily at all, it is a member of the asparagus family, per NC State Extension. If a pet eats a large amount of any plant and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435.

### The safest plant on the windowsill

That non-toxic listing is what makes the spider plant the safest plant on the windowsill, and the comparison with its neighbors is worth making: the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) is toxic if chewed, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) is toxic, and the [Norfolk Island pine](/blog/how-to-care-for-norfolk-island-pine/) is non-toxic, like the spider plant. The spider plant sits at the safe end of that row — not the most dangerous plant in the house, and the one you can hang where the cat can reach it without worry. A cat that treats the leaves as a toy will chew them and may bring them back up, and the plant will look ragged — but neither the cat nor the plant is in danger. If you want a hanging basket in a house with cats, the spider plant is the plant to hang first.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Non-toxic does not mean the plant has to live where pets can chew it — it means the plant can live there safely. But a plant that is chewed every day looks terrible, and the leaves never recover their tips, so hang the basket where the cat can see it but cannot reach it, or accept that the leaves will be nibbled. A cat that treats the leaves as a toy will chew them, get a mild stomach upset, and learn — but the lesson should not have to be learned at all. If a pet eats a large amount of any plant, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. For a household with cats, dogs, and small children, that placement is the one safety rule in how to care for spider plant that matters more than any other — and it is decided by where the pot hangs, not by anything you buy.

## The short version

How to care for spider plant, in one paragraph: buy a green, right-sized plant — 'Mandaianum' for a shelf, a standard 'Vittatum' for a hanging basket, a green plant only if you want the fastest growth and the most babies; hang it in bright indirect light near an east-facing window, with no direct sun; water thoroughly when the top inch feels dry, drain it fully, and empty the saucer — roughly weekly in summer, every two weeks or longer in a cool winter room; use rainwater or distilled water, or tap water left out overnight, because fluoride and salts brown the tips; keep it at 65 to 75°F by day, 50 to 55°F at night, never below 55°F if you can help it, away from drafts and vents; feed at label strength every three to four months in spring and summer, nothing in autumn and winter, and nothing for four to six months after repotting; repot every one to two years, one size up, only when the roots crowd the pot; pin a plantlet onto a small pot while it is still attached to root it, and sever the stem once it has rooted; trim each faded stem at the base; and hang it where the cat can see it, because it is non-toxic to cats and dogs. Do that, and the 12-inch plant you buy this November will be a 2- to 2-and-a-half-foot basket in a few years, making a dozen new plants a year — and a plant you can divide and share.

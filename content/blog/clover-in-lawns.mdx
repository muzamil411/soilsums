Two completely different groups of people search for clover in lawns. One wants it gone. The other
wants to plant it deliberately. Most sites write those as unrelated pages, and they are wrong to,
because **the two questions share a single set of facts** — and each group is better off knowing what
the other one knows.

So this page gives both, honestly, including the parts each side would rather not hear.

## Clover became a weed by convention, not by agronomy

This is the fact that reframes the whole question, and it is not disputed.

**Clover used to be a standard ingredient in lawn seed mixes.** It was included on purpose, because
it does three useful things: it fixes its own nitrogen from the air, it stays green through dry spells
when grass has gone brown and dormant, and it flowers for pollinators.

What changed was not the plant. **Selective herbicides arrived** — products that kill broadleaf
plants while leaving grasses unharmed. Clover is a broadleaf. A lawn treated with one loses its
clover and keeps its grass, and once that was possible, the uniform all-grass lawn became the thing
to aim for, and clover became one of the things that a weedkiller removes.

So "clover is a lawn weed" is a statement about the last few decades of lawn fashion and product
design, rather than about whether clover is good or bad for turf. That is worth knowing whichever
direction you came here from:

- **If you are here to remove it**, you should at least know you are removing something that was put
  there on purpose, for reasons that still hold.
- **If you are here to sow it**, you are not doing anything novel. You are undoing a convention.

None of which means you should not remove it. Plenty of good reasons to, below. It means the decision
is yours to make on the merits rather than inherited.

## Why it is in your lawn

Clover appears where grass is not dense enough to exclude it, and it does especially well where
**nitrogen is short**. That follows directly from the nitrogen fixing: on hungry soil, grass is
struggling and clover is not, because clover brings its own supply.

This is the single most useful diagnostic on the page. A lawn with a lot of clover is usually telling
you it is thin, or underfed, or both. Which is also why the non-chemical removal method works — and
why a herbicide alone tends to be temporary, because it removes the plant without changing the
conditions that favoured it.

[How to add nitrogen to soil](/blog/how-to-add-nitrogen-to-soil/) has the sourced figures for
feeding, and is the page to read alongside this one if you are removing clover.

## If you want it gone

Two routes, and the first is slower and more durable than the second.

### Change the conditions

**Feed the lawn.** This is the main one, and it attacks the advantage rather than the plant. A
well-fed lawn grows densely enough to shade and crowd clover, and removes the nitrogen deficit that
gave it the edge.

**Mow higher.** Clover grows low and spreads sideways; grass grows upright. Cutting short favours the
clover by letting light down to it. Raising the height tips the competition back.

**Overseed the thin patches.** Bare ground is where clover establishes. Filling it with grass first
is the most direct form of prevention, and [how long does grass seed take to
grow](/blog/how-long-does-grass-seed-take-to-grow/) covers the timing, the watering and the rates —
including why the published seeding rates disagree by as much as a factor of two.

**Hand-pull small patches**, ideally after rain, when the whole plant including its roots lifts
rather than snapping. Clover spreads by creeping stems that root as they go, so a patch left with
roots in the ground regrows. This is genuinely practical for a few patches and hopeless for a lawn
full of it.

Expect this route to take a season or two. It is also the route that does not come back, because the
conditions are different afterwards.

### Spot-treat it

A selective broadleaf herbicide kills clover and spares grass, which is the whole reason it is now
called a weed.

Two things worth saying plainly. **Spot-treat rather than blanket-spray** where you can: you are
treating patches, not the lawn, and the smaller the treated area the better. And **it is temporary
unless you also change the conditions** — if the lawn is still thin and underfed next year, clover
returns to the same places, because those places are still where clover wins.

Follow the product label for rate and timing, which is the only figure on this page we are not going
to give you: herbicide rates are product-specific and legally controlled, and they differ by
jurisdiction. The label is the authority, not us.

## If you want to sow it

Also a perfectly good decision, and here are the downsides first, because most pages selling clover
seed do not lead with them.

**It is slippery underfoot when wet.** Noticeably more than grass — the leaves are broad, smooth and
hold water. On a path line, a slope or steps, that is a real consideration rather than a quibble.

**It flowers, and the flowers attract bees.** That is the point for pollinators and a genuine problem
where small children play barefoot, or anyone in the household reacts badly to stings. Mowing keeps
the flowers down, but a clover lawn that is never allowed to flower has given up one of its main
advantages.

**It patches unevenly.** Clover spreads by creeping stems and colonises in drifts rather than forming
an even sward. A pure clover lawn looks mottled, and it does not take wear the way a dense grass turf
does.

Which points at the answer most people actually want: **mix it into grass rather than replacing the
grass.** A lawn with clover through it gets the nitrogen, the drought colour and the flowers, while
the grass gives the uniformity and the wear tolerance. That is, not coincidentally, what the old seed
mixes did.

### How to sow it into existing lawn

1. **Mow short**, shorter than usual, to get light down to soil level.
2. **Open the surface.** Scarify, or rake hard with a spring-tine rake. Seed needs contact with soil,
   and a thatch layer holds it above the soil where it will not establish.
3. **Broadcast the seed.** Clover seed is very fine and hard to spread evenly by hand — **mix it with
   dry sand** and sow the mixture, which makes the coverage visible and far more even.
4. **Do not bury it.** A light rake and then firm contact is all it wants.
5. **Keep the surface damp while it establishes**, on exactly the same terms as grass seed: little and
   often, and a single dry spell during germination loses the sowing.

**Timing** is the same as for cool-season grass seed — late summer into early autumn, or spring —
because the requirement is the same: warm soil and consistent moisture. The germination page has the
published windows.

**One thing to check before you sow:** whether you have used a selective broadleaf herbicide on that
lawn recently. Some persist in the soil long enough to kill a clover sowing, and the label will say.
Sowing clover into ground still carrying a broadleaf herbicide wastes the seed.

## The honest summary

Clover is not a disease and removing it is not a repair. It is a plant that does several useful things
and a few inconvenient ones, and the lawn fashion of the last few decades happens to run against it.

If you want a uniform, hard-wearing, conventional lawn: remove it, and feed and thicken the grass so
it does not come back. If you want a lawn that needs less feeding, stays greener in drought and works
for pollinators: sow it, and accept that it is slipperier, buzzier and patchier.

What does not work is treating it as a mystery. Clover in a lawn is nearly always a message about
density and nitrogen, and that message is worth reading before you decide what to do about it.

A succession planting schedule re-sows fast crops **every two to three weeks** instead of all at
once, so a bed produces continuously rather than in one glut. Radish, lettuce, spinach, bush
beans, carrots and cilantro all repay it. The last sowing of each goes in with enough days to
maturity left before your first fall frost.

## The four techniques that all get called succession planting

Most articles pick one without saying which. They solve different problems, and a garden using
all four produces considerably more than one using none.

| Technique           | What it does                                    | Best for                                |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Repeated sowings    | Spreads one crop over months                    | Radish, lettuce, spinach, beans         |
| Following crops     | Uses the same ground two or three times         | Any bed, over a full season             |
| Interplanting       | Grows a fast crop in a slow crop's future space | Radish in carrots, lettuce in brassicas |
| Staggered varieties | One sowing, different maturity dates            | Sweet corn, potatoes, tomatoes          |

## How often to succession plant lettuce

Every two weeks in spring and autumn, every three in the heat of summer.

Lettuce matures in 45 to 65 days from sowing and holds in good condition for only a week or two
before bolting, so a single sowing gives you far more than you can eat and then nothing.

A practical schedule for one household:

| When                            | What to sow                  | Interval      |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------- |
| 4 weeks before last frost       | First direct sowing          | —             |
| Through spring                  | A short row or 2 squares     | Every 2 weeks |
| Midsummer                       | Heat-tolerant varieties only | Every 3 weeks |
| Late summer to early autumn     | Back to every 2 weeks        | Every 2 weeks |
| 8 weeks before first fall frost | Last sowing                  | —             |

Our [lettuce guide](/crops/lettuce/) carries the 45–65 day maturity range those intervals come
from. Sowing in the heat of high summer is the round most likely to fail — lettuce germinates
poorly above about 80°F and bolts fast once it is up.

## Which vegetables are worth re-sowing

| Worth it              | Days to maturity | Interval  |
| --------------------- | ---------------- | --------- |
| Radish                | 22–30            | 2 weeks   |
| Lettuce, salad leaves | 45–65            | 2–3 weeks |
| Spinach               | 37–50            | 2–3 weeks |
| Bush bean             | 50–65            | 2–3 weeks |
| Cilantro              | 45–70            | 2–3 weeks |
| Beet                  | 50–65            | 3 weeks   |
| Carrot                | 60–80            | 3–4 weeks |

Maturity ranges from our own [crop data](/data-sources/), which is checked against US extension
sources.

**Not worth it:** anything that produces over a long period from one sowing — tomatoes, peppers,
eggplant, pole beans, kale, chard. One sowing does the whole season.

The practical habit is a note in the calendar rather than a plan. Every other Saturday, sow a
three-foot row of something.

## Following one crop with another

A bed that grows peas in spring, beans in summer and spinach in autumn has produced three crops
where most beds produce one.

Sequences that work:

- Peas or broad beans → brassicas → garlic
- Spinach → bush beans → autumn lettuce
- Early potatoes → leeks
- Radish and salad → tomatoes, once the soil is warm → overwintering onions

Put a heavy feeder after a legume, which leaves nitrogen behind, and follow a deep-rooted crop
with a shallow one.

The constraint is fertility. Three crops take three crops' worth of nutrients out, so the bed
needs compost between rounds —
[how often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers the feeding side.

## Interplanting

Putting a fast crop in the space a slow crop will eventually need, and harvesting it before the
slow crop gets there.

[Radishes](/crops/carrot/) sown along a carrot drill come up in days, mark the row while the
carrots germinate, and are pulled long before the carrots need the space. Lettuce between young
brassicas is finished by the time the brassicas close over. Spinach under sweet corn uses the
ground while the corn is still short.

This is the technique with the least downside. There is no claimed chemistry, just use of space
and time, and it reliably increases what a bed produces.

## Planning it around your frost dates

Two dates bracket the whole schedule, and everything else counts from them.

**The first sowing** is set by your last spring frost. Cool-season crops go in before it —
spinach five weeks before, lettuce four weeks before, peas four weeks before, carrots two weeks
before. Warm-season crops wait until after, _and_ until the soil is warm enough: peas want soil
at 45°F (7°C), sweet corn about 60°F (16°C), cucumber and squash about 70°F (21°C).

**The last sowing** is set by your first fall frost. Work backwards:

```
last sowing date = first fall frost - days to maturity - 2 weeks
```

The two-week margin covers slower growth as days shorten — a crop that matures in 50 days in June
can take 65 in September at the same latitude.

The [planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) turns your own frost dates into
actual calendar dates for thirty crops, and
[what does last frost date mean](/blog/what-does-last-frost-date-mean/) explains why the
published date is a probability rather than a promise.

## Staggered varieties

One sowing, different maturity dates. Sweet corn is the classic case: sowing an early, a
mid-season and a late variety on the same day spreads the harvest across a month without needing
warm soil three separate times.

It also works for potatoes — first early, second early and main crop — and for tomatoes, where an
early determinate variety alongside a late indeterminate one gives you fruit weeks sooner and for
longer.

## Keeping ground covered

The point of all four techniques is that nothing sits empty. Bare soil grows weeds, loses
structure to rain and dries out.

If a bed will be empty for more than a couple of weeks, sow a quick cover crop or mulch it. In a
square foot grid the empty squares announce themselves, which is one of the genuine advantages
of the method — [how does square foot gardening work](/blog/how-does-square-foot-gardening-work/)
covers the rest.

**Between 6 and 8 a.m., and probably not an inch a week.** University of Missouri Extension publishes
weekly water use by grass and it runs from 0.3 inches to 1.5, so the figure everyone repeats is
nearly double what a tall fescue lawn needs and short of what perennial ryegrass needs. The amount
depends on which grass you have.

Almost every lawn-watering page starts with the inch and stops there. It is the wrong place to start,
because the grass in front of you is the thing that decides.

Read the last column first. **One inch a week is not a conservative average** — it is too much for
three of these grasses and not enough for the other two. If you have a tall fescue lawn and you have
been putting an inch on it every week all summer, you have been watering it nearly twice as hard as
it asked to be.

The dormant column is a different goal rather than a smaller version of the same one. A green figure
keeps the lawn growing; a dormant figure keeps it alive while it is brown and waiting. Choosing to
let a lawn go dormant through a dry summer is a legitimate decision, not a failure, and the dormant
number is what that decision costs.

**Which grass do I have?** If you do not know, the honest answer is that it is probably a mix, and
the safe reading is to take the lowest figure of the species you might have and watch the lawn rather
than the calendar. A lawn that needs more will tell you before it comes to harm: it goes dull, then
grey-blue, and footprints stay visible.

## Between 6 and 8 in the morning

Missouri's window is **6 to 8 a.m.**, and the reasons stack up rather than resting on one thing:

- **Less is lost to evaporation.** Air is coolest and the sun is weakest, so more of what you put
  down reaches the soil instead of the sky.
- **Wind is usually lightest**, which matters more than it sounds — a sprinkler in a breeze waters
  the path.
- **The leaf dries through the day.** Grass wet at dawn is dry by mid-morning. Grass wet at dusk
  stays wet for eight or ten hours, and a persistently damp leaf surface is the condition fungal
  disease needs to establish.

That last point is why evening watering is the habit worth breaking even though it feels sensible —
the water does go on without evaporating, and then sits there all night. If mornings are genuinely
impossible, early evening beats not watering at all, but it is the worst of the available times
rather than a neutral one.

Midday is the one people ask about and it is mostly a waste rather than a danger. Water lost to
evaporation is water you paid for and the lawn never saw.

## Half an inch an hour, which changes how you run the sprinkler

This is the figure that quietly ruins more watering than any other. **Most Missouri soils absorb only
about half an inch of water an hour.**

Put an inch on in a single hour and the second half of it has nowhere to go. It runs off down the
slope, out onto the path, or sits in a hollow — and the part of the lawn you were watering has had
half of what the gauge says you gave it. Meanwhile the driveway is wet and you have concluded that
your lawn needs more water than it does.

So the week's amount is not one event:

1. **Work out the week's figure** from the table above for your grass.
2. **Split it** across two or three sessions rather than one, or run a session in stages with a pause
   between them so the water can soak in.
3. **Watch for runoff** while it is running. Runoff is the signal that you have passed the soil's
   intake rate, and nothing after that point is doing any good.

Heavy clay takes it more slowly still, and a compacted lawn more slowly again. If water starts
standing or running within minutes, the problem is compaction rather than the sprinkler, and no
watering schedule fixes that.

## Verifying it with a spade

The target is not a reading on a hose timer. Missouri's standard is **moisture through the top 6 to 8
inches of soil**, because that is the root zone that matters, and the only reliable way to know
whether you got there is to look.

**Cut a wedge.** Push a spade in, lever a triangular plug back, and look at the cut face: you can see
exactly where moist soil stops and dry soil starts. Press the plug back and the lawn closes over in a
few days. A long screwdriver is the quick version — it pushes easily through moist soil and stops at
dry.

Do this once at the start of a dry spell and it recalibrates everything. Most people watering by
habit find the moisture stops two or three inches down, which means the roots below that never saw
any of it, and that a deeper, less frequent soak would serve the lawn better than the same total
water spread over daily sprinkles.

## Watering a lawn you have let go brown

A dormant lawn is not a dead lawn, and it is not a lawn that needs nothing either. **Missouri says
dormant turf still needs at least 1 inch every two or three weeks.**

That is a survival ration, deliberately. It will not green the lawn up — if anything, part-watering a
dormant lawn into half-growth and then stopping is worse than either committing to keeping it green
or committing to letting it sleep. The purpose is to keep the crowns alive so the lawn comes back when
the weather does.

The mistake to avoid is treating dormancy as permission to forget about it for two months. A long
enough dry spell with nothing at all is where a dormant lawn stops being able to come back.

## New seed and new sod are different jobs

Neither of these follows the weekly figures above, and getting them confused is how a new lawn fails
in its first fortnight.

**A seedbed has to stay moist to a depth of 1 to 2 inches.** That is a shallow, frequent regime — the
opposite of deep and infrequent — because the seed is sitting in exactly that layer and a single dry
spell while it is swelling ends it. [How long does grass seed take to
grow](/blog/how-long-does-grass-seed-take-to-grow/) goes into what actually governs germination, and
the short version is that moisture at the surface matters more than any day count on the bag.

**New sod is the other extreme: wet the sod and the soil beneath it to a depth of 6 inches.** Sod
arrives with its roots cut off short, and until it knits into the ground below it has almost nothing
to draw on. That one figure is what Missouri gives, and a new lawn from sod deserves a page of its
own rather than a paragraph here.

## What this page does not cover

**Sprinkler output.** How much water your own sprinkler puts down in an hour is a property of your
sprinkler and your water pressure, not something any publication can tell you. The standard trick is
to set a few straight-sided tins out on the lawn, run the sprinkler for a measured time, and measure
what collects — that converts every figure on this page into minutes for your own setup.

**Watering restrictions and local rules**, which vary and change, and which override everything here.

**Warm-season lawns beyond the figures in the table.** Zoysiagrass, bermudagrass and buffalograss
appear above because Missouri publishes their water use, but their wider management runs on a
different calendar we have not sourced.

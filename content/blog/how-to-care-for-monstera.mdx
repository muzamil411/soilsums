A monstera is the houseplant that looks like a piece of the rainforest and grows like a vine that has somewhere to be, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for monstera. It grows fast, forgives a missed watering, and can fill a corner of a room in a single summer — Penn State Extension says it grows quickly and is a great floor plant for large spaces — and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: a bright window, a chunky mix that drains fast, and something sturdy to climb. Then the same plant sits for a year making small, solid, heart-shaped leaves in the hands of an owner who keeps it in a dim corner, because how to care for monstera is not the same as caring for the leafy tropical plants on the same windowsill. A monstera is a climbing vine from the rainforests of Central America that starts life on the forest floor and climbs into the canopy, and it wants bright, filtered light, a pot it can drain from, and a pole to climb. Give it those, and it grows into a floor plant with leaves as wide as dinner plates, split and holed like Swiss cheese.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A monstera that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go limp and droop, and then they lift again soon after watering. A monstera that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its stems grow long and bare between leaves, and the new leaves come out small and solid, with no holes at all. A plant moved suddenly into strong sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much grows soft and burns at the leaf edges. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, yellowing leaves, stretch, scorch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for monstera, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are NC State Extension, Penn State Extension, the University of Wisconsin Extension, the University of Connecticut, and UF/IFAS Extension, with the ASPCA covering the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the yellow-leaves section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most. If you bought it for the split leaves and got solid ones instead, read the light section first — because the holes answer to light, not to fertilizer.

## How to care for monstera: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and introduce strong sun gradually.** A bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or south-facing window, with a little gentle morning sun and no harsh afternoon sun, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for monstera, because the plant is a rainforest climber that grows under a canopy — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and makes small solid leaves, while a plant moved from shade into strong sun in one day scorches.

**Let the top inches of soil dry out between waterings, then water thoroughly.** Water only when the top two inches feel dry, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for monstera.

**Keep it warm, and never let it freeze.** A warm room by day and night, never below 50°F if you can help it, and inside before the first cold snap. Cold is the third rule of how to care for monstera — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single freezing night can kill a plant that filled a room all summer.

### The last two rules in how to care for monstera

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed at half strength once a month from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding burns the roots with salts, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the leaf edges.

**Keep it in a chunky mix, in a pot with holes, and give it something to climb.** An airy mix of potting soil, orchid bark, and perlite in a pot with drainage holes, repotted when the roots fill the pot — and a moss pole or sturdy stake, because a monstera without support sprawls sideways instead of climbing, and its stems break under their own weight. Free drainage and a support are the fifth rule of how to care for monstera, and they protect the roots and the stems for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for monstera does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays upright and full: large, glossy, heart-shaped leaves that develop deep clefts and oblong perforations as they grow, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it climbs: a thick stem with long, tentacle-like aerial roots that grip the pole, per the University of Wisconsin Extension, and leaves that get bigger with every new one once the plant is attached, per the University of Connecticut — and that climbing habit is the reason the plant is worth keeping for years. Third, it grows fast: a plant that is watered on the soil test, kept warm, and given a pole grows to 6 to 8 feet tall indoors, per NC State Extension, and makes a new split leaf every few weeks all summer. The five rules in how to care for monstera are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the rainforest canopy, followed indoors.

## What you bought: a climbing vine in a pot

The first thing to get straight in how to care for monstera is what the plant actually is, because the name tells you only half the story. It is a monstera — *Monstera deliciosa*, also sold as the Swiss cheese plant, the split-leaf philodendron, the cut-leaf philodendron, the ceriman, and the Mexican breadfruit, per UF/IFAS Extension — a woody, perennial, broadleaf evergreen, climbing vine in the arum family, per NC State Extension. The name *deliciosa* refers to the ripe fruit, which is said to taste like a cross between a banana and a pineapple, per NC State Extension — and the name *monstera* refers to the monstrous size the plant can reach. It is not a philodendron, though both are in the arum family, per Penn State Extension, and the University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: this member of the Araceae family was formerly classified in the genus *Philodendron*. That old name is the reason so many shops still label it split-leaf philodendron — and the reason so many owners buy a plant that is not what the label says.

### A plant from the rainforests of Central America

In the wild the plant comes from the hot, humid, tropical forests of Mexico, Guatemala, Costa Rica, and Panama, per UF/IFAS Extension, and NC State Extension says the same in fewer words: native from Mexico to Panama. The University of Wisconsin Extension gives the range as the rainforests of Central America from southern Mexico to Panama. The University of Connecticut says it is native to Mexico and Panama but is found globally in tropical and subtropical regions. That background explains almost everything about how to care for monstera. It is a plant from a warm, wet place where the rain comes often and the soil drains instantly, and it wants bright, filtered light, warm air, and a soil that dries out between rains. It is not a desert succulent and it is not a shade plant — it is a climbing vine that starts on the forest floor and grows up into the canopy, which is why it wants light from above and something to climb. NC State Extension says the same in its own words: in its native habitat it starts its life as a terrestrial plant, turning epiphytic once it comes into contact with a sturdy tree it can climb.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a vine in miniature: a thick, cylindrical stem carrying large, glossy, heart-shaped leaves on long, stiff stalks. The University of Wisconsin Extension describes the leaves as glossy, heart-shaped or rounded, and leathery, developing deep clefts and oblong perforations as they grow, and says the leaves may be as much as 18 inches wide on foot-long leafstalks. UF/IFAS Extension says the same: the heart-shaped leaves are held on stiff, 2- to 3-foot-long petioles, and the leaves are green and large, 3 or more feet long by 2 to 3 feet wide, oval, deeply dissected along the margins and perforated on each side of the midrib with elliptic or oblong holes of various sizes. Penn State Extension says the leaves are simple, alternate, and heart-shaped, with a long petiole, and that juvenile leaves are small, green, and mostly entire, without cuts — and that as the leaves mature they become very large, glossy green, and perforated or cut. The holes are the plant's signature, and a plant with small, solid leaves is either young or under-lit — and reading that difference is the first skill in how to care for monstera.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for monstera, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the size, because the plant grows fast and the pot decides how big it gets. NC State Extension says the plant can grow to 70 feet tall, though it stays between 6 to 8 feet in most home environments. Penn State Extension says the same: in the wild the species grows 30 to 70 feet tall with a spread of 6 to 10 feet, and indoors it will still reach a hefty 6 to 8 feet tall. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant can grow 70 feet or more in nature and rarely branches. Buy the size for the spot: a small plant in a 6-inch pot for a bright shelf, a standard plant for a bright corner with floor space, and a large plant only if you have a tall ceiling and a sturdy pole — because a plant that reaches 8 feet needs room, and a plant without a pole sprawls across the floor instead.

### The age that matters: a plant that outgrows its room

The second is the age, because the split leaves and the fruit both come only to a mature plant. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the young plants appear very different: seedlings grow toward the darkest area until they encounter a tree, then they grow upwards, and the leaves are small and without lobes or holes, growing closely overlapping each other up the tree trunk in a stage called shingle plants — and only as plants grow older do they develop the characteristic foliage of a mature plant. The University of Connecticut says monstera is not commonly grown for its fruit in New England, because specimens grown as houseplants do not typically reach the appropriate size or maturity to flower and produce fruit. A plant you buy this year will not flower this year — and a plant kept indoors may never flower at all — and that is normal, not a failure. The fruit is a bonus, not the point, and a plant that never fruits is still a healthy plant. What the age buys you is the leaf: a monstera kept for a few years makes leaves a foot or more across, per Penn State Extension, and a monstera kept for ten years fills a wall. That is why the plant is worth buying young and keeping: you are not buying a decoration, you are starting a vine, and that long view is the point of how to care for monstera.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The third is the condition of the leaves: they should be thick, glossy, and firm, dark green with no yellow patches, no brown edges, and no bare stems where leaves have already dropped. Yellow leaves on a plant in the shop mean the plant has already been over-watered, and those leaves will not turn green again. Brown, crispy edges mean the air has been too dry, per the University of Wisconsin Extension — and a plant that is already crisp in the shop has been neglected, and a plant with clean green edges is a better buy. The soil should be damp, not wet, and the pot should have drainage holes and enough weight that the plant cannot tip over. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in a light plastic pot with a heavy top will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. Buy the glossy, firm, right-sized plant in damp soil in a heavy pot, and the rest of how to care for monstera is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for monstera, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. NC State Extension says the plant prefers moderate brightness but not direct sunlight. Penn State Extension says to select a site near a sunny window where the plant can receive bright light but not direct sun, and warns that it will get leggy in lower light. The University of Wisconsin Extension says monstera does best in bright light in summer and direct sun in winter, and that it can be grown under fluorescent light but will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate. The University of Connecticut says monstera prefers partial shade with direct sunlight for 3 to 6 hours daily. UF/IFAS Extension says plants grow best under light shade, or filtered sunlight, and that intense sun exposure may cause leaf scorching. The planning figure this guide uses is a bright spot a few feet from an east-facing or south-facing window, with a little gentle morning sun and no harsh afternoon sun — and winter sun through glass is welcome, because the sun is weaker then. That is the light that keeps the plant compact, upright, and making split leaves.

### The sunburn warning

The warning that matters most is the sudden one. The University of Connecticut says it is important to be vigilant when acclimating this plant to new light levels, as its large leaves burn easily. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the same: container-grown plants can be moved outside for the summer, but they need to be acclimated to higher light levels gradually, or the leaves will suffer sunburn. UF/IFAS Extension says intense sun exposure may cause leaf scorching, and that leaves grown in full sun tend to be light green and may show signs of sun burn. The burn shows as pale, bleached patches on the sunny side of the leaves, and a scorched leaf never recovers its color — the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. If your plant came from a dim shop shelf, give it bright indirect light for two weeks, then move it into a spot that gets an hour or two of gentle morning sun, and then into its permanent bright position over the next two to three weeks. That gradual move is the light rule in how to care for monstera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters second is the delayed one. A plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never grows properly, and the owner concludes that monsteras simply grow slowly, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. Penn State Extension says the plant will get leggy in lower light. The University of Wisconsin Extension says it will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate. If your plant stretches toward the window, or its new leaves come out small and solid, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for monstera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Grow lights when there is no bright window

If you do not have a bright window, a grow light is a real option, not a compromise. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant can be grown under fluorescent light, but warns that it will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate — so a light that is too weak is worse than no light at all, because it keeps the plant alive while starving it of the light it needs to make split leaves. A full-spectrum light hung a foot or two above the plant for twelve hours a day gives the plant the bright light it would get from an east window, and the new leaves will come out larger and more split within a few months. Do not leave the light on all night: the plant needs darkness, and the leaves, when they come, answer to a day and a night. That twelve-hour rule is the grow-light rule in how to care for monstera.

### Water

Watering is where most monsteras are lost, and the loss usually comes from one mistake: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain. The watering rule from NC State Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for monstera: water the plant thoroughly, then allow the top quarter to one-third of the soil to dry between waterings. Two halves, both required: water thoroughly, and then let the soil dry. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. And then remove the excess, so the pot never sits in water.

### The soak-then-dry rule in how to care for monstera

The sources give the dry test in different depths, and the spread is worth showing. NC State Extension says to water thoroughly, then allow the top quarter to one-third part to dry between watering. Penn State Extension says to water thoroughly and let the top 1 to 2 inches of the soil dry out between watering. The University of Connecticut says to allow the top 2 to 3 inches of soil to dry between waterings. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to water thoroughly and allow the growing medium to dry slightly between waterings. UF/IFAS Extension says monstera vines are moderately drought tolerant, especially when grown in the shade, and that periodic watering during dry periods will result in better growth. The planning figure this guide uses is the finger test at two inches deep: push a finger two inches into the mix, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — roughly once a week in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to every two to three weeks in a cool room in winter — always letting the excess drain away and never letting the pot sit in water. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the watering that keeps the roots healthy.

### How often to water

There is no fixed schedule, and the plant's own calendar is the season. In summer, when the plant is growing fast and the light is strong, the mix dries in about a week, and the plant wants a thorough watering. In winter, when the growth slows and the days shorten, the mix stays damp for two to three weeks, and the plant wants far less. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to water less in winter, and that plants kept on the dry side grow more slowly. The University of Connecticut says monstera is prone to root diseases, especially during the colder seasons, and that a well-draining soil and consistent watering practices will help this risk. The fix is the finger test, not a new fertilizer, and not a calendar on the fridge — and that is the watering rule in how to care for monstera.

### The sweating leaves

The warning that matters most is the wet one. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaves will sweat if the growing medium is too moist, and that if this happens, you should reduce watering to prevent root rot. Those droplets on the leaf tips are the plant pushing out water it cannot use, and they are the first sign that the mix is staying wet too long. If you see them, stop watering, let the mix dry out, and check that the pot is draining — because a plant that sweats in a pot without holes is a plant that is already rotting. That sweating rule is the watering warning in how to care for monstera.

### Temperature

Temperature is the third daily decision, and the sources agree on it more closely than on anything else. NC State Extension says the plant prefers an average warm temperature of 60 to 85 degrees F. Penn State Extension says monstera prefers warm temperatures between 60° to 85°F, and that they abhor cold drafts. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant prefers warm room temperature, and that plants do not grow below 50°F, and frost will kill them. The University of Connecticut says the plants can be placed outdoors in a semi-sheltered area during the summer months in New England as long as temperatures do not drop below 45°F, and that exposure to colder temperatures for any extended period of time will result in cold damage. UF/IFAS Extension gives the hard numbers: leaves are damaged or killed at 30 to 32°F, and stems at 26 to 28°F. The planning figure this guide uses is 60 to 85°F, never below 50°F if you can help it, and inside before the first cold snap. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the temperature that keeps the plant growing.

### What to avoid: cold and drafts

The warning that matters most is the cold one. Penn State Extension says monsteras abhor cold drafts. UF/IFAS Extension says monstera is not tolerant of freezing temperatures. The University of Connecticut says exposure to colder temperatures for any extended period of time will result in cold damage. A plant left by a drafty window in winter, or a plant moved outside for the summer and forgotten in the first cold night, will show blackened, collapsed leaves — and the damage is permanent, and the fix is prevention, not treatment. Keep the plant away from cold windows in winter, away from air-conditioning vents in summer, and inside before the first frost. That cold rule is the temperature rule in how to care for monstera.

### Humidity

Humidity is the fourth daily decision, and the sources agree on the direction even where they differ on the number. NC State Extension says the plant prefers high humidity, and that using a humidifier or setting the plant container on a tray of wet pebbles helps increase the humidity in dry interior settings. Penn State Extension says monstera likes humidity, preferably above 50%, and that this is one plant that may benefit from the addition of a nearby humidifier if the indoor space is very dry. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant prefers medium to high humidity, but is fairly tolerant of a wide range of conditions once acclimated — and that the leaf edges will turn brown if humidity is too low. The planning figure this guide uses is above 50% if you can manage it, with a humidifier or a pebble tray in a dry house, and average household air if you cannot — because the plant tolerates average air, and the brown edges are a cosmetic warning, not a death sentence.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once, at potting time, and it decides whether the watering rule can work at all. A mix that holds water like ordinary potting soil keeps the roots wet for weeks, and no watering schedule can fix it. A mix that drains fast lets the roots breathe, and the watering rule works. That is why the mix matters more than the watering can in how to care for monstera.

### A chunky mix that drains

The sources agree on the mix, and the agreement is unusually close. Penn State Extension says to use a well-draining soilless potting mix that is rich with organic matter and has a neutral pH of 6.0 to 8.0, and that charcoal, bark, or perlite can be added to a purchased mix. The University of Connecticut says the soil should be a well-draining potting mix with a high organic matter content. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to grow monstera in a fertile soilless mix, with ample root room to promote larger leaf growth. The planning figure this guide uses is a chunky aroid mix: one part good potting soil, one part orchid bark, and one part perlite, in a pot with drainage holes — a recipe that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy, and that lets air reach the roots. Our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself, and the comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite, not vermiculite, is the right loosener here — perlite drains, vermiculite holds water, and a monstera wants drainage. The [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

A pot without drainage holes is a pot that kills the plant, however good the mix is. The University of Connecticut says monstera is prone to root diseases, especially during the colder seasons, and that a well-draining soil and consistent watering practices will help this risk — and a pot without holes makes both impossible. Choose a pot with drainage holes, only a little bigger than the root ball, and heavy enough that the plant cannot tip over — because a monstera grows tall and top-heavy, and a light plastic pot will fall over the first time someone brushes past it. A clay pot is a good choice, because it breathes and helps the mix dry, and its weight keeps the plant upright. That pot rule is the soil rule in how to care for monstera.

### Repotting: when the roots fill the pot

Repotting is rare work, and the plant tells you when it is time. The University of Wisconsin Extension says container-grown plants need frequent repotting to accommodate the root system, and that they can be very vigorous growers. The University of Connecticut says to repot plants only when roots have reached the bottom and side of the pot, and to incrementally increase in pot size — and that monsteras are much more tolerant of being pot bound than over potted. NC State Extension says to prune or re-pot if needed in the spring. The planning figure this guide uses is to repot every one to two years in spring, into a pot one size up, and to add the moss pole at the same time — because a pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots, and a pot that is too small starves them. Go one size up, and water sparingly until it settles.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the least important of the five rules, and the easiest to get wrong. The sources give different schedules, and the spread is worth showing. Penn State Extension says to fertilize every two weeks through the growing season and monthly through the winter with a balanced houseplant fertilizer. The University of Connecticut says monstera is easily burned by high soil salts, but can tolerate regular fertilization during periods of active growth, from May to September in New England. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to fertilize regularly from spring until fall. The planning figure this guide uses is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at half strength once a month from spring through summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the feeding that keeps the plant growing without burning the roots.

### The planning figure: monthly at half strength, or none at all

The reason for the caution is the salt. The University of Connecticut says monstera is easily burned by high soil salts. A plant that is fed every two weeks at full strength, through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the leaf edges and the root tips — and the damage looks like drought, so the owner waters more, and the plant gets worse. The fix is to feed lightly, to flush the pot with plain water every few months, and to stop feeding entirely in autumn and winter. That monthly-at-half-strength rule is the feeding rule in how to care for monstera.

### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding grows soft, leggy stems, and the leaf edges turn brown and crispy — the same symptom as low humidity, so the owner buys a humidifier, and the plant gets worse. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaf edges will turn brown if humidity is too low, and the University of Connecticut says the plant is easily burned by high soil salts — and both symptoms look the same, and the soil is the only way to tell them apart. If the soil is salty and crusty, the cause is feeding, and the fix is to flush the pot and stop feeding. If the soil is clean and the air is dry, the cause is humidity, and the fix is a humidifier. That distinction is the feeding warning in how to care for monstera.

## Support: give it something to climb

Support is the rule that makes a monstera look like a monstera, and it is the rule most owners skip. A monstera without support does not stay small — it sprawls sideways across the floor, its stems grow long and bare, and its leaves stay small and solid. A monstera with support climbs, and its leaves get bigger with every new one. That is why the pole matters as much as the pot in how to care for monstera.

### The moss pole rule in how to care for monstera

The sources agree on the support, and the agreement is unusually close. NC State Extension says sturdy support is necessary to prevent the stems from breaking. Penn State Extension says the plant will need something sturdy to climb, and that moss sticks, trellises, bamboo stakes, and boards covered with sphagnum moss will all work. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to provide a tree bark or strong, moss-covered support sunk into the pot for the aerial roots to attach to. The University of Connecticut says it is common practice to stake vines up with garden stakes or mount them to a moss pole, and that once vines have successfully attached, leaf size typically increases drastically. The planning figure this guide uses is a moss pole sunk into the pot at repotting time, tall enough to last two years, with the stem tied loosely to it until the aerial roots grip.

### Aerial roots: what they are and what to do with them

The aerial roots are the plant's climbing gear, and they are supposed to be there. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant produces numerous, long, tentacle-like aerial roots as it grows upward, which attach to nearby branches and tree trunks, and that the tough roots grow downward from the thick stem and will root if they reach the ground. Penn State Extension says the plant uses the aerial roots that form at the base of its leaves to attach to the rainforest trees. UF/IFAS Extension says long, aerial, tentacle-like roots emerge along the vine stem, taking root when they touch the ground. Do not cut them all off: tuck the long ones into the pot or onto the pole, and water the moss pole as well as the pot, so the aerial roots can take up moisture — the University of Wisconsin Extension says to water the moss-covered support so the aerial roots can obtain water and nutrients. A plant with aerial roots is a plant that is trying to climb, and the pole is the answer.

## Pruning a vine that never stops

Pruning is the rule that keeps a fast-growing vine in its room. A monstera that is never pruned grows across the floor, up the wall, and into the next plant — and its stems get long and bare, with leaves only at the ends. A monstera that is pruned in spring stays full and upright, and the cuttings become new plants. That is why the shears matter as much as the pole in how to care for monstera.

### Prune in spring, back to a node

NC State Extension says to prune or re-pot if needed in the spring. UF/IFAS Extension says vine growth is easily controlled by cutting the stem and pulling the shallow roots out of the ground, and that periodically, vines should be cut back to maintain them in their appropriate space, otherwise they will take over a large part of the landscape. The planning figure this guide uses is to prune in spring, cutting the stem just above a node with clean, sharp shears, and to remove yellow or damaged leaves at any time of year. Cut back to a node, not to a bare stem — because a new shoot grows from the node, and a bare stem does not. That spring rule is the pruning rule in how to care for monstera.

### What the cuttings become

Every stem you cut off in spring is a new plant, and the propagation section covers how to root it. Do not throw the cuttings away: a stem with a node and an aerial root will root in water or damp mix in a few weeks, and a cutting from a plant you love is the best gift you can give a friend. That is the whole point of pruning in how to care for monstera — the plant you cut back becomes three plants, and the room stays full.

## Getting it to make split leaves

The split leaves are the reason most people buy a monstera, and they are the most misunderstood part of the plant. The holes and the cuts are not a sign of health, and they are not a sign of damage — they are a sign of maturity, and of light. A plant that makes solid leaves is not sick. It is young, or it is under-lit, and the fix is time plus a brighter spot. That is the whole of fenestration in how to care for monstera.

### Why the leaves have holes

The University of Wisconsin Extension says the fenestrations, the holes in the leaves, are theoretically a way of allowing high winds to pass through the large leaves without tearing them. The University of Connecticut says fenestrations form at the leaf midrib and radiate out, increasing as leaf size progresses, giving the plant a Swiss cheese look. Penn State Extension says the leaves are simple, alternate, and heart-shaped when young, and that as the leaves mature they become very large, glossy green, and perforated or cut. The holes appear as the leaf grows, not all at once — and a leaf that unfurls solid will not develop holes later. That is why the new leaves are the ones to watch: if the newest leaf has holes, the plant is getting enough light, and if the newest leaf is solid, the plant is asking for a brighter spot.

### The light rule for splits

The light rule for splits is the same as the light rule for the whole plant, and the University of Wisconsin Extension states it plainly: monstera can be grown under fluorescent light, but will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate. NC State Extension says variegated cultivars will need more sunlight than darker green cultivars — and the same is true of any plant that is asked to make large, split leaves, because the splits cost energy, and the energy comes from light. If your plant is mature and healthy and still makes solid leaves, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for monstera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### What about the variegated kinds?

The variegated kinds — 'Variegata', 'Albovariegata', and 'Thai Constellation' — need more light than the solid green plant, per NC State Extension, and their leaves tend to be somewhat smaller than the species, per the University of Wisconsin Extension. The white and cream patches have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesize, and the plant needs more light to make the same amount of food. Keep a variegated plant in the brightest indirect light you have, and expect it to grow more slowly than the green plant — and do not be surprised if a variegated leaf scorches in direct sun, because the pale patches burn first. That variegated rule is the light rule in how to care for monstera, applied to the plants that need it most.

## Making more plants for free

Propagation is the easiest part of how to care for monstera, and the most generous — because a plant that grows this fast makes more plants than any one house needs. A stem cutting with a node and an aerial root will root in a few weeks, and a cutting from a plant you love is the best gift you can give a friend. That is why propagation is worth doing, and worth doing right.

### Stem cuttings: the fast method

The sources agree on the method, and the agreement is unusually close. NC State Extension says to propagate by layering or stem cutting. Penn State Extension says monstera is propagated most easily through air layering or stem cuttings. The University of Wisconsin Extension says to propagate from stem cuttings from mature plants, or by air layering or simple layering, any time of the year: cut the tip of the stem just below an aerial root and pot the cutting, or cut the vine into one-foot sections, press the sections halfway into the surface of a bed of rooting medium, and transplant when roots have developed. The University of Connecticut says the ideal stem cutting is taken 3 to 5 inches below a node, with as many aerial roots present as possible, and that monstera can be propagated from stem cuttings, layering, and cane cutting. UF/IFAS Extension says monstera may be propagated by seed, stem cuttings, suckers, and tissue culture, and that it is most commonly propagated by stem cuttings. The planning figure this guide uses is to cut the stem 3 to 5 inches below a node, keeping as many aerial roots as possible, and to root the cutting in water or damp mix before potting it up. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is the cutting that makes a new plant.

### The node rule in how to care for monstera

The node is the whole of propagation, and the rule is simple: no node, no plant. A leaf on its own, without a piece of stem and a node, will stay green in water for months and never make a new vine — because the new shoot grows from the node, and a leaf has none. A stem with a node and an aerial root will root in a few weeks, and the aerial root is the head start: it is already a root, and it only needs damp mix to grow. Cut below the node, keep the aerial root, and pot the cutting in chunky mix when the roots are a few inches long. That node rule is the propagation rule in how to care for monstera.

### What about seeds?

Seeds are possible, and nobody should bother. The University of Wisconsin Extension says plants can be grown from seed, but seedlings require warm temperatures and are slow to develop. The University of Connecticut says propagation by seed is possible but uncommon, as seedlings are slow growing initially and require warm, humid temperatures which are difficult to replicate on a large scale. UF/IFAS Extension says plants generated from cuttings may come into bearing in 4 to 6 years, whereas suckers begin fruit production in 2 to 4 years — and a seedling takes longer still. Cuttings are faster, surer, and true to the parent plant, and that is why every source lists them first in how to care for monstera.

## Troubleshooting

Most monstera problems have one of four causes — water, light, cold, or feeding — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a yellow leaf never turns green again. Troubleshooting in how to care for monstera is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves, with wet soil, mean overwatering — and it is the most serious problem this plant has. Penn State Extension says overwatering can result in root rot. The University of Connecticut says monstera is prone to root diseases, especially during the colder seasons, and that overwatering is more often the cause of these issues in houseplants than drought. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaves will sweat if the growing medium is too moist, and that if this happens, you should reduce watering to prevent root rot. The fix is to stop watering immediately, take the plant out of its pot, cut away the soft, brown, rotted roots with a clean knife, and repot it in fresh chunky mix — and then to water only when the top two inches feel dry. A yellow leaf will not turn green again, and the fix is the finger test, not a new fertilizer.

### Brown, crispy leaf edges

Brown, crispy edges mean the air is too dry, or the soil is too salty — and the soil is the only way to tell them apart. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaf edges will turn brown if humidity is too low. The University of Connecticut says monstera is easily burned by high soil salts. If the soil is salty and crusty, the cause is feeding, and the fix is to flush the pot with plain water and stop feeding. If the soil is clean and the air is dry, the cause is humidity, and the fix is a humidifier or a pebble tray. NC State Extension says using a humidifier or setting the plant container on a tray of wet pebbles helps increase the humidity in dry interior settings. That distinction is the brown-edges rule in how to care for monstera.

### No holes in the leaves

Small, solid leaves mean the plant is young, or the light is too low — and the new leaves are the ones to watch. Penn State Extension says juvenile leaves are small, green, and mostly entire, and that as the leaves mature they become very large, glossy green, and perforated or cut. The University of Wisconsin Extension says monstera can be grown under fluorescent light but will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate. The University of Connecticut says fenestrations form at the leaf midrib and radiate out, increasing as leaf size progresses. A plant with small, solid leaves is usually either young or under-lit, and the fix is time plus a brighter spot, not fertilizer. Move the plant closer to the window, give it a pole to climb, and wait for the next leaf — because a leaf that unfurls solid will not develop holes later, and the next leaf is the one that matters.

### Leggy, stretched growth

Long, bare stems with leaves only at the ends mean too little light — and the plant is reaching for a window it cannot find. Penn State Extension says the plant will get leggy in lower light. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate. The fix is to move the plant closer to the window first, before you change anything else, and then to prune the bare stems back to a node in spring, so a new shoot grows from the node. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for monstera, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### Sunburn

Pale, bleached patches on the sunny side of the leaves mean the plant was moved into strong sun too fast — and a scorched leaf never recovers its color. The University of Connecticut says the large leaves burn easily, and that it is important to be vigilant when acclimating this plant to new light levels. The University of Wisconsin Extension says container-grown plants can be moved outside for the summer, but they need to be acclimated to higher light levels gradually, or the leaves will suffer sunburn. UF/IFAS Extension says intense sun exposure may cause leaf scorching. The fix is prevention, not treatment: move the plant into brighter light gradually, over two to three weeks, and keep it out of harsh afternoon sun — and that is the sunburn rule in how to care for monstera. A scorched leaf will not heal, but the next leaf will be fine.

### Sweating leaves

Droplets on the leaf tips mean the mix is staying wet too long — and the plant is pushing out water it cannot use. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the leaves will sweat if the growing medium is too moist, and that if this happens, you should reduce watering to prevent root rot. The fix is to stop watering, let the mix dry out, and check that the pot is draining — because a plant that sweats in a pot without holes is a plant that is already rotting. That sweating rule is the watering warning in how to care for monstera.

### Soft, mushy stems

Soft, mushy stems mean root and stem rot, and it is the most serious problem this plant has. The University of Connecticut says root and stem rot shows as wilt and flagging, that stems can become girdled at soil level by rotting tissue, and that affected roots are brown or black with a soft, mushy texture that is easily pulled away from the plant — and that overwatering is more often the cause of these issues in houseplants than drought. The fix is to cut away the rotted stem and roots with a clean knife, repot the healthy part in fresh chunky mix, and water only when the top two inches feel dry. A stem that is mushy all the way through cannot be saved, but a cutting taken above the rot can be — and that is why the node rule matters in how to care for monstera.

### Mealybugs

Mealybugs are the most common insect pest, and they look like white puffs of cotton in the joints of the leaves. The University of Connecticut says mealybugs are a persistent pest of indoor plants, that they feed by sucking plant sap, which can result in yellowing and stunted growth, and in severe cases plant death — and that they can be treated with an insecticidal soap registered for houseplant use, or crushed and removed by hand, or wiped off using a Q-tip. Penn State Extension says to watch for scale, aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, thrips, and fungus gnats. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant has few pests indoors, but may be infested by aphids, mealybugs, scale insects, or spider mites. The fix is to wipe the insects off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and to repeat for several weeks, because the eggs hatch in waves — and that alcohol-swab routine is the pest routine in how to care for monstera. Our guide to [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers the gnats that follow wet soil — and dry soil is the best prevention for those too.

### Spider mites and scale

Spider mites are tiny, and the first sign is fine webbing on the undersides of the leaves. The University of Connecticut says spider mites use a piercing sucking mouth part to drain chlorophyll from leaves, resulting in fine white spots, also called stippling, on the leaves, and that mites can be washed off with water or insecticidal soap, with special attention to the underside of leaves. Scale looks like small brown bumps on the leaves and stems, and the University of Connecticut says scale can be treated with horticultural oil or a spray registered for houseplant use, or crushed and removed by hand, or wiped off using a Q-tip and alcohol. The fix is to check the undersides of the leaves every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the leaf you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back in how to care for monstera.

### Bacterial leaf spot

Dark brown spots on the leaves with a yellow border mean bacterial leaf spot, and it is the one disease the sources name for this plant. The University of Connecticut says bacterial leaf spot shows as dark brown spots on leaves with a yellow border, that the spots tend to be similar in size, and that they often exhibit a sticky ooze — and that because bacteria are spread by water, dry environmental conditions help slow disease transmission, and chemicals are not recommended to manage it. The fix is to avoid wetting the leaves, to increase airflow, and to remove the spotted leaves — and to keep the plant warm and uncrowded, because the University of Connecticut says to avoid low temperatures and overcrowding plants. That dry-leaves rule is the disease rule in how to care for monstera.

## Is monstera poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for monstera, because the plant is so common that it sits within reach of pets in millions of homes. [The ASPCA lists the Swiss cheese plant, Monstera deliciosa, as toxic to dogs and to cats](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/swiss-cheese-plant), with the toxic principle given as insoluble calcium oxalates and the clinical signs given as oral irritation, intense burning and irritation of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. Penn State Extension says all parts of the monstera plant are toxic to humans and pets if ingested, with the exception of the fully ripe fruit, which is rarely seen in a houseplant. The University of Wisconsin Extension says the plant contains oxalic acid, so all parts are poisonous except the ripe fruits. UF/IFAS Extension says all plant parts contain high levels of oxalic acid.

### The riskiest climber on the living-room floor

The risk is not the height — it is the habit. A monstera grows at floor level, with large leaves that hang within reach of a cat or a dog, and a dog that chews a leaf gets a mouthful of glass-like calcium oxalate crystals. The ASPCA says the signs are oral irritation, intense burning of the mouth, tongue and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. The plant is not usually life-threatening, but it is painful, and a pet that chews a leaf needs a veterinarian. Keep the plant where pets cannot chew it — on a tall stand, or in a room the pets do not enter — and keep the cuttings out of reach too, because a cutting on the floor is a leaf a dog can reach.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

The safest houseplants for a home with pets are the ones that are not toxic at all, and the houseplant-care series on this site covers the whole range. The [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/) is non-toxic to cats and dogs, the [aloe vera](/blog/how-to-care-for-aloe-vera/) is toxic if eaten, the [peace lily](/blog/how-to-care-for-peace-lily/) is toxic if chewed, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, and the [jade plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-jade-plant/) is toxic if eaten — and the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is the safest flowering plant on the list. A monstera is not the plant for a home where a pet chews leaves, and a spider plant is the better choice for that room.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap can irritate skin, and the gloves matter. Penn State Extension recommends wearing gloves when handling the plant because of the possibility of contact dermatitis, and the toxic agent is calcium oxalate crystals — oxalic acid. The University of Wisconsin Extension says young fruit contains enough glass-like calcium oxalate crystals to cause immediate and painful irritation to the throat. Wash your hands after pruning, wear gloves when you take cuttings, and keep the cuttings away from children — because the crystals are in every part of the plant, and the ripe fruit is the only part that is not poisonous, and a houseplant almost never makes one. If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any monstera and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435.

a member of the Araceae family, formerly classified in the genus Philodendron; has glossy, heart-shaped or rounded, leathery leaves that develop deep clefts and oblong perforations as they grow; leaves may be as much as 18 inches wide on foot-long leafstalks; cultivars 'Variegata' and 'Albovariegata' have variegated foliage and the leaves tend to be somewhat smaller than the species; in nature an evergreen liana, a trailing or climbing epiphytic vine, which grows high into the rainforest canopy; can grow 70 feet or more and rarely branches; heavy, cylindrical, 2½ to 3 inch stems rough with leaf scars; produces numerous, long, tentacle-like aerial roots as it grows upward which attach to nearby branches and tree trunks; young plants appear very different — seedlings grow toward the darkest area until they encounter a tree, then grow upwards; leaves are small and without lobes or holes, growing closely overlapping each other up the tree trunk in a stage called shingle plants; as plants grow older they develop the characteristic foliage of a mature plant; fenestrations theoretically are a way of allowing high winds to pass through the large leaves without tearing them; flowers rarely if ever seen on houseplants, 8 to 12 inches long, creamy-white, Jack-in-the-pulpit type; takes a little over a year for the fruit to mature, swelling into a 9 inch cone-like structure; edible fruits, called cerimans or monsteras, reportedly taste like a combination of banana, pineapple, and mango and are high in potassium and Vitamin C; fruit ripens from the bottom up; the plant contains oxalic acid, so all parts are poisonous except the ripe fruits; young fruit contains enough glass-like calcium oxalate crystals to cause immediate and painful irritation to the throat; as a houseplant does best in bright light in summer and direct sun in winter; can be grown under fluorescent light, but will not develop the leaf perforations when light is inadequate; prefers warm room temperature and medium to high humidity, but is fairly tolerant of a wide range of conditions once acclimated; plants do not grow below 50°F, and frost will kill them; grow in a fertile soilless mix, with ample root room to promote larger leaf growth; very vigorous growers and need support to keep the stems from breaking; provide a tree bark or strong, moss-covered support sunk into the pot for the aerial roots to attach to; water thoroughly and allow the growing medium to dry slightly between waterings, being sure to also water the moss-covered support so the aerial roots can obtain water and nutrients; leaves will sweat if the growing medium is too moist — reduce watering to prevent root rot; plants kept on the dry side grow slower; water less in winter; fertilize regularly from spring until fall; leaf edges will turn brown if humidity is too low; wipe the dust from the leaves periodically; few pests indoors, but may be infested by aphids, mealybugs, scale insects, or spider mites; container-grown plants need frequent repotting to accommodate the root system; can be moved outside for the summer, but need to be acclimated to higher light levels gradually or the leaves will suffer sunburn; propagate from stem cuttings from mature plants or by air layering or simple layering any time of the year; cut the tip of the stem just below an aerial root and pot the cutting; for more plants, cut the vine into 1-foot sections, press the sections halfway into the surface of a bed of rooting medium and transplant when roots have developed; plants can be grown from seed, but seedlings require warm temperatures and are slow to develop.)

common diseases and disorders: bacterial leaf spot — dark brown spots on leaves with a yellow border, spots tend to be similar in size, often exhibit a sticky ooze, bacteria are spread by water, dry environmental conditions help slow disease transmission, chemicals are not recommended; anthracnose — chlorosis along leaf edges progressing to tan and eventually dark brown, can spread inward and kill whole leaves, avoid misting and wounding leaves; root and stem rot — leaves and stems show wilt and flagging, stems can become girdled at soil level by rotting tissue, affected roots are brown or black with a soft, mushy texture easily pulled away, correct watering procedures are essential, drought stress can damage root tissue leaving it vulnerable, though overwatering is more often the cause; repot plants only when roots have reached the bottom and side of the pot, and incrementally increase in pot size; monsteras are much more tolerant of being pot bound than over potted.)

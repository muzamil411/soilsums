The best time to overseed a lawn is **late summer to early autumn** for cool-season grasses —
roughly six to eight weeks before your first hard frost. The soil is still warm so germination is
fast, nights are cooling so seedlings are not heat-stressed, and annual weeds are finishing
rather than starting. Warm-season grasses want late spring instead.

## Timing by grass type

| Grass type               | Species                                                | Best window                   | Soil temperature  |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Cool-season              | Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue, fine fescue, ryegrass | Late summer to early autumn   | 50–65°F (10–18°C) |
| Cool-season, second best | as above                                               | Early spring                  | as above          |
| Warm-season              | Bermudagrass, zoysia, centipede, bahia, buffalograss   | Late spring into early summer | 65°F+ (18°C+)     |

Six to eight weeks before your first hard frost is the rule of thumb for autumn seeding. New
seedlings need that long to root well enough to survive winter.

Overseeding in midsummer means fighting heat and weeds at once, and it usually loses. Overseeding
too late in autumn means seedlings that germinate and then die over winter.

A soil thermometer is worth more here than a calendar, because the right week varies by several
weeks between a cold spring and a mild one.

## Do you need to rake before overseeding?

Yes — this is the step that decides whether it works.

Grass seed needs contact with soil, not with thatch, and a dense established lawn is very good at
preventing that. Seed lying on top dries out, gets eaten by birds, or germinates and dies before
its root reaches anything.

In order of effort:

1. **Mow short** — about an inch to an inch and a half — and remove the clippings.
2. **Dethatch or rake hard.** If the thatch layer is over half an inch, scarify it. A stiff rake
   is enough on a thinner lawn, and the goal is visible soil between the grass plants.
3. **Aerate if the soil is compacted.** Core aeration pulls plugs out and leaves holes seed can
   fall into. This is optional on loose soil and important on clay or a lawn that gets walked on.
4. **Spread the seed**, half in one direction and half at right angles.
5. **Rake lightly or roll**, so seed settles into the surface rather than sitting on it.
6. **Top-dress thinly** with compost or fine soil if the surface is uneven — no more than a
   quarter inch, or you bury the existing grass.

The two most common reasons overseeding fails are skipping step 2 and skipping step 6.

## How much seed

Overseeding uses roughly half to two-thirds of the rate for a new lawn from bare soil, because
you are filling gaps rather than covering ground. Sowing a full new-lawn rate into existing grass
mostly wastes seed: the seedlings compete with the established plants and with each other.

The [grass seed calculator](/tools/grass-seed-calculator/) scales the rate for your species and
lawn area. It also does something worth knowing about: **for eight of the eleven common species
it could find no extension source publishing an overseeding rate at all**, so it marks those
figures as estimates rather than presenting them as checked. Only Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue
and perennial ryegrass have a sourced overseeding rate behind them.

Two things the rate alone does not tell you:

**Pure live seed.** Published rates are for bulk seed. Divide by purity × germination — both
printed on the bag — to get the weight you actually need. A bag at 95% purity and 85% germination
needs about 24% more by weight than the rate says.

**Region.** Rates for the same grass differ several-fold between states. Buffalograss is 1–2 lb
per 1,000 sq ft in Kansas and 3–5 in Colorado. Check your own extension service before buying for
a large lawn.

## Should you fertilize when overseeding?

A starter fertilizer helps, and nitrogen-heavy lawn feed does not.

New seedlings want phosphorus for root development. A high-nitrogen product pushes the
_established_ grass into growth, which shades out the seedlings you just sowed. Use a starter
formulation, or nothing, and feed properly six weeks later.

This is also the moment to check pH, because it is easier to correct on a thin lawn than a thick
one. Most turf grasses want pH 6.0 to 7.0, and below about 5.5 nutrients already in the soil
become unavailable. See [when to apply lime to garden soil](/blog/when-to-apply-lime-to-garden-soil/)
for the timing and the [lime calculator](/tools/lime-calculator/) for the quantity — note that
Penn State caps a single application to turf at 100 lb per 1,000 sq ft, and Colorado State at 50
lb on established turf.

## Watering, which decides the outcome

Seed must stay damp from sowing until germination. If it dries out once after imbibing water, it
dies.

| Stage                    | Watering                                                 |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sowing to germination    | Light, twice daily — keep the surface damp, never soaked |
| Germination to 1 in tall | Once daily, a little deeper                              |
| 1 in to first mow        | Every other day, deeper again                            |
| After first mow          | Back to normal deep, infrequent watering                 |

Germination takes about 5–10 days for ryegrass, 7–14 for tall fescue and 14–21 for Kentucky
bluegrass. A seed mix containing ryegrass will look successful long before the bluegrass in the
same bag has appeared, which catches people out — do not reseed the gaps too early.

## What not to do

**Do not apply pre-emergent weedkiller.** It stops grass seed germinating exactly as well as weed
seed. Most labels require weeks either side.

**Do not mow until the new grass is about three inches tall**, then use a sharp blade and take no
more than a third of the height.

**Do not walk on it.** Keep traffic off until after the second mow.

**Do not expect it to fix a cause.** Overseeding treats thinness, not the reason for it. If the
lawn thinned because of shade, compaction, drainage or dog traffic, the new grass will thin the
same way. Fix the cause first, then overseed.

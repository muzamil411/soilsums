Every composting guide tells you to balance greens and browns. Far fewer explain what the words
mean or why the balance matters, which makes the advice hard to apply to anything not on the
example list.

## What the words mean

They are shorthand for **nitrogen-rich** and **carbon-rich**.

**Greens** are relatively high in nitrogen: grass clippings, vegetable scraps, coffee grounds,
manure, fresh weeds, seaweed. They are usually moist and they usually rot fast.

**Browns** are relatively high in carbon: dry leaves, straw, cardboard, paper, sawdust, woody
trimmings, pine needles. They are usually dry and they usually rot slowly.

The colour association is a convenient coincidence, not the definition — which is why coffee
grounds are a green and why fresh green shrub trimmings sit closer to the brown end than their
appearance suggests.

## Why the balance matters

The microbes doing the work need carbon for energy and nitrogen to build proteins, in roughly a
25 or 30 to 1 ratio by mass. Give them that and the population explodes, the pile heats up, and
the material breaks down in weeks.

**Too much carbon** and they run short of nitrogen. The population stays small, the pile never
warms, and decomposition crawls along at whatever rate the limited nitrogen allows. It still
works. It takes a year.

**Too much nitrogen** and there is no carbon to build it into. The surplus nitrogen leaves as
ammonia gas, which is the smell, and you have lost the most valuable thing you were trying to
keep. Meanwhile the wet, dense material excludes air and the pile turns sour.

## The volume rule, and its limits

"Two parts brown to one part green by volume" is the standard advice and it is a reasonable
starting point. It works because typical browns are light, dry and bulky while typical greens are
heavy and wet — so two buckets of leaves and one of grass happens to land near the right mass
ratio.

It falls apart with unusual materials, in both directions:

- **Three buckets of sawdust and one of grass** is nowhere near balanced. Sawdust is around 500 to
  1 and dense; that pile is massively carbon-heavy.
- **Two buckets of straw and one of chicken manure** is also unbalanced, the other way. Chicken
  manure is around 7 to 1 and heavy; straw is light.

So the rule is a proxy that works for the common case and misleads for the uncommon one.

## Why you cannot average the ratios

This is the part that trips up even careful gardeners, and it is worth being precise about.

A ratio is not a quantity. Take equal weights of sawdust at 500:1 and coffee grounds at 20:1.
Averaging the two ratios gives 260:1, which sounds hopeless. But work it from actual masses: the
sawdust brings a lot of carbon and almost no nitrogen, while the coffee grounds bring ten times
as much nitrogen as the sawdust does. The mix comes out around **62:1** — still carbon-heavy, but
nowhere near the averaged figure.

The correct calculation goes through masses:

```
dry mass = as-is mass x dry matter %
nitrogen = dry mass x nitrogen % of dry matter
carbon   = nitrogen x that material's C:N ratio
mix C:N  = total carbon / total nitrogen
```

That is more arithmetic than anyone wants to do at the compost bin, which is what the
[compost ratio calculator](/tools/compost-ratio-calculator/) is for. It takes buckets or pounds,
converts using each material's bulk density and moisture, and gives the real ratio.

## The moisture confusion

Greens are mostly water, and this catches people out when thinking about quantities. Fresh grass
clippings are around 75% water; vegetable scraps around 85%. So a bucket of grass contains far
less dry material, and therefore far less actual nitrogen, than its weight suggests.

This is why balancing a heap of dry leaves needs a genuinely large volume of grass. It is not
that the leaves are unusually carbon-heavy; it is that most of the grass is water.

## Practical habits

**Keep a bag of browns beside the bin.** A stash of shredded autumn leaves or torn cardboard
means you can throw a handful on after every kitchen addition, which keeps the balance roughly
right without any calculation.

**Shred the browns.** Whole leaves mat; shredded leaves compost. A mower run over a pile does it.

**Mix rather than layer.** Neat layers look tidy and compost unevenly.

**Add greens in thin layers.** An inch of fresh grass clippings at a time, mixed in, rather than a
six-inch mat that turns to slime.

## The short version

Greens are nitrogen, browns are carbon, and the colour is a coincidence. Aim for 25 to 30 parts
carbon to one part nitrogen by mass. Two parts brown to one green by volume gets you close with
ordinary materials and misleads with unusual ones. Never average the ratios — work from masses,
or let the calculator do it.

**Coffee grounds are a green**, not a brown — despite the colour. Greens and browns are shorthand
for nitrogen-rich and carbon-rich, and coffee grounds run about 20 parts carbon to one part
nitrogen, the same as grass clippings. Cardboard, at around 580:1, is one of the most
carbon-heavy browns you can add.

## What the words actually mean

They are shorthand for **nitrogen-rich** and **carbon-rich**. The colour association is a
convenient coincidence, not the definition.

**Greens** are relatively high in nitrogen: grass clippings, vegetable scraps, coffee grounds,
manure, fresh weeds, seaweed. Usually moist, usually quick to rot.

**Browns** are relatively high in carbon: dry leaves, straw, cardboard, paper, sawdust, woody
trimmings, pine needles. Usually dry, usually slow to rot.

This is why coffee grounds are a green and why fresh, genuinely green shrub trimmings sit closer
to the brown end than their appearance suggests.

## Is cardboard a brown in compost?

Yes — and a much stronger one than most people assume. Plain corrugated cardboard runs about
580 parts carbon to one part nitrogen. A pile wants to land near 30:1, so cardboard on its own is
nearly twenty times too carbon-rich.

That does not make it a bad material. It makes it one that needs a lot of green alongside it, and
it explains the very common outcome of a bin fed mostly on cardboard and kitchen scraps that
never seems to heat up.

at 7:1 is a completely different material from chicken manure with litter at 15:1**; the bedding
does most of the work.

## Why the balance matters

The microbes doing the work need carbon for energy and nitrogen to build proteins, in roughly a
25 or 30 to 1 ratio by mass. Give them that and the population explodes, the pile heats, and the
material breaks down in weeks.

**Too much carbon** and they run short of nitrogen. The population stays small, the pile never
warms, and decomposition crawls along at whatever rate the limited nitrogen allows. It still
works — it takes a year.

**Too much nitrogen** and there is no carbon to build it into. The surplus leaves as ammonia gas,
which is the smell and a straight loss of what you were trying to keep. Meanwhile the wet, dense
material excludes air and the pile turns sour.

[Why is my compost not breaking down](/blog/why-is-my-compost-not-breaking-down/) works through
diagnosing which of those you have.

## The two-to-one rule, and where it breaks

"Two parts brown to one part green by volume" is the standard advice and a reasonable starting
point. It works because typical browns are light, dry and bulky while typical greens are heavy
and wet — so two buckets of leaves and one of grass happens to land near the right mass ratio.

It falls apart with unusual materials, in both directions:

- **Three buckets of sawdust to one of grass** is nowhere near balanced. Sawdust is around 500:1
  and dense; that pile is massively carbon-heavy.
- **Two buckets of straw to one of fresh chicken manure** is unbalanced the other way. Chicken
  manure is around 7:1 and heavy; straw is light.

So the rule is a proxy that works for the common case and misleads for the uncommon one.

## Why you cannot average the ratios

This trips up even careful gardeners, and it is worth being precise about. A ratio is not a
quantity.

Take **equal weights of sawdust at 500:1 and coffee grounds at 20:1**. Averaging the two ratios
gives 260:1, which sounds hopeless. Work it from actual masses instead: the sawdust brings a lot
of carbon and almost no nitrogen, while the coffee grounds bring roughly twenty times as much
nitrogen as the sawdust does. The mix comes out around **62:1** — still carbon-heavy, but nowhere
near the averaged figure.

The correct calculation goes through masses:

```
dry mass = as-is mass x dry matter %
nitrogen = dry mass x nitrogen % of dry matter
carbon   = nitrogen x that material's C:N ratio
mix C:N  = total carbon / total nitrogen
```

That is more arithmetic than anyone wants to do at the bin, which is what the
[compost ratio calculator](/tools/compost-ratio-calculator/) is for. It takes buckets or pounds,
converts using each material's bulk density and moisture, and gives the real ratio.

## The moisture confusion

Greens are mostly water, and this catches people out on quantities. Fresh grass clippings are
around 75% water; vegetable scraps around 85%. A bucket of grass therefore contains far less dry
material — and far less actual nitrogen — than its weight suggests.

This is why balancing a heap of dry leaves needs a genuinely large volume of grass. It is not
that the leaves are unusually carbon-heavy. It is that most of the grass is water.

## Practical habits

**Keep a bag of browns beside the bin.** Shredded autumn leaves or torn cardboard, so you can
throw a handful on after every kitchen addition and keep the balance roughly right without
calculating anything.

**Shred the browns.** Whole leaves mat; shredded leaves compost. A mower run over a pile does it.

**Mix rather than layer.** Neat layers look tidy and compost unevenly.

**Add greens in thin layers.** An inch of fresh grass clippings at a time, mixed in, rather than a
six-inch mat that turns to slime.

Finished compost is the 30% in [what to fill raised garden beds with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/),
and what keeps a bed from slumping year on year.

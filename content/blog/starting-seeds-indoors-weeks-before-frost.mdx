Start seeds indoors **3 weeks before your last frost for squash and cucumbers, 6 for tomatoes, 8
for peppers, eggplant, lettuce and most brassicas, and 12 for onions**. Those counts exist
because a seedling should spend a set time indoors — roughly 3 to 10 weeks — not because earlier
is better. Starting early produces worse plants.

## Seed starting schedule by weeks before last frost

Every indoor sowing date is expressed as weeks before your last spring frost. Find that date
first — [what does last frost date mean](/blog/what-does-last-frost-date-mean/) explains where to
look it up and why it is a probability rather than a promise — then count back.

## Which seeds should not be started indoors

Several crops are worse for it, not better.

Cilantro and dill are the two most often started indoors by mistake. Both have taproots that

## Wait for the temperature as well as the date

For warm-season crops the calendar can say go while the soil is still too cold for anything to
happen. Transplant and direct-sow dates both need a second condition:

| Crop                               | Also wait for                                                   |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pea                                | Soil at 45°F (7°C)                                              |
| Sweet corn                         | Soil about 60°F (16°C), or 65°F (18°C) for supersweet varieties |
| Cucumber, summer and winter squash | Soil about 70°F (21°C)                                          |
| Watermelon                         | Soil above 65°F (18°C)                                          |
| Pepper, eggplant                   | Night temperatures reliably above 50°F (10°C)                   |
| Sweet potato                       | Soil thoroughly warm, not merely frost-free                     |

A cheap soil thermometer settles this in seconds. A pepper set out into cold nights is checked
for weeks and never quite catches up — our [pepper guide](/crops/pepper/) carries the same
condition.

## What happens if you start seeds too early

This deserves its own heading because it is the mistake almost everyone makes in their first
year.

The offsets above are not conservative. A tomato sown ten weeks out instead of six spends a month
outgrowing its pot and arrives at planting day tall, pale and root-bound. A plant sown on time
overtakes it within a fortnight of going out.

What goes wrong, in order:

1. The seedling fills its cell and becomes root-bound.
2. Light indoors is far weaker than outdoors, so it stretches.
3. It needs potting on, which you did not plan for.
4. The weather turns and it waits another two weeks.
5. It goes out stressed and takes weeks to recover.

If you are impatient in February, sow something that genuinely wants sowing in February — onions,
or a tray of salad leaves to eat indoors.

## The four things that matter once they are up

**Light, more than anything.** This is the difference between stocky seedlings and stretched
ones. A south-facing windowsill in early spring provides far less light than it appears to, and
seedlings respond by reaching. A basic shop light or LED bar two to three inches above the
plants, raised as they grow, for fourteen to sixteen hours a day, transforms results. If you buy
one thing, buy this.

**Warmth to germinate, then less.** Warm-season seeds germinate much faster with gentle bottom
heat — peppers in particular can take three weeks in a cool room. Once they are up, reduce it.
Warm plus dim is exactly the combination that produces leggy seedlings.

**A fine, free-draining seed mix.** Not garden soil, which compacts and carries disease.

**Air movement.** A small fan on low for a few hours a day thickens stems and reduces
damping-off. Brushing the tops with your hand daily does something similar.

## Hardening off

Indoor plants have thin leaves and no wind conditioning. Moving them straight outside scorches
them.

Over about a week: an hour in shade on day one, two hours the next, building to a full day in
sun and then a night outside. Bring them in if frost threatens. Then plant, ideally on a dull or
drizzly day rather than in bright sun.

Once they are out, [plant spacing](/tools/plant-spacing-calculator/) decides how many the bed
holds, and [how often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers feeding from there.

      Do marigolds keep pests away?
    answer: >-

Companion planting is one of the more enthusiastically repeated ideas in gardening, and one of
the least critically examined. Some of it works, for reasons that are perfectly clear. Some of it
is folklore that has been copied from list to list for decades. This tries to separate the two
honestly.

## The mechanisms that actually exist

When companion planting works, it is usually one of these five things.

**Physical support.** Beans climb corn. Peas climb anything. This is not subtle and it is not
disputed — one plant provides a structure the other needs.

**Shade and living mulch.** A sprawling squash shades the soil at the base of taller crops,
keeping it cooler and damper and suppressing weeds. This is the real mechanism behind the
traditional corn, beans and squash planting.

**Nitrogen fixing.** Beans and peas host bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form
plants can use. The effect on a neighbouring plant in the same season is modest — most of the
nitrogen stays in the legume until it is turned in — but over a rotation it is real.

**Attracting beneficial insects.** Flowering plants with small open flowers — dill, fennel,
cilantro when it bolts, alyssum, yarrow — bring in hoverflies, lacewings and parasitic wasps,
whose larvae eat aphids. This is well supported and probably the most useful thing on the list
for a vegetable garden.

**Trap cropping.** A plant that a pest prefers, planted to draw it away from the crop. Nasturtiums
for blackfly is the classic example. It works, with the caveat that you now have a plant covered
in blackfly and have to deal with it.

## What the evidence is thinner on

**Repelling pests by smell.** The idea that strongly scented herbs mask or repel pests is
appealing and mostly unsupported at garden scale. Confusing an insect's search pattern with dense
interplanting has some support; a basil plant next to a tomato repelling anything does not.

**Improving flavour.** Basil making tomatoes taste better is a lovely idea with no mechanism
behind it. They ripen at the same time and go well together on a plate, which is reason enough.

**Long lists of specific antagonisms.** Most published "never plant X with Y" tables trace back
to a small number of early-twentieth-century books rather than to trials. The exceptions worth
respecting are below.

## The avoid-list that holds up

**Same family, same bed, year after year.** Tomatoes and potatoes share blight. Brassicas share
clubroot. Onion family crops share white rot. This is about rotation and it matters a great
deal more than any pairing.

**Fennel.** Genuinely inhibits the growth of many neighbouring plants. Grow it in its own corner.

**Tall next to short, on the sunny side.** In the northern hemisphere, corn or staked tomatoes on
the south side of a bed will shade everything behind them from midsummer. This is the most
common real "bad companion" in a garden and it is about geometry, not chemistry.

**Heavy feeders together.** Two hungry crops in the same square foot compete. Brassicas and corn
in adjacent squares will both be disappointing.

## A practical chart

| Crop          | Grows well with                   | Keep apart from          |
| ------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Tomato        | Basil, parsley, onion, marigold   | Potato, corn, fennel     |
| Pepper        | Basil, onion, carrot, spinach     | Fennel                   |
| Cucumber      | Bean, corn, radish, dill          | Potato, sage             |
| Zucchini      | Corn, bean, nasturtium            | Potato                   |
| Lettuce       | Carrot, radish, onion, strawberry | Broccoli                 |
| Carrot        | Onion, leek, lettuce, rosemary    | Dill, parsnip            |
| Bean          | Corn, cucumber, squash, marigold  | Onion, garlic, fennel    |
| Pea           | Carrot, radish, cucumber, spinach | Onion, garlic            |
| Brassicas     | Onion, beet, dill, nasturtium     | Tomato, strawberry       |
| Onion, garlic | Carrot, beet, lettuce, tomato     | Bean, pea                |
| Potato        | Bean, corn, marigold              | Tomato, cucumber, squash |
| Corn          | Bean, squash, cucumber            | Tomato                   |

Read this as a planning convenience rather than a set of rules. The "keep apart" column mixes
well-supported cases — brassicas and strawberries, onions and beans — with traditional lore. If
a pairing you want to try is on it, try it; the downside is small.

## What to do instead, if you want results

The things that reliably improve a vegetable garden are duller than companion planting and work
much better:

- **Rotate families** so soil-borne diseases do not build up.
- **Space plants properly** so air moves between them. Crowding is the single most common cause
  of mildew and blight in a home garden.
- **Grow flowers among the vegetables** — dill, cilantro, alyssum, calendula — to feed beneficial
  insects. This is the one companion planting idea worth taking seriously.
- **Mulch and water consistently.**
- **Get a soil test** and feed what is actually short.

## Interplanting, which is different and genuinely useful

Worth separating from companion planting proper. Interplanting is putting a fast crop between
slow ones and harvesting it before the slow crop needs the room: radishes between carrots,
lettuce between young brassicas, spinach under sweetcorn. There is no claimed chemistry here,
just the use of space and time, and it reliably increases what a bed produces.

## The short version

Use companion planting where the mechanism is obvious: support, shade, flowers for beneficial
insects, trap crops. Respect family rotation and the fennel exception. Ignore the flavour claims.
Then spend the effort you saved on spacing, watering and a soil test, which is where the yield
actually comes from.

Paperwhites are the easiest bulb you can force indoors, and the question of how to force paperwhite bulbs is really a question about timing, not technique. A paperwhite needs no cold storage, no special equipment, and no fertilizer — set the bulb in a bowl of pebbles with water touching its base, or tuck it into a pot of potting mix, and it blooms in a few weeks. That simplicity is why the bulbs show up in shops every November, and why the same bowl of white flowers has become the default holiday centerpiece for gardeners who own nothing more than a windowsill. This guide to how to force paperwhite bulbs follows the extension services that publish the method: which bulbs to buy, the two ways to plant them, the dates that land blooms on Christmas Day, the Cornell alcohol trick that stops the stems from flopping, and what to do with the bulbs when the flowers fade.

## Why paperwhites are the easiest bulb to force

Most spring bulbs need a cold spell before they will flower indoors, and that is what makes forcing them a project. Tulips, daffodils, hyacinths, and crocuses all need weeks of chilling — usually in a refrigerator or a cold garage — before they will bloom, and our [tulip planting guide](/blog/when-to-plant-tulip-bulbs/) covers that cold requirement for the bulbs that go in the ground. Paperwhites skip that step entirely. Illinois Extension says paperwhites do not require a chilling period, and Iowa State University Extension says the same in the sharpest terms: unlike tulips, daffodils, hyacinths, and crocuses, paperwhite narcissus do not require a cold storage period to flower. The University of Florida IFAS Extension calls paperwhite narcissus one of the easiest bulbs to force inside for exactly that reason, and Cornell University agrees — unlike most other daffodils, paperwhites do not require a cold period. That one fact is the whole reason how to force paperwhite bulbs is a beginner project: the bulb arrives ready to grow, and the only things it needs are water, light, and a few weeks.

### Why no chilling is needed

The reason is where the plant comes from. Paperwhites are native to the Mediterranean, where winters are mild, and Illinois Extension points out that they have no chilling requirement because their home climate never gave them one. The common name usually means *Narcissus papyraceus*, although *Narcissus tazetta* also goes by the same name, per Illinois Extension — both are relatives of the daffodil, and the family resemblance is easy to see in the flower: five petals around a showy cup, the bowl-shaped center also known as the corona. The other side of that Mediterranean origin is the limit on the plant. Illinois Extension says paperwhites are only hardy to Zone 8, so in most of the United States they are considered an annual and are not suitable for planting outdoors. A second Illinois Extension guide puts the outdoor range at zones 8 to 11. In colder regions, the plant lives indoors for a few weeks and then it is done — and that is fine, because the indoor show is the point. That hardiness limit is the first thing to understand about how to force paperwhite bulbs: you are growing a short-lived holiday display, not a perennial, and the bulb is spent when the flowers fade.

### What the display looks like

What you get for those few weeks is a real display, and it is the payoff of how to force paperwhite bulbs. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says the extremely fragrant flowers grow on stems 12 to 18 inches tall, in clusters of small white, yellow, or orange flowers — despite the common name, paperwhites are not always white. Iowa State University Extension says the plants may reach 18 to 20 inches, and North Carolina State Extension says the plant typically grows to 18 inches tall outdoors, but has been known to reach 30 inches. ### The scent is the one thing to check

The flowers are strongly scented, and the scent is the one thing about the plant that divides people. Illinois Extension says some folks detest the strong scent of paperwhite blooms, and compares the love-it-or-hate-it reaction to cilantro: some love it and some hate it. The University of Florida IFAS Extension adds that the yellow flowers are particularly fragrant. If you have never grown paperwhites, put the first bowl somewhere you can move it — a strongly scented plant in a small, stuffy room is a lot of fragrance, and the scent is the one part of how to force paperwhite bulbs that no extension service can settle for you.

## Which paperwhite bulbs to buy

Buy the bulbs the way the extension services tell you to buy them: firm, and free of blemishes. Illinois Extension says to select firm bulbs that look healthy, and to avoid bulbs with sunken spots or discoloration. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says the same — look for bulbs that are blemish free and firm — and gives the reason: blemished bulbs can carry basal rot, a decay that starts at the roots and spreads upwards inside the bulb, leaving the plant dwarfed with abnormal flowers. Since no chilling period is required, some bulbs may already be sprouting in the store, and Illinois Extension says that if possible, you should try to find bulbs with no growth or a minimal amount. A bulb that has already thrown a long, pale shoot in a warm shop has spent some of its stored energy before you even get it home. Pick the firm, quiet bulbs, and leave the sprouted ones on the shelf. That selection step is the cheapest insurance in how to force paperwhite bulbs: a healthy bulb forgives a lot of beginner mistakes, and a rotting one forgives none.

### The varieties, and the one that smells different

The variety matters less than the health of the bulb, but the varieties do differ — and choosing one is the one shopping decision in how to force paperwhite bulbs that changes the scent. Iowa State University Extension lists the commonly grown varieties: 'Ziva', with pure white flowers, is the one you are most likely to find in shops, and Illinois Extension calls it the most widely grown; 'Bethlehem' has creamy white petals with a yellow cup; 'Galilee' has pure white flowers; 'Israel' has creamy yellow petals with a sulfur yellow cup; 'Jerusalem' has pure white flowers; 'Nazareth' has soft yellow petals with a bright yellow cup; and 'Grand Soleil d'Or' has yellow petals with an orange cup. 'Grand Soleil d'Or' is the one variety with a different timetable: Iowa State University Extension says it takes one to two weeks longer to force than other paperwhites, and does not bloom as heavily — but it has a sweet, fruity fragrance, while all the other listed varieties have a musky fragrance. The University of Florida IFAS Extension adds three more heavily fragrant varieties: 'Erlicheer' and 'Omri', which produce completely white flowers, and 'Avalanche', with white petals and yellow central cups, plus 'Constantinople', with semi-double white petals. If the scent worries you, 'Grand Soleil d'Or' is the variety to try — its sweet, fruity fragrance is the exception to the musky rule, and that difference is the variety note inside how to force paperwhite bulbs that actually changes the experience of growing them.

### No fertilizer needed

One more thing the bulb does not need: fertilizer. Illinois Extension says paperwhite bulbs can be planted in a standard soilless potting mix, needing no additional fertilizer or nutrients, because they come already packed with everything needed, safely stored inside the bulb structure. The same applies in water: the bulb carries its own food, and the water is only there to let the roots grow. Do not feed the bulbs, and do not add fertilizer to the water — there is nothing in the extension guidance that calls for it, and the bulb has already done the storing, which is why feeding plays no part in how to force paperwhite bulbs. That no-feeding rule is the simplest part of how to force paperwhite bulbs, and the one most often ignored by gardeners who assume every plant wants feeding.

## When to plant paperwhites for Christmas blooms

Timing is the one part of how to force paperwhite bulbs that rewards a calendar. The extension services do not all give the same number of weeks, and the spread is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. Illinois Extension says to allow four to six weeks for the bulbs to bloom, and that the flowers open about a month after planting. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says full flowering takes about four to eight weeks after planting. Cornell University says the white, fragrant flowers usually open within two to three weeks of planting. Those numbers describe slightly different moments — first flowers versus full flowering — and a warm room gets there faster than a cool one, but the practical range for planning is four to six weeks, which is the Illinois figure and the middle of the spread. Count back from the date you want blooms, and plant then.

### Plant November 13 to 27 for Christmas Day

For Christmas Day, that arithmetic lands in mid-to-late November. Four to six weeks before December 25 is November 13 to November 27, so a bulb planted in that window should be in flower for Christmas — and a bulb planted today, November 11, should flower between December 9 and December 23, just ahead of the holiday. That is why the bulbs are in the shops now: mid-November is the planting window, not the buying window, and a bulb bought in December is a bulb that blooms in January. If you want flowers for New Year as well, plant a second bowl ten days after the first — Illinois Extension says that if you pot up paperwhite narcissus bulbs every ten days or so starting in the fall, you can have a succession of bloom all through the winter. The succession is the timing trick inside how to force paperwhite bulbs: one bowl is a decoration, and a bowl every ten days is flowers all winter.

### Make the display last

The display itself lasts two to three weeks once it opens, per Illinois Extension, so a bowl that flowers on December 20 is still showing on New Year. When the flowers begin to open, move the plant to the coolest area in your house, out of direct sun, and the bloom lasts longer — Illinois Extension gives that instruction in both of its forcing guides, and Iowa State University Extension says the same: move the paperwhite narcissus from direct sunlight when the plants begin to flower, to prolong their bloom period. A warm, sunny window hurries the flowers along; a cool room slows them down. That cool-room finish is the last timing decision in how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the one that stretches a two-week display into a three-week one.

## How to force paperwhite bulbs in water and pebbles

The water method is the classic way to force paperwhite bulbs, and the one most people picture: a clear bowl, a layer of pebbles, and white roots growing down where you can watch them. Illinois Extension likes the clear vessel for exactly that reason — it allows for easy observation of the rapidly developing roots, and both children and adults marvel at the speed at which roots and shoots develop. Here is the method as the extension services describe it, step by step.

### Step 1: Choose a container 3 to 4 inches deep

Select a clear container, 3 to 4 inches deep, with no drainage holes, per Illinois Extension — a decorative glass bowl, or as simple as a plastic cup. The container needs to be deep enough to hold the pebbles and the roots, and shallow enough that the bulbs sit near the top. A second Illinois Extension guide gives the same depth: select a 3- to 4-inch deep decorative container that does not have drainage holes. The no-drainage rule matters here because the bowl is the reservoir: the water stays in the bowl, and the roots grow down into it. A container with drainage holes defeats the method, because the water runs out. That container choice is the first step in how to force paperwhite bulbs in water, and it is the only step where the water method and the soil method part ways.

### Step 2: Fill with pebbles, then set the bulbs on top

Fill the container three-quarters full of small rocks or colorful marbles, per Illinois Extension, or place 1 to 2 inches of washed gravel or stones in the bottom, per the second Illinois guide. Iowa State University Extension describes the same setup: partially fill the container with washed gravel or stones, place the bulbs on the gravel or stones, then place additional gravel or stones around the bulbs, leaving the tips — the noses — of the bulbs exposed. The University of Florida IFAS Extension gives the same proportions: fill a pot or bowl up halfway with pebbles, place the bulbs on top of the pebbles, then add more pebbles until the bulbs are about two-thirds covered. Set the bulbs close together, about an inch apart, so the bowl looks full when it flowers, and leave the tips free — the growing point must stay out of the gravel and out of the water. Pack the bulbs in tight: a single bulb alone in a wide bowl looks thin, and a crowded bowl supports itself. That crowding is the planting rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs in water.

### Step 3: Add water until it just touches the bulbs

Fill the container with water until it just barely covers the bottom of the bulb, per Illinois Extension. Iowa State University Extension says the same: add water to the bowl until it touches the bottom of the bulbs, and maintain the water at this level throughout the forcing period. The University of Florida IFAS Extension agrees: add enough water so that the bottoms of the bulbs are touched by the water, then maintain this water level. The water level is the whole method, and the rule is simple: the roots grow down into the water, and the bulb itself stays dry. A second Illinois Extension guide states it as a warning: do not immerse the bulbs in water — only the basal plate of the bulb, where the roots originate, should be in water. A bulb sitting in water rots from the base up, and basal rot is the decay the University of Florida IFAS Extension warns about when it tells you to buy firm, blemish-free bulbs. Keep the water at the base of the bulbs, no higher, and top it up as it evaporates or as the roots absorb it. That water level is the maintenance rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs in water, and it is the one to check every day.

### Step 4: Give them a sunny, warm window — or a cool, dark start

Here the extension services split, and the split is worth stating rather than hiding. Illinois Extension puts the bowl straight into a sunny, warm window: place the container in a sunny, warm window, and observe the bulb every day. A second Illinois Extension guide says the same for the start — place the bulbs in a window area with a southern exposure for the best results — while noting that paperwhites will flower under any light conditions. Iowa State University Extension takes the other route: place the planted bulbs in a cool, dark location at 45 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit for two to three weeks to encourage root growth, and when the shoots reach a height of 3 inches, move the plants to a sunny window at 60 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit. The Iowa State cool-dark start grows roots first and shoots second, which gives the plant a better anchor before it grows tall; the Illinois sunny-window start is simpler and works well in a bright house. Either route ends in the same place: a sunny window at 60 to 70 degrees once the shoots are up. Roots and green tips will begin to appear in one to two weeks, per Illinois Extension, and that first green is the sign the method is working. That choice of starts — cool and dark for two to three weeks, or sunny and warm from day one — is the honest fork in how to force paperwhite bulbs in water, and both published routes produce flowers.

### Step 5: Keep the water level steady until they bloom

Replenish the water as it evaporates or as the roots absorb it, per Illinois Extension, and keep it at the level where it just touches the bottom of the bulbs. Do not let the bowl dry out, and do not top it up so high that the bulbs sit in water. The roots grow fast — you can watch them lengthen in a clear bowl day by day — and a bowl that dries out stalls the plant. Check the level every day or two, and keep it steady through the forcing period. When the bulb begins to flower, about a month after planting, move the plant to the coolest area in your house to prolong the bloom, per Illinois Extension. That move — from the sunny window to the coolest room — is the last step in how to force paperwhite bulbs in water, and it is what keeps the flowers showing for two to three weeks instead of fading in one.

## How to force paperwhite bulbs in potting soil

The soil method is the quieter way to force paperwhite bulbs, and the one to choose if you already own pots and potting mix. The flowers are the same, the timing is the same, and the plant is a little more stable in a pot than in a bowl of pebbles — the soil holds the bulb in place, and the pot has drainage holes, so overwatering is harder. Here is the method as the extension services describe it.

### Step 1: Choose a pot 3 to 5 inches deep, with drainage holes

Look for a pot that is 3 to 5 inches deep and has holes in the bottom for drainage, per the University of Florida IFAS Extension, and fill it with a potting mix that is well drained. Illinois Extension adds the reason the depth matters: the container selected must have drain holes and must be able to accommodate at least 2 to 3 inches of root growth, or the blooming stems may be pushed up out of the container as their roots grow downward. A pot that is too shallow leaves the roots nowhere to go, and the growing roots lever the bulbs out of the soil. A second Illinois Extension guide gives the same depth for the soil method: select a pot 3 to 4 inches deep, and plant the bulbs in a well-drained potting mix with the tops of the bulbs even with or slightly below the rim of the pot. Drainage holes are not optional in this method — the soil method depends on water running through, and a pot without holes turns the mix soggy and the bulbs rot. That pot choice is the first step in how to force paperwhite bulbs in soil.

### Step 2: Set the bulbs pointed-end up, tips exposed

Partially fill the container with potting soil, place the bulbs on the soil surface, then add additional potting soil, per Iowa State University Extension — and when potted, the tips of the bulbs should stick above the potting soil. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says the same: plant the bulbs with the pointed ends up, plant enough bulbs in the pot to fill the space without the bulbs touching each other, and make sure the tips of your planted bulbs are exposed. The Iowa State FAQ adds the finishing detail: the level of the soil mix should be half an inch to one inch below the rim of the container. Pack the bulbs close, as in the water method, so they support each other and the pot looks full — but do not let them touch, because bulbs pressed together trap moisture between them. The tips stay out of the soil so the shoots have a clear path up, and the pointed end stays up so the shoot grows up and the roots grow down. That placement is the planting rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs in soil.

### Step 3: Water thoroughly, then keep the soil moist

Water the potting soil thoroughly and then let it drain for a few minutes, per Iowa State University Extension, and keep the potting soil moist throughout the forcing period. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says the same in the other direction: the bulbs need a potting mix that is well drained, and the soil should be kept moist, not soggy. Watering thoroughly at the start settles the mix around the bulbs and starts the roots growing; keeping it moist after that keeps the roots growing without drowning them. No fertilizer is needed at any point — Illinois Extension says the bulbs come already packed with everything needed, safely stored inside the bulb structure. That watering rule — thoroughly once, moist after — is the maintenance rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs in soil, and it is simpler than the water method because the soil holds the moisture for you.

### Step 4: Start cool and dark, then move to a sunny window

Iowa State University Extension gives the soil method its own start: place the planted bulbs in a cool, dark location at 45 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit for two to three weeks to encourage root growth, and when the shoots reach a height of 3 inches, move the plants to a sunny window with a temperature of 60 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit. The cool, dark start is the part of the soil method that most gardeners skip, and it is the part that gives the plant its anchor: two to three weeks of root growth before the shoot takes off means the plant stands on its own roots when it gets tall. Once the shoots are 3 inches tall, the plant wants its sunny window, and the temperature wants to be 60 to 70 degrees — warm enough to grow, cool enough not to stretch. That cool-dark start is the honest difference between the two methods inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the reason the soil method produces the sturdiest plants of the two.

## Light and temperature while paperwhites grow

Light and temperature are the two things that decide whether a paperwhite grows short and sturdy or tall and floppy, and the extension services agree on the direction even when they differ on the numbers. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says to place the planted bulbs in a sunny, cool location, and warns that when grown in an area that is too warm or has too little light, the leaves and flower stalks will grow too tall and flop over — and that too much sun will cause the flowers to deteriorate. Iowa State University Extension gives the window numbers: a sunny window with a temperature of 60 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit once the shoots are 3 inches tall. Illinois Extension says the bulbs will flower under any light conditions, but for the best results, start them in a window with a southern exposure. The shared point is a bright, cool spot — not a hot windowsill, and not a dim corner. A south-facing window in a room that stays in the 60s is the target, and a room in the 70s with weak winter light is the combination that grows the leggiest plants. That bright-and-cool rule is the light rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the cheapest fix for almost every problem the plant develops.

### The 75-degree ceiling

The temperature split between the two methods is worth keeping straight. For the water method, Illinois Extension puts the bowl in a sunny, warm window from the start, and the plant grows fast. For the soil method, Iowa State University Extension starts the bulbs cool and dark at 45 to 50 degrees for two to three weeks, then moves them to a sunny window at 60 to 70 degrees. Cornell University's forcing guide adds a ceiling: place the bulbs in a well-lit location at 60 to 65 degrees Fahrenheit, and warns that temperatures of 75 degrees or more encourage weak growth, and the flowers do not last long. The number to remember is 75: above it, the stems stretch and the flowers fade fast. Below it, in the 60s, the plant grows at a pace its stems can support — and that pace is the growth rate to aim for in how to force paperwhite bulbs. If your house runs warm, the coolest bright room is the right room — and that cool-room choice is the temperature rule inside how to force paperwhite bulbs that keeps the plant standing up.

### When the flowers open, move them

When the flowers open, the rule changes. Move the plant out of direct sunlight and into the coolest area of the house, per Illinois Extension, and the bloom lasts longer. Iowa State University Extension says the same: move the paperwhite narcissus from direct sunlight when the plants begin to flower, to prolong their bloom period. The University of Florida IFAS Extension agrees from the other side: too much sun will cause the flowers to deteriorate. Direct sun on open flowers fades them and shortens the display, while a cool room out of the sun stretches it — and that move is the last light decision in how to force paperwhite bulbs before the flowers fade.

## A worked example: Christmas blooms from two bowls

A worked example makes the timing concrete. Suppose you want paperwhites in flower on Christmas Day, and again for New Year, and you have two clear bowls, a bag of pebbles, and a dozen bulbs. Here is how to force paperwhite bulbs for both dates, using the Illinois timing of four to six weeks.

### Bowl 1: plant on November 11, for Christmas

Plant the first bowl today, November 11. Take a clear bowl 3 to 4 inches deep with no drainage holes, fill it three-quarters full of small washed pebbles, and set six firm 'Ziva' bulbs on top, pointed ends up, about an inch apart, with the tips exposed. Add a little more pebble around the bulbs to hold them in place, then pour in water until it just touches the bottom of the bulbs — no higher. Put the bowl in a sunny, warm window, per Illinois Extension, and top up the water as it evaporates. Roots and green tips should appear in one to two weeks. At four to six weeks from planting, the flowers open — between December 9 and December 23 — and when they do, move the bowl to the coolest area in the house, out of direct sun, to stretch the display through Christmas Day and beyond. That is bowl one: planted November 11, flowering mid-to-late December, and the first half of how to force paperwhite bulbs for the holidays.

### Bowl 2: plant on November 21, for New Year

Plant the second bowl ten days later, on November 21, the same way: same bowl, same pebbles, same six bulbs, same water level. At four to six weeks from planting, its flowers open between December 19 and January 2 — which covers New Year, and overlaps with the end of bowl one's display. The ten-day stagger is the Illinois succession rule: pot up a new batch every ten days or so, and you have a succession of bloom all through the winter. If the first bowl's stems flop — and at 18 to 20 inches, Iowa State University Extension says they often do — try the Cornell alcohol trick on the second bowl, and compare the two: one bowl on plain water, one on the alcohol solution, and the difference in height will be obvious within a few days. That comparison is the experiment inside how to force paperwhite bulbs that Cornell itself suggests, and it is the best way to learn whether the trick is worth doing every year.

### What the two bowls cost

Two bowls, twelve bulbs, a bag of pebbles, and tap water. No fertilizer, no grow light, no chilling, and no special equipment — Illinois Extension says the bulbs need no additional fertilizer, and the water method needs nothing more than a container, some stones, and water. The only running cost is attention: check the water level every day or two, and keep it at the base of the bulbs. That low cost is the practical appeal of how to force paperwhite bulbs: it is the cheapest holiday decoration a gardener can grow, and the only one that grows itself on a windowsill.

## Why paperwhites flop, and the Cornell alcohol trick

Flopping is the one real problem with paperwhites, and every extension service names it. Illinois Extension says the weakness of the stems under the weight of the blooms is perhaps the only downside to these splendid, dainty flowers — they often tend to flop over and may require some kind of support as they reach full size. Iowa State University Extension says the plants may reach a height of 18 to 20 inches and often require staking. The University of Florida IFAS Extension names the cause: when grown in an area that is too warm or has too little light, the leaves and flower stalks will grow too tall and flop over. The plant grows fast, the stems grow tall, and the heavy flower clusters at the top pull the whole thing over. Staking works — a thin bamboo stake and a loose tie hold the stem up — but there is a better fix, and it comes from a Cornell University experiment. That flopping problem is the reason the alcohol trick exists inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and the trick is the most interesting part of the whole method.

### Where the trick came from

The trick began with a question from Leslie Land, a garden columnist at the New York Times, who asked William B. Miller, director of the Flowerbulb Research Program at Cornell University, whether the folk tip of adding gin to the water of forced bulbs really kept them shorter. Miller and Erin Finan, a Cornell horticulture undergraduate working on her senior project, tested it — and it worked. That experiment is the research behind the most-shared tip in how to force paperwhite bulbs. Cornell's publication, "Pickling Your Paperwhites," reports the result: watering paperwhites with a dilute alcohol solution makes the plants one-third to one-half shorter, with flowers just as large, fragrant, and long-lasting as usual. The flowers do not shrink, the scent does not change, and the bloom does not shorten — only the stems and leaves grow less, and the plant stands up on its own without stakes, wires, or other supports. That result — shorter stems, same flowers — is the finding at the heart of how to force paperwhite bulbs without the flop.

### How to do the alcohol trick

Start the bulbs the normal way, in stones and water, and wait about a week until the roots are growing and the shoot is green and growing about 1 to 2 inches above the top of the bulb. That is Cornell's timing, and it matters: the alcohol goes in after the roots have formed, not before. At that point, pour off the water and replace it with a solution of 4 to 6 percent alcohol, made from just about any hard liquor. To get a 5 percent solution from a 40 percent distilled spirit — gin, vodka, whiskey, rum, or tequila — add 1 part of the spirit to 7 parts of water, which is an 8-fold dilution yielding 5 percent alcohol. If you do not have liquor in the house, rubbing alcohol works just as well: since it is usually 70 percent alcohol when purchased, a dilution of 1 part rubbing alcohol to 10 or 11 parts water is appropriate. Then use this solution, instead of water, for all further watering of the bulbs. You will see results within just a few days, per Cornell, and the plant will be nicely proportioned and will not need support stakes. That sequence — wait a week, pour off the water, replace it with 4 to 6 percent alcohol, and keep using it — is the alcohol method inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, step by step.

### The limits: what not to do

Moderation is the key, and Cornell says so directly. Do not use beer or wine, as the sugars in them will cause major problems for the plants. Do not go above 10 percent alcohol: Cornell says that if plants are given much more than 10 percent alcohol, growth problems will start, and 25 percent alcohol is dramatically toxic — so stay well below 10 percent. The higher the alcohol concentration, within reason, the shorter the plants, so it is not critical whether you use 4, 5, or 6 percent — just stay well below 10 percent. Cornell's explanation for why it works is simple water stress: the alcohol makes it more difficult for the plant to absorb water, and the slight shortage is enough to slow leaf and stem growth but not enough to change flower size or how long the flowers last. If you do not pour off the water first and simply add the alcohol to the existing water, the result will be a lower alcohol concentration around the roots, and the growth will not be reduced as much as you expect — so pour the water off, then add the solution. Those limits — no beer or wine, never above 10 percent, pour the water off first — are the safety rules inside how to force paperwhite bulbs with alcohol, and they are the difference between a shorter plant and a damaged one.

### The other fixes, for when alcohol is not the answer

Alcohol is not the only fix, and it is not the right fix for every household — a home with small children or pets may prefer not to have a bowl of alcohol solution on a low table. The other fixes are the ones the extension services give for the same problem. Give the plants as much light as you can, so they do not have to reach for it, and keep them cooler, in the 60s, so they grow less lanky — that is the light-and-temperature fix, and it follows from the University of Florida IFAS Extension's warning that too warm or too dim grows them too tall. Stake the stems when they get tall: Iowa State University Extension says the plants often require staking at 18 to 20 inches, and a thin stake with a loose tie is the simplest support. Start the bulbs cool and dark for two to three weeks, per Iowa State University Extension, so the roots establish before the shoots take off — a plant with a good root anchor flops less than one grown fast in a warm room. And choose the variety with care: 'Grand Soleil d'Or' grows a little slower and blooms a little less heavily, per Iowa State University Extension, which makes it less likely to topple. Any one of those fixes helps; together, they cover the flopping problem in how to force paperwhite bulbs without a drop of alcohol.

## How long the blooms last, and what to do after

A paperwhite display lasts two to three weeks, per Illinois Extension, and the way to reach the long end of that range is the cool-room move: when the bulb begins to flower, move the plant to the coolest area in your house, out of direct sun, and the flowers last longer. Iowa State University Extension says the same — move the paperwhite narcissus from direct sunlight when the plants begin to flower, to prolong their bloom period — and the University of Florida IFAS Extension warns from the other side that too much sun will cause the flowers to deteriorate. A bowl left in a hot, sunny window fades in days; the same bowl in a cool room out of the sun shows for two to three weeks. That move is the last care decision in how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the one that decides how long the display lasts.

When the flowers fade, the bulb is spent. Iowa State University Extension says paperwhite narcissus bulbs should be discarded after flowering: paperwhites cannot be forced again and are not winter-hardy outdoors. Illinois Extension says the same — enjoy the blooms for a couple of weeks, then discard the plant material as with other annual plants. Do not try to save the bulb for next year, and do not try to force it a second time: the bulb has spent everything it stored, and there is nothing left in it to grow another display. Compost the spent bulb and the pebbles go back in the bag for next November. That ending — discard, do not save — is the honest close to how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the reason the bulbs are cheap enough to buy fresh every year.

## Troubleshooting paperwhites

Most paperwhite problems have one of three causes: water at the wrong level, too little light, or a room that is too warm. The fixes are small, but they only work if the cause is named correctly.

### Bulbs rotting in the bowl

A bulb that turns soft and brown at the base, with a sour smell, is rotting — and the cause is almost always water too high. The University of Florida IFAS Extension warns that basal rot starts at the roots and spreads upwards inside the bulb, leaving the plant dwarfed with abnormal flowers, and that buying firm, blemish-free bulbs helps avoid it. Illinois Extension gives the water rule that prevents it: do not immerse the bulbs in water — only the basal plate of the bulb, where the roots originate, should be in water. If a bulb is already rotting, take it out of the bowl before the rot spreads to its neighbors, and check the water level on the rest: it should just touch the bottom of the bulbs, no higher. A bulb with a sunken spot or discoloration at the shop was already on its way to rot, which is why Illinois Extension says to avoid bulbs with sunken spots or discoloration when you buy. That water-level check is the rot fix inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the first thing to check when a bowl goes wrong.

### No blooms, or blooms that never open

A bulb that grows leaves but no flowers has usually been given too much warmth and too little light, or it simply needs more time. Full flowering takes about four to eight weeks after planting, per the University of Florida IFAS Extension, and Illinois Extension says to allow four to six weeks — so a bulb planted three weeks ago with no buds is not a failure, it is a bulb still on schedule. If the leaves are tall and floppy with no buds at all, move the bowl to the brightest, coolest spot you have: the University of Florida IFAS Extension says that too warm or too little light grows the leaves and stalks too tall, and the same conditions delay the flowers. A bulb that never blooms at all may have been a spent or damaged bulb — the 'Grand Soleil d'Or' variety also takes one to two weeks longer to force than other paperwhites, per Iowa State University Extension, so check the variety before you declare the bulb dead. Patience to six weeks, then a brighter, cooler spot, is the bloom fix inside how to force paperwhite bulbs.

### Tall, leggy stems that flop

Leggy stems are the most common complaint, and the cause is warm air plus weak light. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says that when grown in an area that is too warm or has too little light, the leaves and flower stalks will grow too tall and flop over, and Iowa State University Extension says the plants may reach 18 to 20 inches and often require staking. The fix is to move the plant to the brightest window, keep the room in the 60s, and stake the stems if they are already tall. For the next bowl, start the bulbs cool and dark for two to three weeks, per Iowa State University Extension, and try the Cornell alcohol trick once the shoots are 1 to 2 inches tall: a 4 to 6 percent alcohol solution grows the stems one-third to one-half shorter, with flowers just as large. A plant that is leggy in December is not a failed plant; it is a plant that wants more light and a cooler room, and the next bowl is the place to apply the lesson. That brighter-and-cooler move is the legginess fix inside how to force paperwhite bulbs.

### The scent is too strong

The scent is the one problem no fix fully solves, because the flowers are meant to be fragrant. Illinois Extension says some folks detest the strong scent of paperwhite blooms, and compares the reaction to cilantro — some love it and some hate it. The University of Florida IFAS Extension says the yellow flowers are particularly fragrant, and Iowa State University Extension says 'Grand Soleil d'Or' has a sweet, fruity fragrance while the other varieties are musky. The practical fixes are placement and variety: put the bowl in a larger, airier room rather than a small bedroom, move it to the coolest area of the house when it flowers — which also prolongs the bloom — and try 'Grand Soleil d'Or' next time for its sweeter scent. Do not put a flowering bowl in a small, stuffy space, per Illinois Extension, and choose the location in the house strategically. That placement choice is the scent fix inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the only one that works once the flowers are open.

### Mold on the bulbs or the pebbles

Mold on the pebbles or a white fuzz on the bulb usually means the bowl is too wet and too still. Check the water level first: it should just touch the bottom of the bulbs, and the bulbs themselves should stay dry. Rinse the pebbles, wipe the bowl, and set the water back to the right level. Make sure the bowl is not sitting in a cold, airless corner — a bright spot with a little air movement dries the surface between waterings. Do not add anything to the water to fight the mold: the extension guidance calls for plain water, or the alcohol solution for the flopping fix, and nothing else. A bulb that has gone soft and brown underneath the mold is rotting, and it should come out of the bowl. That water-level check is the mold fix inside how to force paperwhite bulbs, and it is the same check that prevents rot.

## Paperwhites, pets, and children

Paperwhites are toxic to pets, and the bowl sits exactly where a curious dog or cat can reach it — on a low table, a windowsill, or a kitchen counter. The ASPCA lists paper white, under the name Narcissus spp., as toxic to dogs, cats, and horses, with lycorine and other alkaloids as the toxic principles. Ohio State University Extension says the same for paper whites: the bulbs are the most toxic part. The ASPCA record gives the signs to watch for as vomiting, salivation, and diarrhea, with large ingestions causing convulsions, low blood pressure, tremors, and cardiac arrhythmias — and the bulbs, which hold the highest concentration of the toxins, are the most poisonous part of the plant. Keep the bulbs, the plants, and the bowl out of reach of pets and small children, and do not leave loose bulbs where a dog can find them: a dog that digs or chews is the animal most likely to eat a whole bulb. If a pet eats any part of the plant, call your veterinarian — and that safety rule is the one non-negotiable part of how to force paperwhite bulbs in a house with animals. The same caution applies to the alcohol solution, if you use the Cornell trick: a bowl of 5 percent alcohol is not drinking water, and it should stay out of reach of children and pets just like the bulbs themselves.

## Planting paperwhites outdoors in mild-winter zones

Not every gardener needs to force paperwhites indoors. Illinois Extension says paperwhites are only hardy to Zone 8, and a second Illinois Extension guide puts their outdoor range at zones 8 to 11 — the warm end of the country, where frost is rare or absent. If you garden in those zones, the bulbs can go into the ground instead of a bowl. North Carolina State Extension gives the outdoor planting method: plant in the fall, six weeks before the first freeze, at a depth that is two to three times the size of the bulb but at least three inches deep, spacing bulbs four to ten inches apart, with larger bulbs further apart than smaller ones. The plant does best in organically rich, medium moisture, well-drained soil in full sun to part shade, and it tolerates heat but not extremely wet conditions. North Carolina State Extension also notes that the plant is pest and disease free due to its poisonous alkaloid content — though slugs, snails, and bulb mites still watch for it, and bulb rot can occur in wet soils. In those mild zones, the bulbs naturalize and spread, and the flowers open in early to mid-spring. Everywhere colder, the outdoor question does not arise: the bulb is forced indoors and discarded after flowering, per Iowa State University Extension. If you are weighing the outdoor option against the indoor one, our [winterizing guide](/blog/how-to-winterize-a-garden/) covers the rest of the November work, including which tender bulbs come inside before the cold arrives — and our [seed-starting guide](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/) covers the indoor setup for the rest of the garden while the paperwhites bloom on the windowsill.

## The short version

How to force paperwhite bulbs comes down to three decisions made in mid-November: which bulbs are firm enough to buy, which of the two methods fits what you own, and which date you want the flowers for. A firm 'Ziva' bulb in a clear bowl of pebbles, with the water just touching its base, in a sunny window at 60 to 70 degrees, blooms in four to six weeks — plant November 13 to November 27 for Christmas Day, and plant a second bowl ten days later for New Year. A pot of well-drained mix with drainage holes does the same job, started cool and dark for two to three weeks and then moved to the same sunny window. When the stems get tall, give them more light and a cooler room, or water with a 4 to 6 percent alcohol solution once the shoots are 1 to 2 inches tall, per Cornell University, and the stems grow one-third to one-half shorter with the flowers unchanged. When the flowers open, move the bowl to the coolest room out of direct sun, and the display lasts two to three weeks. Then discard the spent bulbs, keep the pebbles for next year, and plant fresh bulbs in November. That is the whole method — no chilling, no fertilizer, and no special equipment — and that simplicity is why how to force paperwhite bulbs is the easiest indoor bulb project there is.

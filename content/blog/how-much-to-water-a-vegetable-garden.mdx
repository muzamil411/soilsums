A vegetable garden needs roughly **one inch of water a week**, from rain or from you. On a 4 × 8
foot bed that is about **20 US gallons (76 litres)** a week; on 100 square feet, about 62 gallons.
The conversion is one line: gallons = square feet × inches × 0.623.

## How much is 1 inch of water per square foot?

**0.623 US gallons**, or 2.36 litres. Here is where that number comes from, because it is
arithmetic rather than a rule of thumb:

```
1 inch over 1 square foot = 144 cubic inches
1 US gallon               = 231 cubic inches
144 / 231                 = 0.623 gallons
```

So:

```
gallons = square feet x inches x 0.623
```

In metric the same sum is cleaner still: a millimetre of water over a square metre is exactly
one litre.

| Garden area          | An inch a week | In litres |
| -------------------- | -------------- | --------- |
| 16 sq ft (4 × 4 bed) | 10 gal         | 38 L      |
| 32 sq ft (4 × 8 bed) | 20 gal         | 76 L      |
| 64 sq ft             | 40 gal         | 151 L     |
| 100 sq ft            | 62 gal         | 236 L     |
| 200 sq ft            | 125 gal        | 473 L     |
| 400 sq ft            | 249 gal        | 943 L     |

The figure that surprises people is the second one. A single 4 × 8 ft raised bed wants about
twenty gallons a week — four full five-gallon buckets, every week, in dry weather. Most gardens
are watered considerably less than their owners believe.

subtracts the rain you have already had.

## Not every crop wants the same inch

An inch is an average. Our [crop data](/data-sources/) records a weekly figure per crop:

| Crop                                      | Inches per week | Notes                                                                |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tomato, cucumber, zucchini, winter squash | 1.5 in          | Heavy drinkers; irregular water causes blossom end rot and splitting |
| Potato, sweet corn                        | 1.5 in          | Critical at tuber set and at tasselling                              |
| Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower            | 1.5 in          | Stress gives small, loose heads                                      |
| Lettuce, spinach, carrot, bean, pea       | 1 in            | Steady rather than heavy                                             |
| Most herbs                                | 1 in            | Several prefer to run slightly dry                                   |

Fruiting crops during fruit fill are where the extra half inch actually matters. In spring and
autumn almost everything wants less than the summer figure.

## Find out what your hose delivers

This takes two minutes and changes how you water.

Time how long it takes to fill a container whose volume you know — a five-gallon bucket is ideal.
Now you know your hose's rate, and "twenty gallons on that bed" becomes a number of minutes
rather than a guess.

Do the same with a watering can, which is easier still: count cans rather than minutes. A
standard two-gallon can means ten cans on a 4 × 8 bed.

## Deep and infrequent, not little and often

This habit matters more than the number.

Water deeply and less often, and roots follow the moisture down. A deeply rooted plant has a
reserve to draw on and rides out a hot week. Water lightly every day and roots stay in the top
inch of soil, where the first missed day finds them.

| Approach           | What the roots do             | What happens in a heatwave                    |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| 20 gal once a week | Root down through the profile | Plants cope for several days                  |
| 3 gal every day    | Stay in the top inch          | Plants wilt within a day of a missed watering |

Two or three deep waterings a week in hot weather is a reasonable compromise for a bed; one is
usually enough in spring and autumn. The exception is new sowings and transplants, whose roots
are only in the top inch — those want light and frequent until established.

## How to tell if a bed is actually dry

Push a finger two inches into the soil. If it is dry at that depth, water. If it is damp, wait.

Surface appearance is a poor guide in both directions. A bed can look dry on top and be damp two
inches down after rain, or look dark and damp on top while the root zone is bone dry because a
light shower wet only the surface.

Wilting is a late signal, and in strong midday sun some plants — squash especially — wilt from
transpiration rather than dry soil and recover by evening. Check the soil before you act on
leaves.

## What reduces the number

**Mulch.** An inch or two of organic mulch measurably slows evaporation from the soil surface
and keeps the root zone cooler. It is the single most effective thing you can do to reduce
watering — [best mulch for a vegetable garden](/blog/best-mulch-for-a-vegetable-garden/) covers
what to use, and the [mulch calculator](/tools/mulch-calculator/) how much.

**Depth.** A deeper bed holds a bigger reservoir and dries out more slowly. See
[how deep should a raised bed be for vegetables](/blog/how-deep-should-a-raised-bed-be/).

**Organic matter.** Compost worked in raises water-holding capacity. A bed with years of compost
in it needs watering less often than a fresh one.

**Watering the soil, not the leaves.** A soaker hose or drip line under mulch loses very little
to evaporation and keeps foliage dry, which matters for disease on
[tomatoes](/crops/tomato/) and [cucumbers](/crops/cucumber/) in particular.

"An inch a week" is the standard advice and it is reasonable. The trouble is that gardens are
watered in gallons from a hose or a can, and almost nobody knows what an inch of water on their
beds actually amounts to. This is the arithmetic, and the handful of habits that matter more
than the number.

## An inch a week, in gallons

An inch of water over one square foot is 144 cubic inches. A US gallon is exactly 231 cubic
inches. So:

```
gallons = square feet x inches x 0.6234
```

That 0.6234 is 144 divided by 231, not a fudge factor. In metric the same sum is cleaner still:
a millimetre of water over a square metre is exactly one litre.

Which gives, for an inch a week:

| Garden area          | Gallons per week | Litres |
| -------------------- | ---------------- | ------ |
| 32 sq ft (a 4x8 bed) | 20               | 76     |
| 100 sq ft            | 62               | 236    |
| 200 sq ft            | 125              | 473    |
| 400 sq ft            | 249              | 943    |

The figure that surprises people is the first one. A single four by eight foot raised bed wants
about twenty gallons a week — which is four full two-and-a-half gallon watering cans, every
week, in dry weather. Most gardens are watered considerably less than they are believed to be.

## Find out what your hose delivers

This takes two minutes and changes how you water. Time how long it takes to fill a container
you know the volume of — a two gallon bucket, say. Now you know your hose's rate, and "twenty
gallons on that bed" becomes a number of minutes rather than a guess.

Do the same with a watering can, which is easier: count cans rather than minutes.

## Deep and infrequent, not little and often

This is the habit that matters more than the number.

Water deeply and less often, and roots follow the moisture down. A deeply rooted plant has a
reserve to draw on and will ride out a hot week. Water lightly every day and roots stay in the
top inch of soil, where the first missed day finds them.

Two long soakings a week is a good default for beds in the ground. Aim to wet the soil six
inches down; dig a small hole afterwards the first few times to see whether you actually did.

Containers are the exception. They hold so little soil that a large pot in high summer can need
water daily, and a small one twice. That is not a failure of technique, it is the volume.

## Measure the rain

Rain counts towards the weekly total, and it is easy to over- or under-estimate. A straight-sided
container left in the open — a jam jar, a tin — is an honest rain gauge. Put it away from walls
and overhanging branches and measure the depth with a ruler.

Half an inch of rain on a 200 square foot garden is 62 gallons you did not have to carry. It is
worth knowing about.

## Mulch does the job for you

Two or three inches of mulch cuts evaporation from the soil surface substantially and keeps the
soil temperature steadier. It is the single most effective water-saving measure available to a
gardener, and once it is down it works every day without further involvement.

The one adjustment: check under the mulch rather than on top of it when deciding whether a bed
is dry.

## Water the soil, not the plant

Wet foliage is how blight, mildew and leaf spot get established. A watering can with the rose
taken off, a soaker hose or a drip line all put water at the roots and leave the leaves dry.

Overhead sprinklers are the least efficient option in a vegetable garden: much of the water
evaporates before it lands, the distribution is uneven, and the foliage stays wet.

## When plants need more

Increase the weekly figure when:

- the weather is hot, dry or windy;
- the soil is sandy and drains fast;
- a crop is flowering or setting fruit, which is the most water-sensitive stage;
- plants are in containers or in a shallow bed.

Reduce it when the weather is cool, the soil is heavy clay, or a crop is finishing — potatoes
and onions both store better for a drier final few weeks.

## The short version

Work out what an inch on your beds actually is in gallons, find out what your hose delivers,
then water deeply twice a week rather than lightly every day. Keep a jar out to measure the
rain, mulch two or three inches deep, and check the soil with a finger rather than trusting the
surface.

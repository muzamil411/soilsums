Compost happens whether you manage it or not. What management changes is how fast, how much it
smells, and whether it ends up as crumbly soil improver or a sour heap of recognisable vegetable
peelings. These are the six failures that account for most complaints.

## 1. Too much carbon, so nothing happens

The most common one by a distance. A pile of autumn leaves, straw or shredded cardboard has
plenty of carbon and almost no nitrogen, and the microbes that do the work cannot build bodies
without it. The pile sits, cool and unchanged, for a very long time.

**Symptoms:** no warmth, no smell, no visible change over months.

**Fix:** add a nitrogen-rich green. Grass clippings, vegetable scraps, manure or alfalfa meal.
Because greens are mostly water, it takes more of them by weight than people expect — balancing
a modest heap of dry leaves can need a surprising quantity of grass.

Work out the actual ratio rather than guessing, because the popular shortcut of averaging the
materials' C:N ratios gives badly wrong answers. The
[compost ratio calculator](/tools/compost-ratio-calculator/) computes it from the real masses of
carbon and nitrogen and tells you how much of a given green would bring the pile into range.

## 2. Too much nitrogen, so it smells of ammonia

The opposite failure, and the one the neighbours notice. A pile of grass clippings or fresh
manure has more nitrogen than the microbes can incorporate, and the surplus leaves as ammonia
gas — which is both the smell and a waste of the nitrogen you wanted to keep.

**Symptoms:** sharp ammonia smell, a slimy texture, the pile collapsing into a mat.

**Fix:** mix in a carbon-rich brown and turn it. Shredded leaves, straw and torn cardboard all
work. Turning is as important as adding, because the pile also needs air.

## 3. Too wet and airless

Composting is aerobic. When a pile is waterlogged or compacted, the organisms that need oxygen
die back and anaerobic ones take over, which is a different and much smellier process.

**Symptoms:** sour or rotten smell rather than ammonia, a sodden feel, no heat.

**Fix:** turn it, adding dry brown material as you go to open the structure. Cover an open pile
in a wet climate. If it sits in a hollow, move it.

The target is a wrung-out sponge: damp enough that a squeezed handful holds together, dry enough
that no water runs out.

## 4. Too dry

Less common than too wet, but it stalls a pile just as completely. Microbes live in water films
around particles, and below about 40% moisture they go dormant.

**Symptoms:** dusty, unchanged material, often with a dry crust on top.

**Fix:** water it while turning, layer by layer, rather than pouring water on top where it runs
off. A pile in full sun in a dry summer may need this several times.

## 5. Too small

A pile generates heat faster than it loses it only above a certain size, because heat escapes
through the surface and volume grows faster than surface area. Below roughly a cubic yard, a pile
will compost — but cold and slowly.

**Symptoms:** a well-balanced, correctly damp pile that still never warms up.

**Fix:** make it bigger, or accept a cold pile. A cold pile produces perfectly good compost in a
year; it just will not kill weed seeds or pathogens, so keep perennial weed roots and diseased
material out of it.

## 6. Putting in things that should not go in

Some of these matter more than others.

**Keep out:** meat, fish, dairy and cooking oils, which attract vermin and go rancid rather than
composting. Dog and cat waste, for pathogen reasons. Anything treated with a persistent herbicide
— including straw and manure from treated fields, which can damage vegetables years later.
Diseased plant material, unless the pile genuinely gets hot.

**Be careful with:** perennial weed roots and seed heads, which a cool pile will happily
propagate. Wood ash, which is strongly alkaline and fine in small amounts. Citrus and onion in
quantity, which slow things down more than they ruin anything.

**Fine, despite the myths:** coffee grounds, tea leaves, eggshells, paper and cardboard,
bread, and cooked vegetable scraps.

## The other frequent complaint: fruit flies

Not really a failure, just exposed food scraps. Bury kitchen waste in the middle of the pile
rather than leaving it on top, and cover it with a layer of browns. A lidded bin helps. So does
keeping a bag of shredded leaves beside the pile to throw a handful on after each addition.

## Turning, and whether you have to

You do not have to turn a compost pile. You have to turn it to make compost quickly.

Turning does two things: it mixes the materials so the balance is even throughout, and it
reintroduces air. A pile turned every week or two in warm weather can be finished in two months.
The same pile left alone takes most of a year and produces the same end result.

Turn when the pile cools down after having been hot. That is the signal that the readily
available material in the current arrangement has been used.

## Knowing when it is done

Finished compost is dark, crumbly, smells of earth and nothing else, and you cannot identify what
went into it. It should be roughly half the volume you started with.

If you can still see recognisable material, it is not done, and using it will tie up nitrogen in
the bed while it finishes. Screening out the coarse bits and returning them to the next pile is
a good habit.

## The short version

Check the carbon to nitrogen balance first, because that explains most stalled and most smelly
piles. Keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge and no wetter. Make it at least a cubic yard if you
want heat. Bury the kitchen scraps. Turn it when it cools, or do not turn it and wait longer.

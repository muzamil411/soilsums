A yard of dirt is a cubic yard — 27 cubic feet, a cube three feet on each side. Spread three
inches deep it covers about **108 square feet**, roughly a 10 by 10 foot patch. At one inch it
covers 324 square feet; at six inches, 54. Depth is what decides how far it goes.

## How big is a cubic yard, really?

A cubic yard is a cube three feet on every side. Three by three by three is **27 cubic feet**,
and that is a definition rather than a measurement — it is exact, and it is the same everywhere.

The number that surprises people is how much space that occupies. Picture a box a little
shorter than an average adult, and as deep and as wide as it is tall. Dumped on a driveway it
makes a heap roughly the size of a small car. A wheelbarrow holds around 3 cubic feet when
full, so a cubic yard is about nine full barrow loads — which is the comparison that usually
lands, because anyone who has moved one barrow load knows what nine feels like.

It is also why bulk and bagged soil feel like such different purchases. A cubic yard delivered
loose is a single heap you have to move. The same volume in bags is a pallet of individually
liftable units, at a price that reflects the packaging.

## How much area does a cubic yard cover?

This is the question most people actually mean, and it has a clean answer.

**A cubic yard covers 324 square feet divided by the depth in inches.**

The derivation is short. One inch is a twelfth of a foot, so spreading 27 cubic feet one inch
deep covers 27 ÷ (1/12) = 324 square feet. Spread it twice as deep and it covers half as much.
Every figure below follows by dividing 324 by the depth.

| Depth     | Area one cubic yard covers |
| --------- | -------------------------- |
| 1 inch    | 324 sq ft                  |
| 2 inches  | 162 sq ft                  |
| 3 inches  | 108 sq ft                  |
| 4 inches  | 81 sq ft                   |
| 6 inches  | 54 sq ft                   |
| 12 inches | 27 sq ft                   |

For a depth not on that list, divide 324 by the depth in inches. Five inches gives 64.8 square
feet; eight gives 40.5. The relationship is exact at every depth, because it is arithmetic
rather than a rule of thumb.

Reading it the other way round is just as useful. If you know the area you want to cover and
the depth you want, multiply them and divide by 324 to get the yards. A 200 square foot border
at three inches needs 200 × 3 ÷ 324 = 1.85 cubic yards, so two.

## How many bags of soil make a yard?

A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so the bag count depends entirely on the size of the bag.

| Bag size   | Bags per cubic yard  |
| ---------- | -------------------- |
| 0.75 cu ft | 36                   |
| 1 cu ft    | 27                   |
| 1.5 cu ft  | 18                   |
| 2 cu ft    | 14 (13.5 rounded up) |

**This is where orders go wrong.** Bag sizes are not standardised, and two bags of what looks
like the same product can hold different volumes — 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3 cubic foot bags all
exist, and potting mixes are frequently labelled in dry quarts rather than cubic feet at all.
Counting bags without reading the cubic feet on the label is how people end up with half the
material they needed, or a third more than they wanted.

Check the volume printed on the bag, then divide 27 by it. If the label is in quarts or litres,
the [soil volume converter](/tools/soil-volume-converter/) will put it into cubic feet first.

## Working out how much topsoil you need

The method is the same whatever you are filling.

1. **Measure length and width in feet**, and decide the depth in inches.
2. **Multiply length × width × depth** to get cubic-foot-inches.
3. **Divide by 12** to get cubic feet, or **divide by 324** to get cubic yards directly.

A bed 4 feet by 8 feet filled 10 inches deep is 4 × 8 × 10 ÷ 12 = 26.7 cubic feet, which is
just under a cubic yard. A 300 square foot lawn area top-dressed at half an inch is 300 × 0.5 ÷
324 = 0.46 cubic yards, so half a yard.

topsoil rather than compost — is a separate question, covered in
[what to fill raised garden beds with](/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/).

## Bulk or bags: how to work out which is cheaper

People searching "how much is a yard of dirt" often mean the price, and the honest answer is
that **there is no national figure worth quoting.** Bulk soil prices vary by region, by
material, by supplier and by season, and any number printed here would be wrong for most
readers and out of date for all of them within a year.

What does not go out of date is the method. Three steps:

**Work out the bagged price per cubic foot.** Take the price of the bag and divide by the cubic
feet printed on it. A bag is a small, known quantity, so this is easy and it is the figure worth
carrying in your head when you are standing in the aisle.

**Work out the bulk price per cubic foot.** Take the price per cubic yard and divide by 27.
This is the comparison most people never make, and it is usually startling — bulk is very often
a small fraction of the bagged rate for the same material.

**Add delivery to the bulk side.** This is where the comparison actually turns. Bulk soil is
cheap and delivery is not, and a delivery charge spread across half a yard makes bulk
expensive, while the same charge spread across four yards makes it cheap. Many suppliers also
have a minimum order, which can decide the question before price does.

The crossover point differs for everyone, which is exactly why the method is more useful than
a number. As a shape of the answer: small jobs favour bags because you buy only what you need
and carry it yourself, and large jobs favour bulk because the delivery charge stops mattering.

## What does a yard of dirt weigh?

Here is the honest answer, and it is more useful than the one most pages give you: **nobody can
tell you, including us.** A yard of delivered topsoil has no fixed weight, because weight is not a
property of soil — it is a property of one supplier's pile on one particular day. How much organic
matter is in that batch, how finely it was screened, how long it has sat, and above all how wet it
is. Water is heavy, and the same soil after a week of rain can weigh a third more than it did dry.

That is why no extension service publishes a figure for it. Every calculator that prints one —
2,200 lb, 2,700 lb, "about a ton" — is printing a number that is wrong for most of the people
reading it.

What _is_ published is two different things, and keeping them apart is the whole trick.

**Compost is published by weight per cubic yard, as supplied.** Oregon State University Extension's
EM 9217 gives about **1,000 lb per cubic yard** as a rule of thumb for screened compost at 50%
moisture. The full published range runs from **800 to more than 1,600 lb per cubic yard** depending
on moisture, particle size and compaction, and very wet composts can exceed 1,500 on their own.
That is a real answer to a real question, and it is a range because the material genuinely varies by
a factor of two.

**Soil is published by texture, for soil in the ground — and that is a different question from what
a delivered load weighs.** The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service publishes, for each soil
texture, the dry bulk density below which roots grow well and the density above which they are
restricted. Those are thresholds for diagnosing compaction in undisturbed field soil, measured
ovendry: the "ideal below" figure is not a minimum weight, the "restricting above" figure is not a
maximum — compacted soils exceed it routinely — and a wet delivered pile carries water the
dry-basis figure excludes, so it can outweigh it. They cannot be turned into a delivered weight.
The NRCS table also does not apply to red clayey soils or to soils formed from volcanic ash.

### Cubic yards to tons, and back

This is the conversion people search for, and the reason the answers disagree everywhere is that
there is no single one. A US short ton is 2,000 lb, so:

- **Compost:** roughly **1.25 to 2.5 cubic yards to the ton**, depending on how wet it is —
  fewer than 1.25 for very wet compost, since Oregon State's published range runs "800 to more
  than 1,600" lb per cubic yard and 1,600 is not a ceiling.
- **Soil:** **no sourced figure to convert from** — it depends on the supplier's material and its
  moisture. If your supplier quotes a weight per cubic yard, divide 2,000 by it: at 2,000 lb per
  yard a ton is a yard, at 2,500 it is eight tenths. Without their figure, a ton of soil cannot be
  turned into yards.

That catches people out, and it is worth stating plainly: **you cannot convert between a ton of
soil and a yard of soil without knowing whose pile it is.** If a supplier quotes by the ton and
you have worked out your needs in yards, ask them for the weight per cubic yard — otherwise you
are not comparing like with like.

The [bulk soil calculator](/tools/bulk-soil-calculator/) does this both ways for your own area and
depth — a published range for compost, your supplier's figure for soil — and shows which source
each figure comes from.

### When the weight actually matters

For spreading soil on a bed, it does not — order by volume and ignore the weight entirely. It
matters in exactly three situations, and in all three the answer is the same:

- **A truck payload**, covered in the next section.
- **A floor or balcony loading.** A raised bed on a roof terrace is a structural question, and a
  range from a calculator is not the right input for it.
- **A large order priced by the ton**, where the wetness of the pile on delivery day changes what
  you get for your money.

In every one of those cases, **ask your supplier what their material weighs.** They handle it daily
and they know, and their figure is better than any published average. That is not a cop-out — it is
the correct answer, and no page that prints a confident single number can give it to you.

## Will a yard of dirt fit in a pickup truck?

By volume, usually. A full-size pickup bed holds roughly two to two and a half cubic yards
level with the sides, and a smaller truck around one and a half — but you do not have to take
that on trust, because a truck bed is a box anyone can measure. **Multiply its length, width
and depth in feet, then divide by 27.** A bed 6 feet by 5 feet, loaded 1 foot deep, is
6 × 5 × 1 ÷ 27 = 1.1 cubic yards. Measure to the height you would actually load to rather than
the top of the sides.

**Volume is rarely what decides it.** Loose soil is heavy — see the section above for how heavy,
and why the honest answer is a range — and what limits a truck is its payload rating rather than
its bed capacity — you will reach the weight the vehicle is designed
to carry long before you run out of room. Check the payload figure for your specific vehicle
before you fill it, and if a supplier is loading for you, tell them what you are driving.

The same applies to the drive home. A load within the payload rating still changes how a
vehicle brakes and corners.

## What does not work

**Assuming a "yard" means a square yard.** In landscaping, a yard always means a cubic yard —
a volume, not an area. Ordering by the square yard is a conversation that ends in confusion at
the supplier.

**Ordering the exact calculated figure.** Soil settles, spreads unevenly, and some of it ends
up somewhere other than where you meant. Rounding up to the next sensible unit costs less than
a second delivery.

**Counting bags without reading the label.** Covered above, and it is the most common error on
this page's subject.

**Treating bulk soil as interchangeable between suppliers.** "Topsoil" is a category rather than
a recipe, and two loads can differ visibly in texture and organic matter. Ask to see a sample
before a large order.

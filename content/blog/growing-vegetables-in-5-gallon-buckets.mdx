Yes — a 5 gallon bucket grows tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, bush beans, chard and salad crops
perfectly well. It holds about **17 dry quarts of mix**, roughly the same as a 12-inch pot, which
is about the minimum for a determinate tomato and generous for most other crops. The limits are
water and feeding, not root space.

## The two things that kill container vegetables

Everything else about growing in pots is a detail beside these two.

**Running out of water.** A bucket holds a small, finite reservoir and is fully exposed to sun
and wind. A 5 gallon bucket in high summer can go from damp to bone dry in a day, and a plant
that wilts hard repeatedly never really recovers.

**Sitting in water.** The opposite, and just as fatal. A bucket with no drainage hole, or
standing in a full saucer, has waterlogged mix at the bottom where roots suffocate. Drill the
holes before you fill it.

## Best vegetables to grow in pots for beginners

Start with these. They tolerate the conditions a container imposes and forgive an occasional
mistake.

| Crop                          | Minimum container   | Per 5 gal bucket | Notes                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Salad leaves, lettuce         | 6 in deep           | 4–6 plants       | Tolerates part shade; fastest return of anything here |
| Radish                        | 6 in deep           | A dozen          | The fastest thing here; good filler                      |
| Bush bean                     | 8–10 in deep        | 3–4 plants       | Crops heavily for a few weeks, then replant           |
| Spring onion, chives          | 6 in deep           | A clump          | Narrow and shallow-rooted                             |
| Chard                         | 10 in pot           | 2 plants         | Productive over a long period                         |
| Pepper, chilli                | 10–12 in pot        | 1 plant          | Likes the reflected heat of a patio wall              |
| Tomato, determinate           | 12 in pot / 5 gal   | 1 plant          | Needs daily water in summer and staking               |
| Tomato, indeterminate         | 14 in pot or larger | 1 plant          | A bucket is the bare minimum; bigger is better        |
| Eggplant                      | 12 in pot           | 1 plant          | Wants warmth; the bucket helps                        |
| Strawberry                    | 12 in pot           | 4 plants         | Also good in hanging baskets, away from slugs         |
| Herbs — basil, parsley, thyme | 8 in deep           | 1–3 plants       | Mint is better in a pot than a bed                    |

One plant per bucket is the rule for anything fruiting. Two tomatoes in one bucket produce less
than one, and dry out twice as fast.

## Which vegetables fail in pots

**Sweet corn.** Needs a block of plants for wind pollination, and takes enormous containers to do
it badly. Skip it.

**Pumpkins and winter squash.** Enormous plants for one or two fruit — our data gives winter
squash a 36-inch spacing and pumpkin 48. A zucchini in a half barrel is just about worth it; a
pumpkin is not.

**Main-crop carrots and parsnips.** They need depth a bucket cannot give without the mix drying
out constantly. Short, stubby carrot varieties in a deep pot are the workaround.

**Anything you want in quantity.** A bucket of potatoes yields a meal, not a crop. If the point
is volume, a bed is the answer.

## How much mix does a bucket take?

About 17 US dry quarts. Not 20 — that is the liquid-quart figure, and bagged potting mix is sold
in dry quarts, which are around 16% larger.

```
5 US gallons = 1,155 cubic inches = 0.668 cubic feet
0.668 x 25.714 dry quarts per cubic foot = 17.2 dry quarts
```

A 25-quart bag fills one bucket with room to spare; three bags fill four.
[How many quarts of soil in a 5 gallon bucket](/blog/how-much-potting-soil-a-container-needs/)
works through the unit confusion, and the
[container soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) will do any pot size and give a bag
count.

Use a **potting mix**, not garden soil and not pure compost. Garden soil compacts in a container
and drains badly. Pure compost holds water beautifully at first, then slumps and can turn
hydrophobic when it dries.

## Watering, which is the whole game

A bucket in full summer sun with a grown tomato in it will want water every day, sometimes twice.

| Check                          | What it tells you                                       |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Finger two inches into the mix | Dry at that depth means water now                       |
| Lifting the bucket             | Light means dry; this is the most reliable test         |
| Water running straight through | The mix has dried and gone hydrophobic — soak it slowly |

Water until it runs from the holes, then stop. Little-and-often keeps only the top wet and
trains roots upwards into the part that dries first.

If a bucket has dried out completely, stand it in a tray of water for an hour rather than
pouring from above — dry mix sheds water. And mulch the surface of a container, exactly as you
would a bed. An inch of bark or straw measurably slows evaporation.

The [watering calculator](/tools/garden-watering-calculator/) works in the same units for beds,
and [how much water does a vegetable garden need per week](/blog/how-much-to-water-a-vegetable-garden/)
covers the arithmetic.

## Feeding, which containers need more of

Every watering carries nutrients out of the drainage holes, and a bucket holds no reserve.

Most container vegetables want a dilute liquid feed **weekly** once they are growing strongly,
against every three to four weeks in the ground. Start about four weeks after planting — most
bought mixes carry enough fertilizer for the first month.

[How often should you fertilize vegetables](/blog/how-often-should-you-fertilize-vegetables/)
covers what to use and when to ease off nitrogen; our [tomato](/crops/tomato/) and
[pepper](/crops/pepper/) guides carry the crop specifics.

## Two more things worth knowing

**Wait for warmth.** Containers warm faster than the ground, but the air does not. Peppers and
eggplant want night temperatures reliably above 50°F (10°C) before they go out, whatever the
frost date says.

**Bigger is better, every time.** The larger the volume, the slower it dries, and drying out is
what kills most container plants. If you are choosing between a bucket and a half barrel, take
the barrel.

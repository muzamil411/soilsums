**Feed a cool-season lawn in September.** University of Missouri Extension makes it the most
important application of the year, at 1 to 1.5 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet, with moderate
rates in mid-October and November for roots and a small feed in early April. Spring is the
afterthought, not the main event.

That is the opposite of how most people run a lawn, and of how fertilizer is sold. The reason is
simple: September is when a cool-season lawn is actually working.

## Why September and not spring

Cool-season grass has two growing seasons a year and summer is not one of them. The lawn comes out of
summer thin, worn and short of reserves, and then gets several weeks of warm soil and cool air —
exactly the conditions in which it builds roots and thickens rather than sprinting upwards.

Feed it then and the nitrogen goes into recovery and root mass that carries the lawn through winter
and into next spring. **Feed it heavily in spring instead and you get the same nitrogen spent on
soft top growth**, right before the season that will punish soft top growth. That is why Missouri's
April rate is deliberately the smallest on the calendar.

This is also why the autumn fertiliser aisle is not a marketing invention even though the labels on
it often are. The timing is right; the product names are the part to ignore.

The spread inside Kentucky bluegrass is the interesting part. **The same grass takes 2 to 3 lb a
year as a decent domestic lawn and 4 to 5 lb if you want it to look like a show lawn** — the higher
figure is a choice about appearance, not a requirement. More nitrogen means more mowing, more
clippings, and more growth to water.

Red fescues want the least of any of them. If your lawn is a fescue mix and it has been fine on
almost nothing, that is not neglect getting lucky.

Two things to read off that. **September carries the largest single rate**, and it is the one to keep
if you drop everything else. And the two autumn applications after it have no published rate — the
publication says "moderate" and gives no number, so neither do we. Anyone quoting you a precise
figure for a mid-October feed is supplying it themselves.

## How many of these does a lawn actually need

Iowa State University Extension answers this by the standard you want rather than by the calendar,
and it is the most practical framing of the whole question:

- **A high-quality lawn:** three applications.
- **A moderate programme:** mid-September, plus late October or early November.
- **A minimally maintained lawn:** late October to early November alone.

Notice what the minimal programme is. It is not a spring feed. **The one application a neglected
lawn most benefits from is a late autumn one**, which is the exact application most people skip
because the grass has stopped growing visibly and it feels like the season is over.

## Where the two publications disagree, and why it matters

Both services tell you to stop putting nitrogen down. They do not tell you the same thing.

- **University of Missouri Extension:** do not apply nitrogen fertilizer, particularly quickly
  available soluble forms, past **May 1**.
- **Iowa State University Extension:** do not fertilize Kentucky bluegrass and other cool-season
  grasses during **the summer months (June, July, and August)**.

These are different claims about different things. Missouri names a date and singles out a form of
nitrogen — the soluble, quickly available kind. Iowa State names a season and all nitrogen.

**So a reader feeding in late May is outside one recommendation and inside the other**, and that is
the most useful thing on this page. Nobody is wrong; the two are drawing the line in different
places, and the honest response is to show you where both lines fall rather than to average them
into a date neither publication gives.

If you want the cautious reading: stop at the beginning of May, as Missouri says, and treat June to
August as closed regardless. If you have a slow-release product and late May is when the job can
actually be done, you are inside Iowa State's rule and outside Missouri's — knowingly, which is the
whole point of knowing that both exist.

## What the bag should say

Missouri's ratio guidance is the short version of what to buy:

- **Routine maintenance: 3:1:1 to 4:1:2.** Heavily weighted to nitrogen, because that is what a
  lawn removes most of and what mowing carries away.
- **After a soil test: 1:1:1 or 2:1:1**, if the test shows phosphorus or potassium is actually
  short.

The order matters. A balanced bag is not the safe default — it is the answer to a test result.
[What the three numbers on fertilizer mean](/blog/what-the-three-numbers-on-fertilizer-mean/) covers
how to read the label itself, and phosphorus in particular is restricted in some places precisely
because it was routinely applied where it was not needed.

## This is a cool-season calendar only

Everything above applies to Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue, the fine and red fescues, and the
ryegrasses — the grasses that grow in spring and autumn and suffer in July.

**Bermudagrass, zoysiagrass, centipedegrass, bahiagrass and buffalograss run the other way round.**
They do their growing in the heat that shuts a cool-season lawn down, so a calendar built around
September and November is wrong for them in a way that would do real harm. We do not have a sourced
warm-season feeding schedule, so this page does not print one. If your lawn is warm-season, the
thing to look for is your own state extension service's calendar for that species.

## Turning pounds of nitrogen into pounds of fertilizer

Every rate on this page is **pounds of nitrogen**, not pounds of product. A bag of fertilizer is
mostly not nitrogen, so the two are never the same number, and the arithmetic to get from one to the
other is where most people give up and guess.

The [fertilizer calculator](/tools/fertilizer-calculator/) does that conversion: give it your area,
the rate you want, and the first number on the bag, and it works out how much of that bag to spread.

What it cannot do is decide the rate for you. That comes from the grass you have and the standard
you want it kept to, which is what the annual figures above are for.

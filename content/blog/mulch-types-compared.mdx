Mulch does three jobs: it blocks light from weed seeds, it slows evaporation from the soil
surface, and as it breaks down it feeds the soil. Different materials are good at different
parts of that, and the right choice for a vegetable bed is not the right choice for a shrub
border.

## Straw

**Good at:** weed suppression, moisture retention, easy to move aside, breaks down within a
season and improves the soil as it goes.

**Costs you:** it can contain seed, so a bale of the wrong thing seeds your bed with cereal.
It is light and blows about until it settles. And it is worth asking whether the field it came
from was treated with a persistent herbicide, which can survive composting and damage
vegetables.

The default choice for annual vegetable beds. Apply three to four inches, since it settles a
lot.

## Shredded leaves

**Good at:** everything straw is good at, and free. Leaf mould is one of the best soil
conditioners there is, and a mulch of shredded leaves becomes that in place.

**Costs you:** whole leaves mat into layers that shed water, so they need shredding — a pass
with a mower over a pile does it. And you can only collect them once a year.

If you have deciduous trees anywhere nearby, this is the best value mulch available.

## Compost

**Good at:** feeding the soil directly, holding moisture, and looking tidy. It is the only mulch
that is also a fertility input of any significance.

**Costs you:** it suppresses weeds less well than coarser material, because it is a perfectly
good seed bed itself. And it is the most expensive option per cubic foot if you are buying it.

A good choice as a thin layer under a coarser mulch, or as the annual top-up on a bed.

## Grass clippings

**Good at:** free, high in nitrogen, breaks down fast.

**Costs you:** applied thick and fresh, clippings mat into a slimy, airless layer that smells.
Apply them in thin layers, dried for a day first, and never more than an inch at a time. Also
avoid clippings from a lawn treated with weedkiller.

Best used as a supplement to another mulch rather than on its own.

## Bark chips and wood chips

**Good at:** lasting. Two or three seasons before they need topping up, and excellent weed
suppression.

**Costs you:** they are awkward in a bed that gets dug and replanted several times a year, and
they tie up nitrogen at the soil boundary while decomposing. On the surface that effect is
small; dug in, it is significant.

Right for paths, perennials, fruit bushes and permanent plantings. Wrong for an annual
vegetable bed you turn over twice a year.

## Pine needles

**Good at:** light, easy to spread, slow to break down, and they knit together so they do not
blow away.

**Costs you:** slower to feed the soil than leaves or straw. The claim that they acidify soil
significantly is largely overstated — finished pine needle compost ends up close to neutral —
but they are slow enough that the point rarely comes up.

## Black plastic and landscape fabric

**Good at:** total weed suppression and warming the soil early in spring, which genuinely helps
heat-loving crops such as melons and peppers in a short season.

**Costs you:** it adds nothing to the soil, it stops rain getting in evenly, it can overheat
roots in high summer, and landscape fabric eventually shreds into the soil in a way that is
unpleasant to deal with years later.

A tool for a specific job — early warmth for a specific crop — rather than a general mulch.

## Living mulch and cover crops

Not a mulch in the usual sense, but the same job done by plants. A low cover crop between rows
shades the soil, holds it in place and feeds it when turned in.

**Costs you:** it competes for water and nutrients, and it takes managing. Better suited to
paths and between widely spaced crops than to a dense bed.

## How much to use

Two to three inches is right for most beds. Enough to block light from weed seeds and slow
evaporation, not so much that the soil stays cold and airless.

Coarse mulches can go to three or four inches because water and air move through them more
freely. Fine mulches such as compost work at the shallower end.

Work out the volume before you buy, because mulch is sold by volume and spread by area — and
the two are easy to mismatch. A two cubic foot bag covers eight square feet at three inches
deep, which is less than most people expect. The
[mulch calculator](/tools/mulch-calculator/) gives the bag count and the coverage per bag for
whatever depth you choose.

## Two rules whatever you use

**Keep it off stems and trunks.** Mulch piled against bark stays damp, and damp bark rots.
Leave an inch or two clear.

**Check what is already there before adding more.** Layers accumulate into a mat that sheds
water. Rake the old layer to break the crust, then top up to the depth you want in total.

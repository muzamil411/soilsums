## How to care for string of pearls: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and keep it out of harsh direct sun.** The brightest spot you can spare, a few feet from an east- or south-facing window, with bright light all day and no harsh midday sun on the pearls, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for string of pearls, because the plant is a desert succulent that grows under bushes in bright, filtered light — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and loses its beads, while a plant moved into harsh direct sun in one day scorches.

**Let the mix dry, then water thoroughly — and read the pearls.** Water only when the mix has dried through and the pearls are starting to shrivel, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for string of pearls.

**Keep it warm in summer, cool in winter, and never let it freeze.** Warm days of 70 to 80°F in summer, a cool rest of 50 to 60°F in winter, and inside long before the first cold snap. Cold is the third rule of how to care for string of pearls — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a single cold night can turn the pearls to mush.

### The last two rules in how to care for string of pearls

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed in spring, less often than a leafy houseplant gets, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding does not make a string of pearls grow faster; it makes it grow soft and rot faster. Feeding is the fourth rule of how to care for string of pearls.

**Keep it in a gritty mix, in a shallow pot with holes, and renew it from cuttings.** A very well-drained cactus mix in a shallow clay pot with drainage holes, repotted only every year or two — and new plants from cuttings when the old one dies back after a few years, because a string of pearls is a plant you renew, not one you rescue. Free drainage and a fresh start are the fifth rule of how to care for string of pearls, and they protect the roots and the beads for years.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

The first warning that the light is wrong is stretch, and it comes in one form in how to care for string of pearls. A plant in too little light does not die — it stretches. ### Water

Water is the decision that kills the most string of pearls in how to care for string of pearls, and the sources are blunt about it — and they disagree on one point, which this guide shows honestly. UW–Madison Extension says to allow the potting medium to dry out completely between waterings, providing more water in summer than in winter when the plant is not actively growing, and that plants need to be watered when the leaves start to look a bit shriveled. ### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for string of pearls

The rule is dry, then soak, and the order matters in how to care for string of pearls. A little water every few days keeps the top of the soil wet and the bottom dry, and the roots in the middle rot while the top looks fine. A thorough soak, only when the mix has dried through and the pearls are starting to shrivel, wets the whole root ball and then lets it drain, and the roots get water and air in turn. That is why both sources say the same thing in different words: water infrequently, let it drain, and never let the pot stand in water. A pot without a drainage hole breaks the rule at the start, because the water has nowhere to go — and a saucer left full after watering is the same mistake in slow motion. If the plant is in a pot without holes, repot it into one with holes before you change anything else, because no watering schedule fixes a pot that cannot drain.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once and live with for a year in how to care for string of pearls, and the sources agree on what it should do. ### An airy mix that drains

The air in the mix matters as much as the water in how to care for string of pearls, because the roots breathe — and this plant has almost no roots to spare. ### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the second half of the soil decision in how to care for string of pearls, and the rule is simple: shallow, never deep. ### Repotting: when the crown fills the pot

Repotting is rare work for a string of pearls in how to care for string of pearls, and the plant tells you when it is time. ## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the rule owners overdo in how to care for string of pearls, and the sources are restrained about it. A string of pearls in fresh gritty mix, watered well and kept in bright light, grows steadily on almost no food, and the signs of too much food are worse than the signs of too little: soft, pale growth that rots at the first overwatering, and a white crust on the soil surface.

### The planning figure: lightly in spring, nothing in winter

A plant in lower light gets fed not at all — because it is barely growing, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that rot the roots. If the pearls go soft after a feed, stop feeding and flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear.

### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding does not make a string of pearls grow faster in how to care for string of pearls — it makes it grow soft and rot faster. The salts in fertilizer build up in the mix, the roots cannot take up water against the salt, and the pearls go soft while the plant sits in damp soil. The fix is to stop feeding, flush the mix with plain water, and wait: a plant that has been over-fed for a month needs a month of plain water before it starts growing again. Fresh mix has food in it already, and a plant repotted in spring needs no feed until the next year — which is why the planning figure starts feeding in spring, not at repotting. A string of pearls that is fed lightly and watered well will outgrow a string of pearls that is fed heavily and watered badly, every time.

## Pruning a plant that trails

The fix is to cut the stems back, and the cuttings are not waste: they are new plants, and the pruned crown fills out from the nodes below the cut.

Prune in spring, when the plant is growing, and cut the long, bare stems back to the crown in how to care for string of pearls. Cut a long, bare stem back by a third, or by half if it is very bare, and the plant will fill out from the crown over the summer. Wear gloves, because the cut drips sap that both extension sources say may irritate skin — and let the cut end dry before you handle it, because the sap keeps bleeding until it skins over. Do not prune in autumn or winter, when the plant is barely growing, because a cut made then heals slowly and the plant drops beads instead of branching.

### What the cuttings become

The cuttings from a spring pruning are the next plant in how to care for string of pearls, and they are not waste. A stem tip three to five inches long, cut just below a node, roots in damp mix in a few weeks — and a plant that is pruned every spring gives you cuttings every spring, which is how one string of pearls becomes three. Lay the cutting on the mix with a few nodes covered, per NC State Extension, and keep the mix barely damp — mist the surface, per UW–Madison Extension, rather than soaking it, because a cutting in a wet mix rots before it roots. A cutting taken in spring or summer roots fastest, when the plant is growing — and a cutting taken in winter sits in the mix for months and often rots, because the plant is dormant and the mix stays wet.

## Propagation: cuttings, not rescue

Propagation is the reward for a healthy plant in how to care for string of pearls, and both extension sources list the same method: stem cuttings. Cuttings suit a young plant with plenty of stem tips; they also suit an old plant that is dying back, because the tips are still healthy even when the crown is not. Both cases work, and both are done in spring or summer, when the plant is growing — because a cutting taken in winter roots slowly, and a cutting in a wet winter mix rots before it roots.

### Stem and tip cuttings

Take a stem tip three to five inches long, strip three to four leaves from the bottom, and lay it in or on moist potting mix, lightly covering the last few bottom nodes, in how to care for string of pearls. UW–Madison Extension says roots should quickly develop at each node, and to mist the soil surface to avoid overwatering until the roots are established. Keep the cutting in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, keep the mix barely damp but not wet, and wait: roots form in a few weeks, and the first new beads are the sign that the cutting has taken. A leaf on its own, dropped on the mix, is a slower and less reliable form per NC State Extension — use stem cuttings, and the plant renews itself.

### Renewing an old plant

Renewal is the method for an old, dying plant in how to care for string of pearls, because it makes new plants from the healthy tips without trying to save the old crown. NC State Extension says these plants tend to die back after several years, and to propagate by stem cuttings instead of trying to revive the older plant. Take cuttings from the healthy stem tips, lay them on fresh gritty mix in a shallow pot, and let them root — then keep the best one and compost the old crown, because the old crown will not fill out again. Renewal takes a few weeks, but it gives you a full young plant at the start, because the new plant already has several rooted stems when you pot it up. Do it in spring, when the plant is growing, and keep the mix barely damp, because mix that stays wet stops the roots from forming — and a renewed plant, hung in bright light and watered on the pearl test, lasts for years before it needs renewing again.

## Problems: shrivel, mush, and bare stems

Problems in how to care for string of pearls are almost always water, light, or cold, and the sources name the same three causes for nearly every symptom. Work through them in order — soil first, then light, then cold — and the plant usually answers within a week. A plant that is checked weekly never gets a bad problem; a plant that is never checked gets one that takes a month to clear.

### Mushy beads: the plant's loudest complaint

Mushy beads are the first problem in how to care for string of pearls, and they are the one owners panic about most. UW–Madison Extension says root rot from overwatering is the most common cause of its demise. NC State Extension says soggy wet soil will result in root rot and the plant's demise, and that wilting leaves can occur from underwatering or overwatering. If the pearls near the soil are yellow, soft and mushy and the mix is wet, it is overwatering: stop watering, let the mix dry out, and take cuttings from the healthy stems above the rot, because the rotted part will not recover. If the pearls are shriveled and wrinkled and the mix is dry, it is underwatering: water thoroughly and the plump pearls further along the stem will firm up again. A bead that has gone mushy will not turn firm again, but the stem above it is still alive, and a cutting from that stem is the next plant.

### Yellow beads

Yellow beads are the second problem in how to care for string of pearls, and they are the overwatering signal. NC State Extension says soggy wet soil will result in root rot, and that yellow or graying leaves may be caused by insects. UW–Madison Extension says root rot from overwatering is the most common cause of its demise. If only the oldest beads at the base are yellowing, that is the plant aging, and no action is needed — the plant sheds its oldest beads as it grows longer, and a bead that has already turned yellow will not turn green again. If many beads yellow at once, check the soil first: if it is wet, stop watering and let it dry out, and check that the pot has drainage holes and the saucer is empty; if the soil is dry and the beads are yellow, the light is too low or the plant is catching a cold draft, so move it to a brighter, warmer spot. If the beads are yellow and gray and the undersides show white cottony masses, it is mealybugs — wipe them off and wash the plant, and repeat a week later.

### Bare stems and the dying crown

Bare stems are the third problem in how to care for string of pearls, and they are the age signal. A plant that is three or four years old goes bare at the crown while the stem tips are still healthy — and that is the plant telling you it is time to renew it, not to rescue it. NC State Extension says these plants tend to die back after several years, and to propagate by stem cuttings instead of trying to revive the older plant. The fix is to take cuttings from the healthy tips, lay them on fresh gritty mix, and start again — because a crown that has died back will not fill out, and a plant that is watered harder in the hope of reviving it rots faster. A plant that is renewed every few years never looks old, and a plant that is never renewed looks tired by year four. The bare crown is not a failure; it is the plant's life cycle, and the cuttings are the next generation.

### Mealybugs, aphids, and gnats

Mealybugs are the first pest in how to care for string of pearls, and they are easy to see once you know what to look for: white, cottony masses in the leaf axils and on the undersides of the beads. UW–Madison Extension says mealybugs or aphids may infest plants but otherwise they have few pest problems. NC State Extension says to monitor for aphids, whiteflies, gnats, ants, and mealybugs. The fix is to check the undersides of the beads every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the bead you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back. Wipe the visible mealybugs off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later, because the eggs hatch after the first treatment. Gnats mean the mix is staying wet — let it dry out, and the gnats leave on their own. Aphids cluster on the new growth at the stem tips; wash them off with plain water and check again in a week. A plant in a dry, bright room with a gritty mix gets few pests — and a plant in a damp, dark room gets all of them.

### Keep the beads clean

Washing the beads is the maintenance that keeps a string of pearls healthy in how to care for string of pearls, and the sources both name the habit in general terms. Dust blocks the light the beads need through their windows, and a dusty plant grows slower than a clean one — so rinse the plant gently with plain water every few weeks, or wipe the trailing stems with a damp cloth, and let it drain completely before you hang it back up. Do not use leaf-shine sprays, because they clog the windows on the beads, and do not handle the stems roughly when they are wet, because a wet bead falls off more easily than a dry one. A clean plant is a plant that can use its light, and a plant that can use its light grows — and every bead that falls off in washing is a cutting, so lay it on the mix and let it root.

## Is string of pearls poisonous to pets?

UW–Madison Extension says the leaves are slightly toxic, that ingestion may cause vomiting or diarrhea, and that the sap may cause skin irritation or rash in sensitive individuals. The honest reading is that all three sources agree on the same picture: mildly toxic, not deadly, but enough to make a pet sick — and the sap is the part that does the harm to skin.

### The riskiest plant in the room

The riskiest string of pearls in the house is the one hanging low in how to care for string of pearls, because its trailing stems hang where a cat can reach them. A string of pearls in a low hanging basket has beads at nose height for a cat and at paw height for a dog, and a cat that bats at a low stem will chew it — and a dog that finds a fallen bead on the floor will eat it. ### Keep it out of reach anyway

Keep it out of reach anyway in how to care for string of pearls, because the risk is not worth the test. A pet that has never chewed a plant before will chew one eventually, and a child that has never eaten a bead before will eat one eventually — and the plant is flagged for both, per NC State Extension for pets and per UW–Madison Extension for sensitive skin. If a child eats a piece, call your pediatrician or your local poison control center. The plant is worth keeping, and the risk is worth managing — but only if the plant is placed where it cannot be eaten.

### The sap can irritate skin

The sap can irritate skin in how to care for string of pearls, and that is the second safety fact. UW–Madison Extension says the sap may cause skin irritation or rash in sensitive individuals. NC State Extension says the leaves and sap are slightly toxic, and to wear protective gloves to prevent skin irritation. Wear gloves when you prune or repot a string of pearls, wash your hands afterwards, and keep the sap out of your eyes. A person who handles the plant every day without gloves may develop a rash over time, and a person who gets sap in a cut will feel it sting. The sap is not dangerous to healthy skin in small amounts, but it is an irritant — so the simplest habit, gloves on and hands washed, is enough.

## The short version

The short version of how to care for string of pearls is five rules, and they fit on a card. Bright, indirect light, a few feet from an east- or south-facing window, with bright light all day and no harsh direct sun on the pearls. Water when the mix has dried through and the pearls are starting to shrivel, soak thoroughly, and empty the saucer. Keep it warm, 70 to 80°F in summer, with a cool rest of 50 to 60°F in winter, and inside before the first frost. Feed lightly at quarter strength once or twice in spring and summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Keep it in a gritty cactus mix in a shallow clay pot with holes, and renew it from cuttings when it dies back after a few years. Do those five, and the plant trails three feet long, with round, firm pearls a quarter inch across that get fuller every year — and the cuttings from one pruning become the next plant.

- Lifespan and renewal: NC State Extension says these plants tend to die back after several years, and to propagate by stem cuttings instead of trying to revive the older plant. No disagreement to show.
- Propagation: UW–Madison Extension says 3- to 4-inch stem tip cuttings, strip 3 to 4 leaves from the bottom, lightly cover the last few bottom nodes, mist the soil surface until roots are established; NC State Extension says 3- to 5-inch stem cuttings, covering a few of the nodes in the potting mixture. Planning figure: 3- to 5-inch cuttings, a few nodes covered, misted not soaked, in spring or summer.
- Pests: UW–Madison Extension says mealybugs or aphids may infest plants but otherwise they have few pest problems; NC State Extension says to monitor for aphids, whiteflies, gnats, ants, and mealybugs, and that yellow or graying leaves may be caused by insects.
- Pet safety: NC State Extension says the plant is toxic to both humans and pets, the leaves and sap are slightly toxic, avoid ingestion, wear protective gloves, monitor small children and pets; the ASPCA lists string of pearls, under its former name Senecio rowleyanus, among the toxic succulents — if ingested, it can cause stomach upset, predominantly vomiting, and lethargy — and notes it works well as a hanging plant, making it easier to keep out of reach; UW–Madison Extension says the leaves are slightly toxic, ingestion may cause vomiting or diarrhea, and the sap may cause skin irritation or rash in sensitive individuals. The guide states the ASPCA nuance explicitly: the ASPCA covers string of pearls in its succulents article, not as a standalone toxic-plants entry.
- String of pearls vs string of bananas: the common name "string of pearls" is sometimes confused with string of bananas, Curio radicans, a related trailing succulent with banana-shaped leaves; this guide covers Curio rowleyanus only, and the round, pea-sized bead with a window stripe is the check.

Almost every planting instruction is written relative to your last spring frost: "six weeks
before", "two weeks after". That one date unlocks the whole planting calendar, and it is the one
piece of information a website cannot supply for you.

## Where to look it up

**United States.** The NOAA regional climate centres publish freeze-date probabilities by
weather station, which is the primary source. Most state extension services turn those into a
local planting calendar, which is usually the easier document to read and is written for
gardeners rather than climatologists. Search for your state's extension service and "vegetable
planting calendar".

**Canada.** Environment and Climate Change Canada publishes climate normals by station,
including frost dates. Provincial agriculture ministries publish planting guides.

**United Kingdom.** The Met Office publishes regional climate averages. The Royal
Horticultural Society's grow-your-own guides give sowing windows by month for UK conditions,
which is often more directly useful than a date.

**Australia.** The Bureau of Meteorology publishes frost risk data, though across much of the
country frost is not the limiting factor and heat is.

**Anywhere.** A gardener who has grown vegetables locally for twenty years is a genuinely good
source, often better than a national map for your particular street.

## What the number means, and what it does not

An "average last frost date" is a statistic, and which statistic matters.

- A **50% date** means half of years have a frost after it. That is a coin toss, and planting
  tomatoes against it will lose you a crop every other year.
- A **10% date** means only one year in ten has a frost after it. This is the date to plant
  tender crops against.
- A **90% date** means nine years in ten have a frost after it — the earliest date worth risking
  anything tender at all.

If a source gives you a single figure without saying which, assume it is the 50% date and add
one to two weeks before planting anything that cannot take a frost.

## Your garden is not the map

Published dates are regional. Frost is local to a degree that surprises people.

**Cold air sinks.** A garden at the bottom of a slope or in a hollow collects cold air on still
nights and can be several degrees colder than one a short distance uphill. This is why frost
sometimes damages a valley-bottom garden and not the one above it.

**Walls and paving hold heat.** A bed against a south-facing wall in the northern hemisphere can
run a week or two ahead of an open bed in the same garden.

**Shelter and cover matter.** An open, exposed site frosts more readily than a sheltered one.
Overhead tree cover reduces radiative cooling and can prevent a light frost entirely.

**Water moderates.** Proximity to a large lake or the sea softens both extremes.

The practical consequence: treat the published date as a starting point, then adjust it from
what you observe. Keep a note each year of when you saw the last frost in your own garden. After
three or four years your own record is more useful than any map.

## Using it

Once you have a date, the offsets in seed catalogues become actual calendar dates. The
[planting date calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/) will do that for thirty crops at
once, giving you a date to start seeds indoors, a date to transplant out, a date to direct sow,
and a likely harvest window.

A few things worth keeping in mind while you use it:

**Hardy crops can go out well before the date.** Peas, spinach, lettuce, kale and radishes all
take a light frost. Some of them prefer to start in cold soil.

**Tender crops want margin.** Tomatoes, peppers, beans, cucumbers and squash all want to go out
after the date, and some of them want the soil warm as well as frost-free — a cold soil check is
a separate consideration from a frost check.

**Soil temperature is the other half of the question.** Beans and corn rot rather than germinate
in cold wet soil whatever the air is doing. A cheap soil thermometer settles it better than a
calendar.

## Get the fall date too

Your average **first fall frost** matters as much for the second half of the season. Together the
two give you the length of your growing season, which tells you whether a 110-day pumpkin is
realistic where you live.

For autumn sowings, the useful arithmetic runs backwards: days to maturity, plus a couple of
weeks of margin, subtracted from the first fall frost date.

## Watch the forecast anyway

An average is an average. Every year has weather in it, and a late cold snap arrives regardless
of what the map says. Keep fleece or an old sheet somewhere accessible through spring: covering
tender plants for one night is far cheaper than replacing them.

## The short version

Look up the freeze probabilities for your nearest station, note whether the figure is a 10%,
50% or 90% date, adjust for your own garden's slope, shelter and walls, keep a record of what you
actually see, and add a week or two of margin for anything tender. Then let the calculator turn
that one date into a whole season's dates.

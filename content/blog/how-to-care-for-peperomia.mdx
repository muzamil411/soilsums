A peperomia is the houseplant that fits on a desk and behaves like a succulent, and that combination is the first lesson in how to care for peperomia. It sits in a bright window with thick, glossy leaves in green, silver, red and cream, it stays under a foot tall for years, and it does all of that while asking for almost nothing: bright, indirect light, a mix that drains, and water only when the mix has dried. Then the same plant sits for a month dropping its leaves one by one in the hands of an owner who waters it every week, because how to care for peperomia is not the same as keeping a plant watered. A peperomia is a forest-floor plant from the shaded understory of Central and South America that stores water in thick, fleshy leaves, and it wants bright light filtered through a canopy, a loose pot it can drain from, and to be left mostly alone. Give it those, and it stays compact and glossy for years with leaves an inch or two across, each one a tiny water tank. Give it a dark corner, a deep pot without holes, and a weekly watering, and it drops its leaves from the soil up until only bare stems are left.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A peperomia that needs water does not hide it: the leaves go soft and droop, the stems feel light, and the pot feels light when you lift it. A peperomia that is over-watered looks worse — its leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and then they drop, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. A plant in too little light stays alive but stretches, its stems grow long and bare between leaves, and the new leaves come out small and far apart. A plant moved suddenly into strong direct sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much grows soft and rots faster. Learning to read those five signals — drooping leaves, dropping leaves, yellow leaves, stretch, and a pot that stays wet — is the whole of how to care for peperomia, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are Clemson Cooperative Extension and NC State Extension, with UF/IFAS Extension covering the baby rubber plant in Florida. If you bought the plant because someone told you it is impossible to kill, read the watering section first — because the one thing that kills it is the one thing owners do most. If you bought it for the patterned leaves and got plain green ones instead, read the light section first — because the leaf pattern answers to light, not to fertilizer.

## How to care for peperomia: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright, indirect light, and keep it out of direct sun.** The brightest spot you can spare, an east-facing window or a few feet back from a south or west window, with bright light all day and no direct sun on the leaves, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for peperomia, because the plant is a forest-floor plant that grows under a canopy in bright, filtered light — and a plant in a dim room stretches, goes leggy, and loses its pattern, while a plant moved into direct sun in one day scorches.

**Let the mix dry, then water thoroughly — and read the leaves.** Water only when the top inch or two of the mix has dried and the leaves are starting to soften, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates wet feet more than anything else: soggy soil rots the roots, and a pot that never dries out kills the plant faster than any drought. Drying between waterings is the second rule of how to care for peperomia.

**Keep it warm and steady, and never let it catch a cold draft.** Warm room air all year, a spot away from cold windows, exterior doors and heating vents, and outside only after the nights stay above 60°F. Cold and drafts are the third rule of how to care for peperomia — the plant has no frost tolerance at all, and a cold draft chills it just as surely as a cold night.

### The last two rules in how to care for peperomia

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A light feed in spring and summer, less often than a leafy houseplant gets, and nothing in autumn and winter — or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. Over-feeding does not make a peperomia grow faster; it makes it grow soft and rot faster. Feeding is the fourth rule of how to care for peperomia.

**Keep it in a loose mix, in a small pot with holes, and renew it from cuttings.** A well-drained houseplant or cactus mix in a pot only slightly larger than the one it came in, with drainage holes, repotted only every few years — and new plants from cuttings when the old one goes leggy, because a peperomia is a plant you renew, not one you rescue. Free drainage and a fresh start are the fifth rule of how to care for peperomia, and they protect the roots and the leaves for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for peperomia does three things a neglected one does not. First, it stays compact and glossy: a bushy plant eight to fifteen inches tall and wide, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, with thick, fleshy leaves in green, silver, red or cream — the look of a plant that is getting enough light and not too much water. Second, it holds its pattern: a watermelon peperomia keeps its silver stripes, a ripple peperomia keeps its wrinkled texture, and a variegated baby rubber plant keeps its cream edges, because the pattern answers to bright light, not to feeding. Third, it keeps its leaves: a plant that is watered on the leaf test, kept warm, and set in bright, indirect light holds its leaves for years, per NC State Extension, instead of dropping them one by one until only bare stems are left. The five rules in how to care for peperomia are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the forest floor, followed indoors.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for peperomia, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on watering depth. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the decision that shapes the plant more than any other in how to care for peperomia, because the plant answers to it in its leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to place peperomia where it will receive indirect light from east or south-facing windows, and that it can even tolerate somewhat low light near a large north-facing window. NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant prefers bright indirect light, and that it tolerates low light for several months without stress, especially the non-variegated cultivars. UF/IFAS Extension says Florida peperomia thrives in low-light conditions in both partial and deep shade. The spread is narrow and the direction is the same: bright, indirect light is best, direct sun burns, and shade is survived, not enjoyed. The planning figure is an east-facing window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, with bright light all day and no direct sun on the leaves. A peperomia in a north-facing window will survive and grow slowly; a plant in the same window will stretch, its stems growing long and bare between leaves, because the leaves need light to stay close together — and a plant moved into direct sun in one day scorches, because Clemson Cooperative Extension says if it gets direct sun, the leaves may scorch, and NC State Extension says sunlight that is too strong or direct will burn the leaves.

### The stretching warning

The first warning that the light is wrong is stretch, and it comes in one form in how to care for peperomia. A plant in too little light does not die — it stretches. The stems grow long and bare between the leaves, the new leaves come out small and far apart, and the plant looks thin and leggy instead of full and bushy. The fix is the same as the fix for yellow leaves: move it to a brighter spot, with no direct sun, and prune the bare stems back in spring, because the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length — but the cuttings from those stems root easily, and they are the next plant. The new growth will show whether the light is right — thick, firm leaves, close together on the stem — and the old bare stem is the record of the year in the dark.

### The scorch warning

The second warning is faster, and it is the one owners cause in how to care for peperomia. A plant moved suddenly into strong direct sun scorches across the leaves that caught the sun, and the patches do not heal — move it back a few feet from the window and leave the marked leaves alone, because they still feed the plant. Clemson Cooperative Extension says if it gets direct sun, the leaves may scorch. NC State Extension says sunlight that is too strong or direct will burn the leaves. UF/IFAS Extension says placing the plant in direct sunlight will burn the leaves, so be sure to stick to filtered light. That warning covers the south window in July, the balcony in a heatwave, and the plant set outside for the summer without a week in the shade first. A plant that sits in direct sun goes pale and scorched, and the scorched leaves drop — so give it bright light with a sheer curtain between the plant and the glass, and a week of shade before full sun outdoors.

### Grow lights when there is no bright window

A grow light is a fair answer when there is no bright window in how to care for peperomia, and the plant is honest about whether it is working. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the plant only needs indirect light, and that is the most shade-tolerant reading the sources give — but bright is the word, and a weak lamp in a dark room is not bright. A plain white LED or fluorescent tube a foot or two above the plant, on for twelve to fourteen hours a day as a planning figure, is enough for a peperomia — and the test is the same as the test for a window: new leaves close together on the stem, thick and firm and green. If the new leaves are still small and far apart, the light is still too weak, and the plant needs to be closer to the lamp or under a brighter one. A plant on a desk wants the light on the top of the pot as much as on the leaves, because the crown is where the plant grows from — so hang the lamp above the pot, not beside the trailing stems, and the crown stays full.

### Water

Water is the decision that kills the most peperomias in how to care for peperomia, and the sources are blunt about it — and they disagree on one point, which this guide shows honestly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia prefers to dry out between waterings, and that if it is overwatered, it will drop its leaves. NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant is intolerant of wet soil and of very dry soil, and that it prefers dry soil — and that overwatering is a big problem and causes the leaves to yellow, and it can lead to root rot. UF/IFAS Extension says when watering, less is more, and that Florida peperomia does better with slightly dry soil. The spread is real: one source says dry out between waterings, another says the plant is intolerant of very dry soil — and the finger test plus the leaves settle it. Push a finger into the mix. If it feels damp, wait. If the top inch or two feels dry and the leaves are starting to soften, water thoroughly until it runs out of the drainage holes, and empty the saucer after it drains.

### The dry-then-soak rule in how to care for peperomia

The rule is dry, then soak, and the order matters in how to care for peperomia. A little water every few days keeps the top of the soil wet and the bottom dry, and the roots in the middle rot while the top looks fine. A thorough soak, only when the mix has dried through the top inches and the leaves are starting to soften, wets the whole root ball and then lets it drain, and the roots get water and air in turn. That is why all three sources say the same thing in different words: water infrequently, let it drain, and never let the pot stand in water. A pot without a drainage hole breaks the rule at the start, because the water has nowhere to go — and a saucer left full after watering is the same mistake in slow motion. If the plant is in a pot without holes, repot it into one with holes before you change anything else, because no watering schedule fixes a pot that cannot drain.

### How often to water

The honest answer to how often is that the calendar does not know, and in how to care for peperomia the leaves do. In a warm, bright room in summer, a peperomia in a small clay pot will want water roughly every one to two weeks; in a cool room in winter, the same plant may go two to three weeks between waterings — and those are planning figures, not schedules, because the pot size, the light, the mix, and the season all change the answer. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to let the soil dry out between waterings, and that is the only seasonal rule the sources give in so many words. The leaf test is the schedule: check the leaves every few days, water only when they are starting to soften and the top of the mix is dry, and write down what you find for the first month, because after a month you will know your plant's rhythm in your room. A plant that is watered on the leaf test grows steadily; a plant that is watered every week because it is the weekend drops its leaves by spring.

### The drooping leaves

Drooping leaves are the plant's own watering signal in how to care for peperomia, and they are worth learning to read. A peperomia that is thirsty goes soft: the leaves droop and feel limp, the stems feel light, and the pot feels light when you lift it. Water it thoroughly and the leaves firm up again within a day. A peperomia that is over-watered looks different: the leaves go yellow and soft while the soil is still wet, and then they drop, and the pot feels heavy, and no amount of extra water helps it, because the roots are already rotting. The two kinds of soft need opposite fixes, and the soil test tells them apart: dry soil and drooping leaves means water; wet soil and dropping leaves means stop watering and let it dry. If the soil is wet and the plant is dropping leaves, take the plant out of the pot and look at the roots — white and firm is healthy, brown and mushy is rot, and the fix is to cut the rot away, take cuttings from the healthy stems, and start again in fresh, dry mix.

### Temperature

Temperature is the quiet rule in how to care for peperomia, because none of the three sources gives an indoor temperature range for the genus — and this guide does not invent one. What the sources do give is the outdoor limit. Clemson Cooperative Extension says plants can be taken outdoors for the summer after temperatures are consistently 60°F or higher at night, typically in May, and that they should be placed in deep shade to acclimate. NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant is intolerant of very drafty sites. UF/IFAS Extension says Florida peperomia can be grown in USDA Hardiness Zones 10 through 11. The planning figure is warm and steady room air all year, away from cold windows, exterior doors and heating vents, and outside only after the nights stay above 60°F — because a peperomia left outside in a cold snap chills, and the leaves drop and do not grow back.

### What to avoid: cold and drafts

Cold is not a slow decline in how to care for peperomia — it is a sudden injury. NC State Extension says the plant is intolerant of very drafty sites, and Clemson Cooperative Extension says to move plants outdoors only after the nights are consistently 60°F or higher. That warning covers the plant on a cold windowsill in winter, the plant by an exterior door that opens all day, and the plant left on a balcony in a cold snap. A plant that catches a cold draft drops its leaves, and the leaves do not grow back on the bare stem — so keep it away from cold windows in winter, and away from exterior doors, because a cold draft chills the leaves just as surely as frost does. If the plant has to spend a winter near a cold window, move it a few feet into the room for the winter — a few feet is often the whole difference.

### Humidity

Humidity is the rule the sources disagree on slightly in how to care for peperomia, and the spread is worth showing. ## Soil, potting, and repotting

Soil is the decision you make once and live with for a year in how to care for peperomia, and the sources agree on what it should do. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to plant peperomia in a well-drained houseplant or cactus potting mix. UF/IFAS Extension says it can grow in most soils, but prefers rich, organic soils, and needs well-drained conditions to prevent stem and root rot. The mix should hold a little water and then let go of it: a bagged houseplant mix on its own, or a general [potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) cut with [perlite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) or coarse bark, both work — and the test is what happens after you water. If the mix is still soggy three days after watering, it is too dense, and the plant will rot; if it dries out in a week in a bright room, it is right for this plant, because a peperomia wants to dry out. A mix that is dry on top in a few days and ready for water when the leaves soften is right, and that is the mix to keep.

### An airy mix that drains

The air in the mix matters as much as the water in how to care for peperomia, because the roots breathe — and this plant has fine, shallow roots that drown quickly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot, caused by overwatering, is the most common disease of peperomia. NC State Extension says overwatering is a big problem and can lead to root rot. UF/IFAS Extension says the plant needs well-drained conditions to prevent stem and root rot. The fix is in the mix, not in the watering can: perlite, coarse bark, or pumice mixed into the potting soil opens air pockets that stay open after watering, and a pot with drainage holes lets the excess water out. Clay is a good choice for a peperomia that is watered too often, because the clay breathes and the mix dries faster — and plastic is a poor choice for this plant, because it holds moisture longer and the fine roots sit wet. Either way, the drainage hole is non-negotiable — a pot without one is a pot that will rot the plant.

### The pot matters as much as the mix

The pot is the second half of the soil decision in how to care for peperomia, and the rule is simple: small, never large. A large pot holds a lot of wet mix that the small, shallow root system cannot drink, and that wet mix rots the roots — which is why a plant repotted into a large pot often dies faster than a plant left pot-bound. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia should be planted in a slightly larger pot than the grower's pot. The sign to act is unmistakable: leaves yellowing at the base, water sitting in the saucer, or a plant that stays wet for a week after watering because the pot is all mix and no roots. Choose a small pot one size larger than the last, with drainage holes, and fresh loose mix — and if the plant is trailing, choose a hanging basket or a pot on a high shelf, because a peperomia in a floor pot trails across the carpet and the leaves get trodden on.

### Repotting: when the plant fills the pot

Repotting is rare work for a peperomia in how to care for peperomia, and the plant tells you when it is time. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia does not need to be repotted every year, and that it can remain in the same pot for several years. A young plant in a small pot may need a new pot every two or three years; a mature plant may go longer in the same pot. The method is the same either way: water the plant a few days before, so the mix is barely damp and holds together; tip the plant out gently, because the stems are brittle and snap at a touch; shake off the loose mix; trim any brown, mushy roots with clean scissors; set the plant at the same depth in the new pot, with fresh loose mix around the roots; and water lightly, then leave it alone for a week. Do not feed a newly repotted plant for a month — the fresh mix already has enough food in it, and a stressed plant does not want fertilizer. A plant that drops a leaf or two after repotting is normal; a plant that drops many leaves was repotted too roughly, or into a pot too large, and the fix is to leave it alone in a bright, warm spot and let it settle — and to root the fallen stems as cuttings, because they are not waste.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the rule owners overdo in how to care for peperomia, and the sources are restrained about it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to fertilize peperomia only when it is actively growing in the spring and summer, and that a low-strength liquid fertilizer can be used occasionally during the growing season, or a slow-release granular fertilizer once per year. The spread is narrow — a light feed in spring and summer at the most, nothing in autumn and winter — and the reason is that the plant is a slow-growing forest plant that grows steadily on very little food. A peperomia in fresh loose mix, watered well and kept in bright, indirect light, grows steadily on almost no food, and the signs of too much food are worse than the signs of too little: soft, pale growth that rots at the first overwatering, and a white crust on the soil surface.

### The planning figure: lightly in spring and summer, nothing in winter

The planning figure this guide uses in how to care for peperomia is a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at quarter to half strength, once or twice in spring and summer, and nothing in autumn and winter — or a single slow-release granular feed in spring, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, or no feed at all, which the plant tolerates happily. That figure follows Clemson's "low-strength liquid fertilizer occasionally during the growing season," cut to quarter strength, because a full-strength feed on a dry peperomia burns the roots, so water the plant first, then feed, and never feed a plant that is wilted, newly repotted, or sitting in the dark in winter. A plant in lower light gets fed not at all — because it is barely growing, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that rot the roots. If the leaves go soft after a feed, stop feeding and flush the mix with plain water until it runs clear.

### What over-feeding does

Over-feeding does not make a peperomia grow faster in how to care for peperomia — it makes it grow soft and rot faster. The salts in fertilizer build up in the mix, the roots cannot take up water against the salt, and the leaves go soft while the plant sits in damp soil. The fix is to stop feeding, flush the mix with plain water, and wait: a plant that has been over-fed for a month needs a month of plain water before it starts growing again. Fresh mix has food in it already, and a plant repotted in spring needs no feed until the next year — which is why the planning figure starts feeding in spring, not at repotting. A peperomia that is fed lightly and watered well will outgrow a peperomia that is fed heavily and watered badly, every time.

## Pruning a plant that stays small

Pruning is the maintenance that keeps a peperomia full in how to care for peperomia, because a plant that is never pruned goes bare at the base. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to trim off old leaves when needed, and that to make plants look fuller, you should prune a stem above a leaf node so the plant will branch. A peperomia that grows for two years without pruning becomes a bare crown with long, thin stems and a tuft of leaves at the ends — and the bare stems will not sprout new leaves along their length, because the plant only grows from the tips and from the nodes. The fix is to cut the stems back, and the cuttings are not waste: they are new plants, and the pruned crown fills out from the nodes below the cut.

### Prune in spring, back to the crown

Prune in spring, when the plant is growing, and cut the long, bare stems back to the crown in how to care for peperomia. A clean cut with sharp scissors, just above a leaf node, and the stem below the cut will branch, often with two new shoots where there was one. Cut a long, bare stem back by a third, or by half if it is very bare, and the plant will fill out from the crown over the summer. Wear gloves, because the cut drips sap — and let the cut end dry before you handle it, because the sap keeps bleeding until it skins over. Do not prune in autumn or winter, when the plant is barely growing, because a cut made then heals slowly and the plant drops leaves instead of branching.

### What the cuttings become

The cuttings from a spring pruning are the next plant in how to care for peperomia, and they are not waste. A stem tip three to five inches long, cut just below a node, roots in damp mix in a few weeks — and a plant that is pruned every spring gives you cuttings every spring, which is how one peperomia becomes three. Lay the cutting in the mix with a few nodes covered, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and keep the mix barely damp — cover it with clear plastic, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, rather than soaking it, because a cutting in a wet mix rots before it roots. A cutting taken in spring or summer roots fastest, when the plant is growing — and a cutting taken in winter sits in the mix for months and often rots, because the plant is dormant and the mix stays wet.

## Propagation: cuttings, leaf cuttings, and division

Propagation is the reward for a healthy plant in how to care for peperomia, and Clemson Cooperative Extension lists three methods: stem cuttings, leaf cuttings, and water propagation. Cuttings suit a young plant with plenty of stem tips; they also suit an old plant that is going leggy, because the tips are still healthy even when the base is not. All methods work, and all are done in spring or summer, when the plant is growing — because a cutting taken in winter roots slowly, and a cutting in a wet winter mix rots before it roots.

### Stem cuttings

Take a stem three to five inches long, strip the leaves from the lower third, and set it in potting soil or a rooting medium in how to care for peperomia. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to cut a three- to five-inch piece of the stem from the parent plant, remove the leaves on the lower third of the cutting, dip the stem in rooting hormone, then insert it into potting soil or a rooting medium, cover with clear plastic, and place it in a location out of direct sunlight. Keep the cutting in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, keep the mix barely damp but not wet, and wait: roots form in a few weeks, and the first new leaves are the sign that the cutting has taken. A cutting taken in spring or summer roots fastest, when the plant is growing — and a single healthy stem gives you several cuttings, which is how one peperomia becomes the next one, and the one after that.

### Leaf cuttings

Leaf cuttings are the second method in how to care for peperomia, and they work because a peperomia leaf carries a whole plant in its stalk. Clemson Cooperative Extension says to remove a leaf with at least an inch of the petiole, the leaf stem, insert the leaf into a rooting medium, and new plants will form at the base of the petiole, which can then be removed and potted. Keep the leaf in a warm, bright spot with no direct sun, keep the medium barely damp, and wait: new plants form at the base of the petiole in a few weeks, and they are ready to pot up when they have a few leaves of their own. A leaf cutting is slower than a stem cutting, but it turns one leaf into a whole plant — and a leaf that falls off in handling is a cutting, so lay it in the mix and let it root.

### Water propagation and division

Water propagation is the third method in how to care for peperomia, and it suits the baby rubber plant best. Clemson Cooperative Extension says some species, such as the baby rubber plant, Peperomia obtusifolia, are easily propagated in water: take cuttings four to six inches long, remove the lower leaves, cut just below a leaf node, place the cutting in a small container where it will receive bright, indirect light, and pot it up after roots and new leaves form. Change the water weekly, and pot the cutting up as soon as the roots are an inch or two long, because roots grown in water are brittle and rot if they are left in the water too long. Division is the fourth method, and UF/IFAS Extension says divisions can also be used: when a plant has grown into a clump with several stems, tip it out of the pot in spring, pull the clump gently apart into pieces that each have roots and stems, and pot each piece up on its own. A divided plant is a full plant at once, not a cutting that has to grow — and a plant divided in spring fills its new pot by summer.

## Problems: leaf drop, yellow leaves, and bare stems

Problems in how to care for peperomia are almost always water, light, or cold, and the sources name the same three causes for nearly every symptom. Work through them in order — soil first, then light, then cold — and the plant usually answers within a week. A plant that is checked weekly never gets a bad problem; a plant that is never checked gets one that takes a month to clear.

### Leaf drop: the plant's loudest complaint

Leaf drop is the first problem in how to care for peperomia, and it is the one owners panic about most. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia prefers to dry out between waterings, and that if it is overwatered, it will drop its leaves. NC State Extension says overwatering is a big problem for the baby rubber plant, and that it causes the leaves to yellow and can lead to root rot. If the leaves are dropping from a plant in wet mix, it is overwatering: stop watering, let the mix dry out, and take cuttings from the healthy stems above the rot, because the rotted part will not recover. If the leaves are dropping from a plant in bone-dry mix after weeks without water, it is underwatering: water thoroughly and the firm leaves that remain will hold. A leaf that has already dropped will not reattach, but the stem above it is still alive, and a cutting from that stem is the next plant.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the second problem in how to care for peperomia, and they are the overwatering signal. NC State Extension says overwatering is a big problem, and that it causes the leaves to yellow and can lead to root rot. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root rot, caused by overwatering, is the most common disease of peperomia, and that after watering you should drain the water from the pot before placing it back in its saucer. If only the oldest leaves at the base are yellowing, that is the plant shedding old leaves, and no action is needed — the plant sheds its oldest leaves as it grows, and a leaf that has already turned yellow will not turn green again. If many leaves yellow at once, check the soil first: if it is wet, stop watering and let it dry out, and check that the pot has drainage holes and the saucer is empty; if the soil is dry and the leaves are yellow, the light is too low or the plant is catching a cold draft, so move it to a brighter, warmer spot. If the leaves are yellow and the undersides show white cottony masses, it is mealybugs — wipe them off and wash the plant, and repeat a week later.

### Bare stems and the leggy plant

Bare stems are the third problem in how to care for peperomia, and they are the light signal. A plant that grows for two years in low light goes bare at the base while the stem tips are still healthy — and that is the plant telling you it needs more light, not more water. The fix is to move it to a brighter spot, with no direct sun, and to prune the bare stems back in spring, because the bare stem will not sprout new leaves along its length — but the cuttings from those stems root easily, and they are the next plant. A plant that is pruned every spring never looks old, and a plant that is never pruned looks tired by year three. The bare stem is not a failure; it is the plant's record of the year in the dark, and the cuttings are the next generation.

### Mealybugs, spider mites, gnats, scale, and whiteflies

Mealybugs are the first pest in how to care for peperomia, and they are easy to see once you know what to look for: white, cottony masses in the leaf axils and on the undersides of the leaves. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the most frequently encountered pests are mealybugs, spider mites, [fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/), scale, and whiteflies. NC State Extension says the plant has no serious pest or disease problems, and to monitor for mealybugs, spider mites, and whiteflies. UF/IFAS Extension says some short-term issues include mites and leaf spot disease, and that these problems can be avoided with proper care. The fix is to check the undersides of the leaves every week, and to treat the whole plant, not just the leaf you can see — because the insects you cannot see are the ones that come back. Wipe the visible mealybugs off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, wash the plant with insecticidal soap, and repeat a week later, because the eggs hatch after the first treatment. Gnats mean the mix is staying wet — let it dry out, and the gnats leave on their own. Spider mites cluster on the undersides of dry leaves; wash them off with plain water and raise the humidity around the plant. A plant in a dry, bright room with a loose mix gets few pests — and a plant in a damp, dark room gets all of them.

### Fungal leaf spots: keep the leaves dry

Fungal leaf spots are the disease to watch for in how to care for peperomia, and the fix is in the watering, not in a spray. Clemson Cooperative Extension says fungal leaf spots can be prevented by ensuring the leaves stay dry when the plant is watered. Water the mix, not the leaves: pour slowly at the base of the plant, keep the leaves dry, and let the pot drain completely before you put it back in its saucer. A plant with spotted leaves should have the worst leaves removed, and the watering changed — because a plant that is watered from above in a humid room will spot again, and a plant that is watered at the base in a bright room will not. Do not mist a peperomia with spotted leaves, because wet leaves are what the fungus wants.

### Keep the leaves clean

Washing the leaves is the maintenance that keeps a peperomia healthy in how to care for peperomia, and the sources both name the habit in general terms. Dust blocks the light the leaves need, and a dusty plant grows slower than a clean one — so rinse the plant gently with plain water every few weeks, or wipe the leaves with a damp cloth, and let it drain completely before you put it back. Do not use leaf-shine sprays, because they clog the pores on the leaves, and do not handle the stems roughly when they are wet, because a wet stem snaps more easily than a dry one. A clean plant is a plant that can use its light, and a plant that can use its light grows — and every leaf that falls off in washing is a cutting, so lay it in the mix and let it root.

## Is peperomia poisonous to pets?

No, for the peperomias the ASPCA lists by name — and that is the safety fact to know in how to care for peperomia, because this is one of the few houseplant groups a pet owner can keep without worry. Clemson Cooperative Extension says most peperomia are considered safe for pets, and to consult the [ASPCA Poisonous Plants](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants) site for more information. The ASPCA lists the baby rubber plant, ripple peperomia, watermelon peperomia, red edge peperomia, ivy peperomia, Yorba Linda, trailing peperomia, vining peperomia and leather peperomia as non-toxic to cats and dogs. The honest reading is that the ASPCA lists peperomias species by species, not the whole genus at once — and its own list says it is not all-inclusive but a compilation of the plants it encounters most often, so a peperomia with no ASPCA entry has not been rated, which is not the same as being rated safe. The ASPCA also says the consumption of any plant material may cause vomiting and gastrointestinal upset for dogs and cats, so a pet that grazes on a peperomia may still get an upset stomach. The plant is worth keeping, and the risk is worth managing — but only if you have checked the name on the label against the list.

### Do not confuse the names

The names are the trap in how to care for peperomia, because three different plants share one name. Baby rubber plant is a peperomia, Peperomia obtusifolia, and the ASPCA lists it as non-toxic to cats and dogs. Rubber plant is usually Ficus elastica, a fig, and it has no ASPCA entry of its own — and the ASPCA entry filed under "Indian rubber plant" is Ficus benjamina, the weeping fig, which is toxic. The plant most shops call rubber plant, Ficus elastica, is not listed, which means unknown, not safe — and our own rubber plant guide rests its safety warning on NC State Extension and Clemson Cooperative Extension, not on an ASPCA entry, for exactly that reason. The second trap is the raindrop peperomia, which Clemson Cooperative Extension says is often mistaken for the Chinese money plant, Pilea peperomioides — a different plant in a different genus, with different care. The third trap is the cupid peperomia, which Clemson Cooperative Extension says closely resembles a [philodendron](/blog/how-to-care-for-philodendron/) — and a philodendron is toxic to cats and dogs, while the peperomia is not. Read the botanical name on the label, not the common name on the shelf, and the pet question answers itself.

### If a pet eats a piece anyway

If a pet eats a noticeable piece of any peperomia and seems unwell, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. A pet that chews a listed peperomia is not expected to be in danger, because the ASPCA says plants listed as non-toxic are not expected to be life-threatening — but a pet that eats a lot of any plant material can still vomit, and a peperomia with no ASPCA entry is an open question, not a cleared plant. Keep the label from the shop, so you can give the vet the botanical name, and keep the plant where a pet cannot graze on it all day. A peperomia on a desk is a safe plant; a peperomia on the floor where a puppy can chew it all afternoon is a plant that will be eaten, listed or not.

## The short version

The short version of how to care for peperomia is five rules, and they fit on a card. Bright, indirect light, an east-facing window or a few feet back from a south or west window, with bright light all day and no direct sun on the leaves. Water when the top inch or two of the mix has dried and the leaves are starting to soften, soak thoroughly, and empty the saucer. Keep it warm and steady, away from cold drafts, and outside only after the nights stay above 60°F. Feed lightly at quarter strength once or twice in spring and summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Keep it in a loose, well-drained mix in a small pot with holes, and renew it from cuttings when it goes leggy. Do those five, and the plant stays eight to fifteen inches tall, with thick, firm leaves in green, silver, red or cream that get glossier every year — and the cuttings from one pruning become the next plant.

### And a second plant for the same bright room

A second plant for the same bright room in how to care for peperomia is the [spider plant](/blog/how-to-care-for-spider-plant/), and the two belong together because they are the pair a pet owner can keep without worry. A spider plant wants bright, indirect light and watering when the top inch dries, per the same rules, and it hangs where a peperomia sits — and the ASPCA lists it as non-toxic to cats and dogs, just like the peperomia. A [pothos](/blog/how-to-care-for-pothos/) is the third plant for the same room, and it wants the same bright spot, and it trails where the cupid peperomia trails — but a pothos is toxic to cats and dogs, so it hangs high, out of reach, while the peperomia sits on the desk. An [aloe vera](/blog/how-to-care-for-aloe-vera/) is the fourth, and it wants brighter light still, and it stores its water in thick leaves where the peperomia stores it in its own. All four are easy, all four want bright light and a pot that drains, and two of the four are safe for pets — and all four reward the same five rules, with the watering turned down a notch for the succulents. Start with the peperomia, add the spider plant, and then the aloe vera, and the bright room fills itself.

## Key figure spreads to present honestly

- Watering: Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia prefers to dry out between waterings, and that if it is overwatered, it will drop its leaves; NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant is intolerant of wet soil and of very dry soil, and that it prefers dry soil; UF/IFAS Extension says when watering, less is more, and that Florida peperomia does better with slightly dry soil. Planning figure: let the top inch or two of the mix dry, then water thoroughly and drain; never soggy, never bone-dry for weeks — the leaves are the signal, not the calendar.
- Light: Clemson Cooperative Extension says indirect light from east or south-facing windows, that it can tolerate somewhat low light near a large north-facing window, and that direct sun may scorch the leaves; NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant prefers bright indirect light, tolerates low light for several months without stress, especially the non-variegated cultivars, and that sunlight that is too strong or direct will burn the leaves; UF/IFAS Extension says Florida peperomia thrives in low-light conditions in both partial and deep shade, and that direct sunlight will burn the leaves. Planning figure: east-facing window, or a few feet back from a south or west window, bright light all day, no direct sun; variegated forms need more light than plain green ones to hold their pattern.
- Temperature: none of the three sources gives an indoor temperature range for peperomia, so this guide states none. Clemson Cooperative Extension says plants can be taken outdoors for the summer after temperatures are consistently 60°F or higher at night, typically in May, and placed in deep shade to acclimate; NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant is intolerant of very drafty sites; UF/IFAS Extension says Florida peperomia can be grown in USDA Hardiness Zones 10 through 11. Planning figure: warm, steady room air all year, away from cold windows, exterior doors and heating vents, and outside only after the nights stay above 60°F.
- Humidity: NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant prefers medium to low relative humidity; Clemson Cooperative Extension says it will benefit from a humidifier in the winter. Planning figure: average household air is fine; a humidifier in a dry winter room helps but is not required; do not mist the leaves, because Clemson Cooperative Extension says fungal leaf spots can be prevented by ensuring the leaves stay dry when the plant is watered.
- Feeding: Clemson Cooperative Extension says to fertilize peperomia only when it is actively growing in the spring and summer, that a low-strength liquid fertilizer can be used occasionally during the growing season, and that another option is a slow-release granular fertilizer once per year. Planning figure: balanced liquid at quarter to half strength once or twice in spring and summer, or one slow-release granular feed in spring, and nothing in autumn and winter, or no feed at all.
- Size: Clemson Cooperative Extension says peperomia typically grows 8 to 15 inches tall and wide; NC State Extension says the baby rubber plant grows up to 2 feet tall; UF/IFAS Extension says Florida peperomia grows 6 to 12 inches tall and spreads 1 to 2 feet wide. Planning figure: most indoor peperomias 8 to 15 inches tall and wide; the baby rubber plant reaches up to 2 feet.
- Flower spikes: Clemson Cooperative Extension says the flower spikes are produced when the plants are actively growing in the spring and summer, that they are considered unattractive and are said to resemble a rat's tail, and that removing them enables the plant to focus its energy on leaf production; NC State Extension says the inflorescence is a cream-colored spike; UF/IFAS Extension says the miniscule green-white flowers grow along reddish, leafless stems about 5 inches long, appear periodically throughout the year when grown outside, and rarely appear when grown as a houseplant. Planning figure: spikes in spring and summer, snip them off if you dislike them; the plant is grown for its leaves.
- Propagation: Clemson Cooperative Extension says for stem cuttings, cut a three- to five-inch piece of the stem, remove the leaves on the lower third, dip the stem in rooting hormone, insert it into potting soil or a rooting medium, cover with clear plastic and place it out of direct sunlight; for leaf cuttings, remove a leaf with at least an inch of the petiole, insert it into a rooting medium, and new plants will form at the base of the petiole; for water propagation, some species, such as the baby rubber plant, are easily propagated in water with cuttings four to six inches long; UF/IFAS Extension says cuttings root easily, and that divisions can also be used. Planning figure: stem cuttings 3 to 5 inches in spring or summer, leaf cuttings with at least an inch of petiole, water propagation for the baby rubber plant, division for clumping plants.
- Pests: Clemson Cooperative Extension says the most frequently encountered pests are mealybugs, spider mites, fungus gnats, scale, and whiteflies, and that root rot from overwatering is the most common disease; NC State Extension says the plant has no serious pest or disease problems, and to monitor for mealybugs, spider mites, and whiteflies; UF/IFAS Extension says some short-term issues include mites and leaf spot disease. No disagreement to show beyond emphasis.
- Pet safety: Clemson Cooperative Extension says most peperomia are considered safe for pets, and to consult the ASPCA Poisonous Plants site; the ASPCA lists the baby rubber plant, ripple peperomia, watermelon peperomia, red edge peperomia, ivy peperomia, Yorba Linda, trailing peperomia, vining peperomia and leather peperomia as non-toxic to cats and dogs, and its own list says it is not all-inclusive but a compilation of the plants it encounters most often; the ASPCA also says the consumption of any plant material may cause vomiting and gastrointestinal upset for dogs and cats. The guide states the ASPCA nuance explicitly: the ASPCA lists peperomias species by species, not the whole genus, and a peperomia with no ASPCA entry has not been rated. The guide also states the name traps explicitly: baby rubber plant is a peperomia and is non-toxic, while the rubber plant most shops sell, Ficus elastica, has no ASPCA entry of its own, and the ASPCA entry filed under "Indian rubber plant" is Ficus benjamina, the weeping fig, which is toxic; the raindrop peperomia is often mistaken for the Chinese money plant, Pilea peperomioides; and the cupid peperomia closely resembles a philodendron, which is toxic to cats and dogs.
- Peperomia vs philodendron: the cupid or vining peperomia, Peperomia serpens, has heart-shaped leaves and closely resembles a philodendron, per Clemson Cooperative Extension; this guide covers peperomias only, and the thick, fleshy, succulent-like leaf is the check — a philodendron leaf is thin and papery by comparison, and a philodendron is toxic to cats and dogs while the peperomia is not.

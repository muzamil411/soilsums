The harvest is in the basket, and now the question is how to store potatoes so they are still good in February instead of sprouting in the pantry by December. Storage is where most homegrown potatoes are lost: the digging went fine, the curing was skipped, and a warm, lit shelf did the rest. The good news is that the rules are simple, and university extension services agree on them. This guide to how to store potatoes follows the order the sources recommend: sort, cure, set up the spot, pick a container, and check them through the winter.

If you have not dug them yet, our [potato harvest guide](/blog/when-to-harvest-potatoes/) covers when the vines, the skin test, and the weather say it is time. What follows assumes the potatoes are already out of the ground.

## How to store potatoes: the four conditions

Every storage decision comes back to four conditions, and the numbers below are the ones extension services publish — not folk rules:

- **Temperature: 40 to 46°F.** Oregon State University Extension says to gradually lower the storage temperature to 40–46°F for table use, and Iowa State University Extension gives 40°F with 90 to 95 percent relative humidity. That range is the whole game: warm enough to avoid sweetening, cool enough to hold sprouting back.
- **Humidity: high, around 90 percent.** Potatoes are about 80 percent water, per Oregon State, so high humidity is essential to prevent shriveling. Iowa State puts the storage target at 90 to 95 percent relative humidity.
- **Darkness: total.** Light turns potatoes green, and green tubers are unfit for consumption (see below).
- **Airflow: steady but gentle.** Ventilated containers, not sealed ones — potatoes need to breathe, and trapped moisture is what starts rot.

Get all four right and mature, late-season potatoes last seven to eight months, per Oregon State. Miss on temperature alone and the same potatoes last two to three months. The table shows what each temperature band does, so you can judge the spot you actually have:

| Storage temperature            | What to expect                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 40–46°F, 90–95% humidity, dark | Seven to eight months for mature, late-season potatoes (Oregon State)                             |
| Above 46°F                     | Storage life drops to two to three months, with sprouting and shriveling (Oregon State)           |
| Above 50°F                     | Sprouting begins in two to three months (Iowa State)                                              |
| Below 38–40°F                  | Sugar buildup and a sweet taste; fried potatoes turn darker and oilier (Oregon State, Iowa State) |

## Sort before you store anything

Storage only works for sound tubers, so the first job after digging is sorting — not curing, not boxing. Sorting is the unglamorous first step in how to store potatoes well: the bin only preserves what goes into it sound. Oregon State University Extension is blunt about this: before long-term storage, sort and discard any injured or diseased tubers, because damaged tubers do not store well and may spread decay to their neighbors.

What to set aside for the kitchen instead of the bin:

- Potatoes with cuts, bad spots, or signs of disease — use them within the first month, per Oregon State.
- Any tuber that feels soft or smells off.
- Green potatoes — light exposure during or after digging has already started the greening process.

The sorting rule is also the reason to handle potatoes gently from the moment they leave the ground. Every nick is a door for rot, and curing can only heal minor wounds — it cannot save a tuber that was gashed by the fork. If you are still deciding when to dig, the skin test in our [potato harvest guide](/blog/when-to-harvest-potatoes/) is worth doing first: skins that rub off easily mean the crop needs another week in the ground.

## Cure homegrown potatoes before storing

Curing is the step most people skip, and it is the step that makes the difference between potatoes that last to spring and potatoes that rot by Christmas. Curing heals the small wounds from digging and thickens immature skins, and both effects make the tubers more resistant to decay later. That is the whole reason to cure, and it is why any guide to how to store potatoes puts curing before boxing.

Iowa State University Extension gives the precise window: cure the tubers at 50 to 60°F and 85 to 90 percent relative humidity for two weeks. Oregon State University Extension gives a shorter version of the same job — 7 to 10 days in a dark, well-ventilated space with moderate temperatures and high humidity. Either way, the conditions are the same shape: warmer than storage, humid, dark, and ventilated.

In practice, curing looks like this:

1. **Spread the potatoes in a single layer** in a dark, well-ventilated place — a garage, shed, or covered porch works if the temperature holds in the 50 to 60°F range.
2. **Do not wash them.** Brush off loose soil; rinse only if the soil is fine, sticky clay, and then let the tubers dry completely before they go anywhere near storage, per Oregon State.
3. **Keep them out of the light** while they cure. Light exposure during cleaning and curing starts the greening you are trying to avoid.
4. **Wait the full two weeks.** The skin should feel set and papery, not thin and easy to rub off.
5. **Sort again after curing.** Any tuber that developed a soft spot or a bad smell during the cure goes to the kitchen, not the bin.

Store-bought potatoes skip this section entirely — they were cured before they reached the shop, so they go straight into storage conditions. The same applies to any potatoes you bought as food rather than dug yourself.

## Set up the storage spot

Once cured, the potatoes need to move somewhere cooler — gradually, not all at once. The second half of how to store potatoes is the spot itself: temperature, humidity, and darkness have to hold for months, not days. Oregon State University Extension says to lower the storage temperature gradually to 40–46°F for table use after curing, rather than dropping freshly cured tubers straight into the coldest spot available.

The spots that work are the ones that hold that temperature through the winter:

- **An unheated basement or cellar corner**, away from the furnace and any hot-water pipes. The classic choice, and the one most likely to sit in the 40s.
- **An insulated garage**, against the wall shared with the house so it does not freeze. Watch for the coldest nights — a garage can dip below 38°F, and Oregon State warns that below 38°F sugar builds up and sweetens the tubers.
- **A refrigerator set to 40–46°F.** Oregon State lists a refrigerator set to that range as a suitable storage space, and for a small harvest it is the most reliable option. Check the temperature where the potatoes actually sit with a thermometer, not just the dial setting.

Before you load the spot, put a cheap thermometer there for a week and read it morning and evening. Basements are rarely one temperature: one corner can read 58°F by the stairs and 46°F in the far corner, and the difference between those two spots is the difference between two months and seven. If the only spot you have runs above 50°F, the potatoes will still keep — Iowa State says to expect sprouting in two to three months at those temperatures — so plan to eat that batch first and keep the longest-storing varieties for the coldest corner.

Freezing is the hard stop. Potatoes stored where temperatures can fall below freezing will crack and turn to mush when they thaw, so a spot that freezes on the coldest nights is worse than a spot that runs a little warm.

## Pick a container that breathes

The container matters because potatoes need humidity and airflow at the same time, which sounds contradictory until you see it: humid air around the tubers, but no standing moisture on them. The container is the smallest decision in how to store potatoes — the temperature of the room matters far more than the box — but the wrong container still ruins a good spot. Oregon State University Extension recommends perforated plastic bags or well-ventilated containers to maintain humidity, and the same principle covers every container that works.

Good choices:

- **Paper bags or burlap sacks**, loosely closed — breathable, dark, and cheap.
- **Cardboard boxes with holes punched in the sides**, lined with newspaper between layers.
- **Bushel baskets, mesh bags, or open crates**, covered with a cloth or a lid that blocks light without sealing the air out.
- **Perforated plastic bags**, per Oregon State — the holes are the point.

What not to use: sealed plastic bags, lidded tubs with no ventilation, or anything airtight. Sealed containers trap moisture, and trapped moisture is rot. And whatever container you choose, cover it or keep it in a dark corner — even a little light causes greening, so a ventilated box in a lit room still needs a cloth over it.

One more rule for the shelf: keep potatoes away from ripening fruit. Iowa State University Extension warns not to store potatoes with apples or other fruit, because ripening fruit give off ethylene gas, which promotes sprouting of the tubers. Give the potatoes their own shelf, away from the fruit bowl.

## Check them every week

Storage is not a job you finish once. A weekly check takes five minutes and is part of how to store potatoes for the long term, not an optional extra — it is what stops one bad tuber from taking the whole bin. Damaged tubers can spread decay, which is exactly why Oregon State has you sort them out before storage and use the questionable ones within the first month.

The weekly routine:

1. **Lift the lid and smell.** Rot has a sour smell you will catch before you see it.
2. **Feel for soft spots.** Any tuber that has gone soft or slimy comes out at once.
3. **Look for sprouts.** A few short sprouts early on are normal at the warm end of the range; snap them off and use those potatoes soon. Long, pale sprouts mean the spot is too warm — eat that batch first.
4. **Check for green.** Any tuber that has greened goes in the trash, not the pot — Oregon State says to discard potatoes with excessive greening.

If you find rot, remove the affected tuber and the ones touching it, and check the rest twice as often for the next few weeks. Rot spreads fastest in warm, still air, so a bin that starts going bad is also a sign to check the ventilation and the temperature.

## What goes wrong, and what to do about it

Most failures in how to store potatoes trace back to one of the four conditions, and each one has a distinct signature:

- **Sprouting.** The spot is too warm, or the potatoes are stored near ripening fruit. Iowa State says sprouting starts in two to three months above 50°F, and ethylene from apples or other fruit speeds it up. Move the bin somewhere cooler, keep fruit away, and use the sprouted potatoes first.
- **Greening.** Light is reaching the tubers. Cover the container, move it into a darker corner, and discard any potato with excessive greening — Oregon State is clear that green tubers are unfit for consumption, because the green often signals solanine.
- **Shriveling.** The air is too dry. Potatoes are about 80 percent water, and Oregon State says high humidity is essential to prevent shriveling. A ventilated container in a damp basement holds humidity better than an open crate in a dry, heated room.
- **Rot.** A damaged tuber went in, or moisture sat on the skins. Sort harder next time, make sure tubers are completely dry before storage, and never store in a sealed container.
- **Sweet taste and dark frying.** The spot is too cold. Iowa State says potatoes stored below 40°F develop a sugary, sweet taste, and Oregon State puts the floor at 38°F — below that, sugar buildup makes fried potatoes darker and oilier. Iowa State adds that sugary potatoes can be restored to their natural flavor by holding them at room temperature for a few days before use.

## How long will they last? A worked comparison

The figures above give a concrete answer if you run them through two identical harvests, and the comparison is the practical payoff of learning how to store potatoes by the numbers rather than by habit. Take 20 pounds of mature, late-season russets — the thick-skinned type Oregon State says stores longest — cured for two weeks at 50 to 60°F, then split into two spots:

- **In a basement corner holding 40 to 46°F**, dark, at about 90 percent humidity: Oregon State says seven to eight months in good condition. That is a harvest dug in October still usable in May.
- **In a pantry holding 55°F**, lit, in an open crate: Iowa State says sprouting begins in two to three months, and Oregon State says storage life above 46°F drops to two to three months with sprouting and shriveling. That is the same potatoes, eaten by January or lost to sprouts.

The potatoes are identical; the temperature is the whole difference. That is why the thermometer check before loading the spot is worth more than any container choice — a ventilated box cannot fix a warm room, and a cold room forgives a plain paper bag.

Variety matters too, and it is the one factor you decide at planting time rather than at storage time. Oregon State notes that red potatoes and thin-skinned types generally have shorter storage life than thick-skinned varieties like russets, and that late-maturing varieties typically store better than early types. If you grow for winter storage, the late, thick-skinned varieties earn their place — our [potato growing guide](/crops/potato/) covers the variety choice and the growing side in full.

## Mistakes that cut storage life short

The short list of what goes wrong when people learn how to store potatoes — all of it avoidable:

- **Skipping the cure.** Freshly dug skins are thin and wounded; without two weeks at 50 to 60°F they rot early.
- **Washing before storing.** Surface moisture promotes rot. Brush off the soil, and wash only when you are ready to cook.
- **Storing in a warm, lit pantry.** Above 50°F the tubers sprout in two to three months, and light greens them.
- **Sealed containers.** Trapped moisture is rot. Ventilated, always.
- **Storing with fruit.** Ethylene from apples and other ripening fruit pushes sprouting, per Iowa State.
- **Skipping the sort.** One injured tuber can spread decay through the bin.
- **Letting them freeze.** A spot that drops below freezing on cold nights ruins the whole batch.
- **Saving sprouted tubers for seed.** Oregon State warns against planting sprouted or shriveled tubers the following spring because of disease, especially viruses — plant only certified, healthy seed potatoes.

Get the cure, the temperature, the dark, and the airflow right, and check the bin once a week, and that is how to store potatoes the extension-backed way: the harvest you dug in autumn is still dinner in spring.

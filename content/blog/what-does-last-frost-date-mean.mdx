Your last frost date is a **probability, not a guarantee**. The commonly published figure is the
date after which there is a 50% chance of no further frost — which means that in half of all
years, frost arrives later than it. It is a planning anchor for counting backwards from, not a
day on which it becomes safe to plant tomatoes.

## The number is a coin flip

This is the part almost every frost-date lookup tool leaves out.

Climate records are used to work out, for each date in spring, the probability that no further
frost will occur after it. Publishers then pick a threshold. The commonest choice is 50%, and
that is what "average last frost date" usually means.

| Threshold | What it means                              | Use it for                       |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| 50%       | Frost is later than this in half of years  | Counting back for seed starting  |
| 30%       | Frost is later in about three years in ten | Hardy transplants                |
| 10%       | Frost is later in about one year in ten    | Tender crops with no backup plan |

If a source gives you several dates, the later one is the safer one. If it gives you one, assume
it is the 50% figure and treat it accordingly: fine for scheduling, not for gambling a tray of
tomato seedlings on.

## How do you find your last frost date?

Enter your zip or postal code into a frost-date lookup, then sanity-check it against local
knowledge.

The commonly used sources — the Old Farmer's Almanac, the National Gardening Association at
garden.org, Dave's Garden — all draw on the same underlying climate normals, so they broadly
agree. Where they differ is which probability threshold they publish and how far your nearest
weather station is from you.

Outside the US, national meteorological services publish the equivalent. In the UK the last air
frost is typically late April to mid-May in the south and into June in upland areas.

Then ask a neighbour who has gardened locally for a decade. They will know whether your street
runs early or late, which no map will tell you.

## Why your frost date differs from your neighbour's

Because the published date comes from a weather station that may be miles away, and frost is
intensely local.

| Feature                      | Effect on last frost                       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Bottom of a slope or valley  | Later — cold air drains downhill and pools |
| Top or side of a slope       | Earlier — cold air flows past              |
| Beside a large body of water | Earlier — water moderates temperature      |
| Urban area                   | Earlier — buildings and paving hold heat   |
| Open, exposed, north-facing  | Later                                      |
| Next to a south-facing wall  | Earlier, sometimes by two weeks            |

A frost pocket at the bottom of a garden can be a full two weeks behind the top of it. This is
worth knowing before you site a bed, not after you lose a crop.

## What to do with the date once you have it

Count backwards from it. Every sowing instruction on a seed packet is expressed as weeks before
or after this date, which is what makes it useful.

| Crop     | Start indoors  | Transplant out | Direct sow     |
| -------- | -------------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| Tomato   | 6 weeks before | 1 week after   | —              |
| Pepper   | 8 weeks before | 2 weeks after  | —              |
| Lettuce  | 8 weeks before | 3 weeks before | 4 weeks before |
| Cucumber | 3 weeks before | on the date    | 1 week after   |
| Carrot   | —              | —              | 2 weeks before |
| Pea      | —              | —              | 4 weeks before |

## The date is only half the instruction for warm crops

For anything warm-season, the calendar can say go while the soil is still too cold for the seed
to do anything but rot.

| Crop                               | Also wait for                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Pea                                | Soil at 45°F (7°C)                                    |
| Sweet corn                         | Soil about 60°F (16°C), or 65°F (18°C) for supersweet |
| Cucumber, summer and winter squash | Soil about 70°F (21°C)                                |
| Watermelon                         | Soil above 65°F (18°C)                                |
| Pepper, eggplant                   | Night temperatures reliably above 50°F (10°C)         |

A cheap soil thermometer settles this in seconds and is the single most useful five dollars in a
seed-starting setup. Our [cucumber guide](/crops/cucumber/) and
[pepper guide](/crops/pepper/) carry these conditions alongside the dates.

## Frost, freeze, and what survives which

A **frost** is ice forming on surfaces. It can happen on a clear, still night when the air
thermometer reads a couple of degrees above freezing, because surfaces radiate heat to the sky
and cool below air temperature.

A **freeze** is the air itself at 32°F (0°C) or below.

Tender crops — tomato, pepper, basil, squash, beans — are damaged by the first and killed by the
second. Hardy ones — kale, spinach, leeks, parsnips — take a hard freeze without complaint, and
several taste better for it.

That is why our planting offsets for cool-season crops are negative numbers. [Lettuce](/crops/lettuce/)
goes out three weeks _before_ the last frost date, and [spinach](/crops/spinach/) is sown five
weeks before it.

## Do not let the date make you impatient

The commonest seed-starting mistake is starting too early, and a 50% date encourages it.

A tomato sown eight weeks before the last frost, then held indoors for two more because the
weather turned, is worse at transplanting than one sown on time — leggy, root-bound and checked.
Nothing about a large indoor seedling produces an earlier harvest.

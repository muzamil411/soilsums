The honest answer to this question is not a number, and every page that gives you one is inventing
it. We went looking for a published table of germination days by grass species across Penn State,
Missouri, Iowa State, UMass and NC State. **It does not exist.**

What extension services publish instead is one day count, a set of temperature ranges, and a great
deal about watering. That turns out to be more useful, because it tells you what to change.

## The one figure anybody publishes

University of Missouri Extension's G6700 says **Kentucky bluegrass requires two to three weeks to
germinate in the spring.** That is the only numeric germination statement in any of the
publications we read.

Everything else is comparative. UMass Extension calls Kentucky bluegrass **among the slowest to
establish**, and says perennial ryegrass **offers more flexibility for later planting** — a
statement about relative speed with no day count attached. NC State, Iowa State and Penn State
describe establishment the same way.

That is not an oversight on their part. It is a judgement that a day count would mislead, and once
you see what actually governs the wait, it is obviously the right call.

## What actually governs the wait

**Soil temperature, and whether the seed stays damp.** Not the species, and not the number on the
bag.

UMass gives the ideal ranges for cool-season grass as roughly **55 to 65°F soil temperature for root
growth** and roughly **65 to 75°F air temperature for shoot growth**. Those two ranges are the whole
mechanism. Above them growth slows because the grass is heat-stressed; below them it slows because
the biochemistry does.

The consequence is the thing most readers need to hear: **seed sown into cold soil does not fail and
does not run late. It waits.** It sits, intact, until the soil reaches a temperature it can work
with, and then it germinates on its own schedule. Dormant seeding — covered below — is that
behaviour used deliberately.

So a reader who has been told "7 to 14 days" and is standing over a bare patch on day 15 has been
given the wrong instrument. The useful measurement is not the calendar. It is a soil thermometer,
which costs very little and answers the question directly.

## Watering, which is where most sowings are actually lost

If there is one thing to take from this page, it is this. **The commonest reason grass seed fails is
that it dried out once.**

A germinating seed absorbs water, swells, and begins to grow. If the surface dries while that is
happening, the seedling dies, and no amount of watering afterwards brings it back. It is not
recoverable, and it is invisible — you do not see a failure, you see nothing happening, and you
conclude the seed was bad.

Iowa State's guidance is specific and worth following literally: after the initial watering,
**irrigate frequently and lightly, usually daily, and several times daily in windy, sunny weather.**

Read that again, because it inverts normal watering advice. Established lawns want infrequent deep
watering, which drives roots down. A germinating seedbed wants the opposite: little and often,
because the seed is in the top half-inch of soil and that is the part that dries first. Wind and sun
matter more than air temperature, which is why the guidance escalates to several times a day rather
than by the clock.

Two practical consequences:

- **Do not sow before a week you cannot water.** A sowing left to a forecast that turns dry is a
  sowing thrown away.
- **Light matters more than volume.** A heavy soak washes seed into drifts and puddles; a fine spray
  keeps the surface damp without moving anything.

## When to sow

Three publications, broadly agreeing, and the agreement is worth more than any of them alone.

**Late summer into early autumn is the best window for cool-season grass.** Missouri gives
**August 25 to October 10**. Iowa State gives **mid-August to mid-September**. The reasoning behind
both: the soil is still warm from summer while the air is cooling, which is exactly the combination
those two temperature ranges describe, and annual weeds that would compete with seedlings are
finishing rather than starting.

**Spring is the second-best option**, and Iowa State puts it at **early April to mid-May**. It works,
but a spring sowing has to establish before summer heat arrives and has to compete with germinating
weeds. It is why Missouri's spring seeding rates are higher than its autumn ones — more seed to
offset a harder start.

**Dormant seeding is the third route**, and Iowa State gives it as **late November through early
February**. The seed goes down into cold ground, does nothing at all through the winter, and
germinates when the soil warms in spring. It is the temperature mechanism used on purpose, and it
suits ground that is too wet to work in spring.

Two things are worth drawing out of that.

**The spread on perennial ryegrass and tall fescue is large.** Penn State gives 4 to 5 lb per 1,000
square feet for perennial ryegrass where Missouri gives 7 in autumn and 10 in spring — a factor of
two at the extremes. Averaging those would produce a figure neither service publishes. They are
recommendations written for different states, and the difference is real rather than an error.

**Missouri's seasonal split is the insight most pages miss.** Sowing in spring calls for more seed
than sowing in autumn, for the same grass, because a higher proportion of a spring sowing fails.
Penn State does not split its figures that way; Missouri does, and it is the more actionable form.

## What to do if nothing has come up

In order of likelihood:

1. **Check the soil temperature.** If it is below about 55°F, the seed is waiting rather than
   failing. Nothing you do will speed that up.
2. **Think back over the watering.** Did the surface go dry at any point, even for an afternoon in
   wind? That is almost certainly the answer, and the only fix is to sow again.
3. **Check the depth.** Grass seed needs light contact with soil, not burial. Seed raked in too deep
   germinates and exhausts itself before reaching the surface.
4. **Check how long it has actually been.** Two weeks is not long. Kentucky bluegrass in spring is
   published at two to three weeks by Missouri, and that is under good conditions.
5. **Only then suspect the seed.** Old seed germinates poorly, and a bag stored in a hot shed over
   summer is worth replacing — but it is the last thing to blame, not the first.

Resowing into unchanged conditions gives the same result. Work out which of the first three it was
before you buy more seed.

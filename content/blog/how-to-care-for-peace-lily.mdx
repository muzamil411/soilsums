A peace lily is the houseplant that forgives almost everything except two things: a dry root ball and a dark corner. It droops dramatically when it is thirsty, perks up within hours of a drink, and flowers in low light where most houseplants only survive. That forgiveness is why it is one of the most common houseplants in the United States — and why so many owners are surprised when it stops flowering, browns at the tips, or droops and does not recover. The truth is that how to care for peace lily is simple, but it is not the same as caring for the succulents and cacti on the same windowsill. A peace lily is a tropical rainforest-floor plant, not a desert plant, and it wants steady moisture, warm air, and bright light without direct sun. Give it those, and it flowers for years.

### The plant talks

The most useful thing to know first is that the plant talks. A peace lily that needs water does not hide it: the whole plant droops, leaves and all, and then stands back up soon after watering. A plant that is over-watered droops too — but its soil is wet, and no amount of extra water helps it. A plant in too little light stays green and alive but never flowers. A plant in direct sun scorches, and a plant that is fed too much burns at the leaf tips. Learning to read those four signals — droop, no flowers, scorch, brown tips — is the whole of how to care for peace lily, and this guide works through each one with the figures from US university extension sources.

Every figure here comes from a US university extension source, verified on the source page itself, and where the sources disagree, the spread is shown and one planning figure is chosen. The main sources are Clemson Cooperative Extension, NC State Extension, UF/IFAS Extension, and South Dakota State University Extension, with the ASPCA corroborating the toxicity figures. If you bought the plant for its white flowers, the flowering section covers that. If you bought it because someone told you it cleans the air, read the honest note in the flowering section first.

## How to care for peace lily: the five rules

Five rules cover the whole plant — the skill is doing all five together.

**Give it bright indirect light, and never direct sun.** A few feet back from an east-facing window, or near a north-facing window with good ambient light, is the sweet spot. Light is the first rule of how to care for peace lily, because the plant tolerates low light but only flowers in bright light — and a plant that never flowers is a plant in too little light.

**Keep the soil moist, never soggy, never bone dry.** Water thoroughly when the top inch of soil feels dry, and empty the saucer after it drains. The plant hates both extremes: soggy soil rots the roots, and a dry root ball makes the whole plant droop. Even moisture is the second rule of how to care for peace lily.

**Keep it warm, and away from cold drafts.** A room at 68 to 85°F during the day, a few degrees cooler at night, and never below 60°F. Cold air is the third rule of how to care for peace lily — the plant is tropical, and a cold windowsill in winter slows it and can damage it.

### The last two rules in how to care for peace lily

**Feed lightly, and only while it is growing.** A balanced liquid fertilizer at one-quarter strength every six to eight weeks from spring to summer, and nothing in autumn and winter. Over-feeding burns the leaf tips, and a plant that is fed too much looks worse than a plant that is never fed at all.

**Repot rarely, one size up, in spring.** The plant likes to be slightly pot-bound, so repot only every one to two years, when roots show through the drainage holes, into a pot just one or two inches wider. A pot that is too large stays wet and rots the roots. Repotting restraint is the fifth rule of how to care for peace lily, and it protects the roots for years.

### What the five rules buy you

A plant kept to these rules in how to care for peace lily does three things a neglected one does not. First, it flowers: bright indirect light, steady moisture, and a cool autumn with short days are what make a peace lily produce its white spathes, and a plant that gets all three can flower year after year, per UF/IFAS Extension. A plant in a dim corner with erratic watering stays green and never flowers, which is the most common complaint about this plant — and the fix is light, not fertilizer. Second, it stays green to the tips: even watering, the right light, and light feeding stop the brown tips that are the second most common complaint. A plant whose leaves are green edge to edge is a plant whose owner is watering and feeding correctly, and the leaves show it. Third, it grows steadily without drama: a plant that is watered on the finger test, kept warm, and repotted rarely puts on a few inches a year and reaches 2 to 3 feet in a few years, per Clemson Cooperative Extension — a floor plant you can keep, not a plant you have to replace every spring. The five rules in how to care for peace lily are not fussy houseplant trivia. They are the rainforest floor, followed indoors.

## It is not a true lily — know what you bought

The first thing to get straight in how to care for peace lily is what the plant actually is, because the name misleads in two ways. It is not a lily — it is a *Spathiphyllum*, a member of the arum family (Araceae), which also includes caladiums, elephant ears, anthuriums, and callas, per Clemson Cooperative Extension. And the white "flower" is not a flower at all, but a modified leaf called a spathe, sheltering a cream-colored spike — the spadix — that carries the tiny true flowers. UF/IFAS Extension says the inflorescence is a white, hood-shaped spathe sheltering a cream or white spadix covered with bisexual flowers, and that from an ornamental point of view the spathes and spadices are called flowers rather than the tiny true flowers on the spadix. That distinction matters twice: once for safety, because a peace lily does not carry the kidney poison of true lilies, and once for flowering, because what you are trying to grow is a leaf, and the plant makes it when it is happy — not on a fixed calendar.

### A rainforest-floor plant

In the wild the plant grows on the shaded floor of tropical rainforests in Central and South America, and that background explains almost everything about how to care for peace lily. It is a tropical, evergreen plant native to Central and Southern America, per South Dakota State University Extension, with approximately 40 different species — Clemson Cooperative Extension says over 40 species, and NC State Extension says about 60 species in the genus. The spread between those counts is a reminder that the genus is large and still being sorted out, and that the plant in your pot is almost certainly a hybrid cultivar, not a wild species. The cultivars are the result of a selective breeding program by the tropical plant industry, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and they are what you actually choose between in the shop.

### What it looks like in the pot

What you actually see in the pot is a clump of glossy, dark green, oval leaves that narrow to a point, rising directly from the soil on short stalks, with white spathes standing above them on longer stalks. Clemson Cooperative Extension describes sturdy, easy to grow plants with glossy, dark green oval leaves that narrow to a point, and South Dakota State University Extension describes the same: glossy, dark-green, oval-shaped leaves coming to a narrow point, with a white bloom that is long lasting, showy, unique in shape, and slightly fragrant. The leaves rise directly from the soil — there is no woody stem — which is why a peace lily grows as a widening clump rather than a tree, and why it is divided, not pruned, when it gets too big. The white spathes are long-lasting: NC State Extension says the white blooms change to pale green after 10 days and then remain lovely for at least another month, and UF/IFAS Extension says individual blooms may last for a month or so once the plant is in interior conditions. Clemson Cooperative Extension says the same — the long-lasting, pure white flowers will turn a pale green as they age. A spathe that turns green is not sick; it is old, and it can be trimmed off at the base.

### The numbers that matter before you buy

Three numbers decide how good a plant will be in how to care for peace lily, and all three are visible in the shop. The first is the cultivar, because size is decided at the garden center, not by your care. Clemson Cooperative Extension gives the range: most peace lilies grow between 1 to 4 feet tall and wide, but a few cultivars may get as large as 6 feet tall and wide. Its dwarf 'Wallisii' reaches 12 to 15 inches, its 'Little Angel' 12 to 15 inches, its 'Starlight' 12 to 18 inches, its 'Jetty' 19 to 24 inches, its 'Allison' around 24 inches, its 'Mauna Loa Supreme' 2 to 3 feet, and its 'Sensation' 6 feet tall and wide. NC State Extension gives the same scale in plainer terms: while peace lilies can grow up to 6 feet tall, they more typically reach 3 feet high and wide in an indoor setting. UF/IFAS Extension says cultivars range in height from 12 inches to 4 feet. Buy the size for the spot: a dwarf 'Wallisii' for a shelf, a standard plant for a table, and 'Sensation' only if you have floor space for a 6-foot plant.

### Check the leaves and the soil

The second is the condition of the leaves: they should be glossy and green to the tips, with no browning, no yellow edges, and no droop. Brown tips in the shop mean the plant has already been stressed — over-fed, under-watered, or sun-scorched — and those tips will not recover. The third is the soil: it should be moist, not wet and not dry, and the pot should have drainage holes. A plant sitting in a saucer of water in the shop is already on its way to root rot, and a plant in bone-dry soil has already wilted once. Buy the glossy, green, right-sized plant in moist soil, and the rest of how to care for peace lily is straightforward.

## Light, water, and temperature while it grows

Light, water, and temperature are the three daily decisions in how to care for peace lily, and the sources agree on all three more closely than they do on feeding. Get these three right and the plant forgives small mistakes elsewhere. Get one of them wrong for a whole winter and the plant shows it in the spring, leaf by leaf.

### Light

Light is the most important of the three, and the most commonly gotten wrong — because the plant survives bad light for so long that the mistake looks harmless. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies will tolerate low light conditions but grow best in bright indirect light, and they should not be placed in direct sun, as the heat will scorch the foliage. NC State Extension says the same in fewer words: peace lilies prefer partial to deep shade, and do not place plants in direct sun. UF/IFAS Extension agrees: bright, indirect light, no direct sun. The planning figure this guide uses is bright indirect light — a few feet back from an east-facing window, or near a north-facing window with good ambient light, or a south or west window with a sheer curtain — and no direct sun at any time of day. That range satisfies all three sources, and it is the light that makes the plant flower.

### The delayed warning

The warning that matters most is the delayed one. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies are one of the few foliage plants that will flower in low light — but "will flower" is not "will flower well." A plant in a dim corner flowers rarely or not at all, and the owner concludes that peace lilies simply do not flower indoors, when the plant is actually asking for a brighter spot. South Dakota State University Extension calls the peace lily the "closet plant" because it does well in low-light conditions — and that reputation is the trap. A plant that survives in a closet is not a plant that thrives in one — and thriving, not surviving, is the point of how to care for peace lily. If your plant has not flowered in a year, move it closer to the window first, before you change anything else. That move is the cheapest fix in how to care for peace lily, and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

### The droop is the signal

The next habit in how to care for peace lily is really a watering habit, and it is the one that makes this plant easier than most: the droop tells you when to water. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that if the soil is allowed to dry out excessively, the plant will wilt, and the leaves and edges will turn yellow. South Dakota State University Extension says drooping or wilting leaves happen when the plant is not receiving enough water. UF/IFAS Extension is blunter: the most common problem with peace lilies is underwatering, which causes the entire plant to severely wilt — in the trade it is called a "crash." The droop is the plant's alarm, and it is a reliable one: a peace lily that droops in dry soil is thirsty, and it usually stands back up within hours of a thorough watering. Learn to water on the droop, or better, just before it — when the top inch of soil feels dry — and the plant will rarely droop at all.

### A droop in wet soil means the opposite

But the droop has a second meaning, and it is the one that kills plants. A peace lily that droops in wet soil is not thirsty: it is drowning. Clemson Cooperative Extension says root and stem diseases are the most common problem and are usually associated with over-watering practices. A plant that has been watered every day, or that sits in a saucer of water, rots from the roots up, and the first sign is the same droop as drought — which is why so many owners respond to over-watering by watering more. Check the soil before you water, every time. If it is dry, water. If it is wet and the plant droops, let it dry out. That finger test is the daily habit in how to care for peace lily: it takes five seconds, and it prevents both kinds of watering mistakes at once.

### Water

Watering is where most peace lilies are lost, and the loss usually comes from one of two opposite mistakes: too much water, given too often, in a pot that cannot drain — or too little water, given too rarely, until the plant crashes. The watering rule from Clemson Cooperative Extension is the one to follow exactly in how to care for peace lily: soil should be kept moist but not soggy and allowed to dry out slightly between waterings, and after watering, never let the plant sit in a saucer of excess water, as this will lead to root rot issues. Two halves, both required: water thoroughly, and then remove the excess. A thorough watering means water until it runs out of the drainage holes, so the whole root ball is wet, not just the top inch. Removing the excess means emptying the saucer, so the pot never sits in water.

NC State Extension gives the same rule with a useful extra detail: keep the soil moist, but not soggy; allow it to dry between waterings, and watering from below works very well for peace lilies. Watering from below — setting the pot in a basin of water for twenty minutes, then letting it drain — wets the whole root ball evenly and is a good method for a plant that has dried out badly. UF/IFAS Extension says the same in its own words: keep moist, but do not allow the plant to sit in water. And Clemson Cooperative Extension adds a small detail that matters: when watering, it is best to use room temperature water. Cold water straight from the tap is a shock to a tropical root ball, and room-temperature water is simply kinder.

### How often to water

The planning figure this guide uses is simple: check the top inch of soil with a finger, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry — roughly once a week in a warm, bright room in summer, stretching to every two weeks or longer in a cool room in winter. That range satisfies all the sources, and it keeps the soil evenly moist without ever staying soggy. It is a planning figure, not a schedule from any source: no extension source gives a fixed watering calendar for peace lilies, because the right interval depends on the pot, the room, and the season. A small pot in a warm, bright room dries faster than a large pot in a cool, dim one, and a plant in summer drinks faster than the same plant in winter. That finger test is the schedule in how to care for peace lily, and it is the only one that works in every house.

### The pot and the saucer

A decorative cache pot with no drainage is a slow death for this plant — water collects at the bottom where you cannot see it, and the roots sit in it for weeks. If your plant came in a foil-wrapped pot or a cache pot with no holes, take it out, water it in the sink, let it drain fully, and put it back. The second mistake is the saucer that is never emptied: Clemson Cooperative Extension warns twice about it, and it is worth repeating, because it is the single most common cause of root rot in this plant. Water thoroughly, wait ten minutes, and empty the saucer. The thorough watering itself, and the empty saucer, are the rules in how to care for peace lily, and they come straight from Clemson Cooperative Extension.

### Temperature

Temperature is the easiest of the three to get right, because the range the plant wants is the range a house already has. The sources give a small spread, and it is worth showing honestly. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies prefer warm daytime temperatures between 68 and 85°F and nighttime temperatures about 10°F cooler, and to try to avoid temperatures below 60°F, as cold damage may occur. NC State Extension says the plant enjoys warm conditions between 68 and 85 degrees Fahrenheit during the day and no more than a 10-degree drop at night, and that cold and drafty conditions, specifically temperatures of 40 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit, will slow growth considerably, while extended periods below 40 degrees Fahrenheit can damage leaves, stems, and roots. UF/IFAS Extension says the temperature preference is warm and tropical, 75 to 85°F, that active growth is reduced at or below 50°F, and that some cultivars can be injured at 35°F. UF/IFAS Extension's production guide adds that exposure to 45°F could cause chilling injury in some cultivars, and that delayed or reduced growth occurred when plants were exposed to 52°F for five days, with a preferred production range of 70 to 90°F.

The planning figure this guide uses is 68 to 85°F during the day, with nights about 10°F cooler, and never below 60°F indoors. That range satisfies all the sources, and it is ordinary indoor temperature in a house that is not overheated. That is why temperature is the easiest of the three decisions in how to care for peace lily: the house already provides it, and the job is simply not to put the plant somewhere colder than the room itself.

### What to avoid: cold and heat

Two things matter more than the exact number. The first is cold: do not put the plant next to a drafty window, an exterior door, or an air-conditioning vent that blows directly on it. Clemson Cooperative Extension says not to place peace lilies where heat or air-conditioning vents blow directly on the plants, as hot, cold, or drafty exposures will damage the plant. A chill that a hardy houseplant would ignore can damage this tropical plant, and the damage shows as browning and yellowing leaves. The second is heat: do not put the plant next to a radiator or a heating vent. Hot, dry air is the combination that browns the leaf tips fastest, and a plant that sits over a heating vent in January will show it by February. Warm and steady is the temperature rule in how to care for peace lily, and a room at 70 to 75°F away from vents and drafts is very nearly ideal.

### Humidity

Humidity is the winter problem, and the one most owners never think about. The peace lily evolved on a humid rainforest floor, and the dry air of a centrally heated home in winter is genuinely hostile to it. NC State Extension names low humidity as one of the three causes of brown leaf tips, alongside excessive direct sunlight and underwatering. South Dakota State University Extension agrees: brown tip leaves are caused by excessive light, over-fertilizing, low water, or low humidity. No extension source gives a humidity percentage for peace lilies, so this guide gives none — the honest statement is that average household humidity is usually enough, and that brown tips in a dry heated room point to low humidity among the other causes. A humidifier running near the plant, or a pebble tray under the pot — a saucer filled with water and pebbles, with the pot sitting on the pebbles above the water, not in it — helps in a dry room. Misting the leaves helps briefly and is not a substitute. Get the humidity right and the leaf tips stay green through the heating season; get it wrong and the tips brown from the edges inward, which is the most common winter complaint in how to care for peace lily.

## Soil, potting, and repotting

The soil rule in how to care for peace lily is short: rich, moist, and free-draining, in a pot with holes. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies will flourish in almost any well-drained potting mix. NC State Extension says to use a potting mix high in organic matter. UF/IFAS Extension says the soil preference is a rich, organic peat- or bark-based bagged potting soil. ### A rich mix that drains

The planning figure this guide uses is a rich, well-drained potting mix: ordinary potting soil with extra perlite or bark for drainage, or a peat-based mix with perlite. Free drainage is the foundation of how to care for peace lily in a pot, because every watering rule in this guide assumes the excess can leave. Our [potting soil calculator](/tools/potting-soil-calculator/) works out how much mix a pot this size needs, and our guide to [how to make potting soil](/blog/how-to-make-potting-soil/) shows the mix itself — a recipe of potting soil, perlite, and compost that holds moisture evenly without staying soggy. The comparison in [perlite vs vermiculite](/blog/perlite-vs-vermiculite/) explains why perlite is the right lightener here: it drains and keeps air in the mix, and this plant needs drainage more than it needs moisture retention — the mix should hold water like a wrung-out sponge, not like a swamp.

### Repotting: one size up, in spring

Repotting is the part of how to care for peace lily that surprises people, because the answer is to do it rarely. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies like to be slightly pot-bound, but it is time to repot if roots start growing through the drainage holes or circle on top of the soil surface, and to choose a container that is just one to two inches larger than the original pot, because using too large of a pot may keep the potting mix too wet, leading to root rot. Repotting can be done any time of the year, but the plant will recover quicker if repotted during the growing season, and the root system should be loosened gently before placing the plant in the new container. NC State Extension agrees that the plant requires a large pot and should be kept somewhat pot-bound, and says to re-pot, when necessary, in February or March.

The planning figure this guide uses is every one to two years, in spring — February or March, per NC State Extension — only when the roots circle the pot or poke out of the drainage holes, or when the soil dries out very fast after watering.

### Go one size up, and keep the root ball intact

When you do repot, go only one pot size up — an inch or two wider — and keep the plant at the same depth, with the root ball mostly intact. Do not break the root ball apart or wash the old soil off: the roots resent disturbance, and a rough repotting can set the plant back a whole season. South Dakota State University Extension adds a useful diagnostic: older leaves will yellow if the plant has not been repotted in a while, which is a sign that the old soil is exhausted and the roots have filled the pot. A plant that is repotted gently, rarely, and one size up will keep growing steadily for years; a plant that is repotted often and roughly will droop and yellow every time. That restraint is the repotting rule in how to care for peace lily.

## Feeding: lightly, and only while it is growing

Feeding is the one point where the sources genuinely differ, so here is the spread. Clemson Cooperative Extension says these plants need very little fertilizer, and that when fertilizing, to use a balanced, liquid houseplant fertilizer, such as 20-20-20, every six to eight weeks during the spring and summer growing season, mixed at one-quarter of the recommended strength. NC State Extension says to fertilize at one-quarter of the recommended strength and flush the soil between applications of fertilizer, because over-fertilizing and salt build-up can burn the leaf tips and roots. UF/IFAS Extension says to apply a low dose of liquid fertilizer according to manufacturer instructions three times per year. UF/IFAS Extension's production guide says that using a 20-20-20 water soluble fertilizer solution with a nitrogen level at 50 parts per million once a month, or maintaining a soluble salts range of 0.8 to 1.2 decisiemens per meter, should be adequate under interior conditions — and warns to avoid over-fertilizing, as high soluble salts will burn leaf tips and margins.

The planning figure this guide uses is a balanced liquid fertilizer at one-quarter strength every six to eight weeks from spring to summer, and nothing at all in autumn and winter, with an occasional flush — watering thoroughly with plain water and letting it drain — between feedings. That range satisfies all the sources: it is the middle of the spread, it is dilute rather than full-strength, and it stops when growth stops. All the sources agree on the season, and on stopping when growth stops, which is the part of how to care for peace lily that matters most: a plant that is fed only while it is growing never accumulates the salts that burn its leaf tips.

### What over-feeding does

A peace lily that puts on a few inches a year does not need much food, and a plant that is fed through the winter, when it is barely growing, accumulates salts in the soil that burn the roots and the leaf tips. Clemson Cooperative Extension says overfertilizing peace lilies can cause the leaf tips and roots to burn and turn brown. NC State Extension says the same: over-fertilizing and salt build-up can burn the leaf tips and roots. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer at one-quarter strength, feed only when the soil is already moist, never onto dry soil, and skip a feeding if the plant has just been repotted or is showing stress. If the tips are already brown from over-feeding, flush the soil with plain water — a thorough watering that runs out of the drainage holes, repeated once — and stop feeding until spring. That light hand is the feeding rule in how to care for peace lily, and it is the cheapest of the five rules to follow: a single bottle of balanced liquid feed lasts years at this rate.

## Getting it to flower

Flowering is the reason most people buy a peace lily, and it is also the most misunderstood part of how to care for peace lily — because the plant flowers on its own schedule, not yours, and because the shop plant you bought was probably forced into bloom before you ever saw it. Understanding that is the key to not being disappointed.

### Why a young plant does not flower

The first reason is youth. Clemson Cooperative Extension says lack of flowering usually occurs when plants are less than a year old or are growing in insufficient light. A plant under a year old simply has not matured enough to flower, and no amount of fertilizer will change that. The second reason is light, and it is the common one: a plant in a dim corner stays green and alive but never flowers, because flowering takes energy the plant is not making. Move it to bright indirect light, give it a season, and the spathes usually follow. NC State Extension says peace lilies have no specific bloom time but flower freely when they are happy, and UF/IFAS Extension says that with proper plant care, they may produce blooms year after year. "When they are happy" is doing the work in that sentence: bright light, steady moisture, warm air, and light feeding, all together, for months.

### The short-days trigger

The third reason is the season. UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County says peace lilies naturally set flowers in response to short days, long nights, and lower temperatures. UF/IFAS Extension's production guide says most cultivars only flower from January to August, and that growers can program flowering with a gibberellic acid spray — a one-time foliar spray that makes flowers appear in 9 to 12 weeks. That is why the plant you bought in the shop was in full bloom in November: a grower sprayed it to force flowers for the retail season. Your plant at home will not be sprayed, so it will flower on the natural cycle — short days and cooler nights in autumn and winter, with blooms through spring and summer — and it will flower less reliably than the forced shop plant did. That is normal, not a failure, and it is worth knowing before you decide the plant is broken. Give it a cool autumn with short days, bright light through the winter, and light feeding in spring, and the spathes will come.

### What to do when it will not bloom

If the plant is over a year old, in bright indirect light, watered evenly, and still has not flowered, check the season first: a plant bought in November may not flower again until spring. Then check the feeding: a plant that has never been fed may be short of nutrients, and a light feeding in spring can help. Then check the pot: a plant that has been in the same pot for three years, with exhausted soil, may need repotting in spring before it has the energy to flower. And then wait. A peace lily that is healthy and green but not flowering is a plant that needs time, not a rescue. Do not buy "bloom booster" fertilizer or move the plant into direct sun to force flowers — direct sun scorches the leaves, and heavy feeding burns the tips, and a scorched, burned plant flowers less, not more. The patience is the flowering rule in how to care for peace lily: bright light, a cool autumn, light feeding in spring, and a season of waiting.

### A note on the air-cleaning claim

You may have bought the plant because someone told you it cleans the air. UF/IFAS Extension notes that NASA praised peace lilies in its Clean Air Study for their ability to remove formaldehyde, benzene, and carbon monoxide from interior air. That study is real — and it was done in small sealed chambers, not in a lived-in room with doors, windows, and ventilation. A pot or two on a windowsill does not meaningfully clean a room's air, and no houseplant does. Buy the peace lily for its glossy leaves and its long-lasting white flowers, which are real, and not for air cleaning, which is not. That honesty is part of how to care for peace lily: the plant is worth having for what it actually does, and it does that very well.

### Dividing it to make more plants

A peace lily grows as a clump, and when the clump fills the pot, you can divide it into two or more plants — which is also the only pruning this plant ever needs. UF/IFAS Extension says propagation can be accomplished through division, and that basal shoots can be separated from mother plants and potted in container substrates. The time to divide is when you repot, in spring: take the plant out of its pot, gently pull or cut the clump into sections, each with its own leaves and roots, and pot each section in fresh mix in its own pot, one or two inches wider than the section needs. Water thoroughly, keep the new plants warm and out of direct sun, and expect them to droop for a week while they settle — that is normal, and they recover. A divided plant will not flower in its first year, per Clemson Cooperative Extension's rule that young plants do not flower, so do not divide a plant you want in bloom this spring. Divide the plant you want more of, not the plant you want flowers from, and that timing is the dividing rule in how to care for peace lily.

### A worked example: one plant, bought today

Here is the whole first year as a single story, for a plant bought on Wednesday, 18 November 2026. The plant is a standard peace lily, 15 inches tall in a 6-inch pot, glossy and green to the tips, with two white spathes already open. You set it near an east-facing window, where it gets bright indirect light and no direct sun, and you mark Sundays for the finger test: push a finger an inch into the soil, and water thoroughly only when it feels dry, emptying the saucer every time. You do not feed it at all: it is November, and feeding waits for spring. The spathes turn pale green after ten days or so, per NC State Extension, and stay lovely for at least another month — you trim each one off at the base when it fades.

Spring comes, and the feeding starts: a balanced liquid fertilizer at one-quarter strength every six to eight weeks, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and nothing in winter. The plant grows a few inches over the year, puts out new leaves from the center of the clump, and by next autumn it has had a cool season with short days — the trigger UF/IFAS Extension Charlotte County describes — and new spathes appear in spring. You repot it in February or March 2028, per NC State Extension, one pot size up, keeping the root ball mostly intact. After a few years of that routine, the plant is 2 to 3 feet tall and wide, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, and it has flowered every year. That is the whole story of how to care for peace lily: a finger in the soil, a bright spot without sun, a warm room, a little food in summer, and patience.

## Troubleshooting

Most peace lily problems have one of four causes — water, light, feeding, or cold — and the fix is usually to correct the cause and wait, because the plant recovers slowly and a damaged leaf never heals. A browned leaf tip will not turn green again, so troubleshooting in how to care for peace lily is mostly about protecting the leaves that are still healthy.

### Drooping

Drooping is the most common complaint, and the most useful symptom, because it points at the cause — if you check the soil first. South Dakota State University Extension says drooping or wilting leaves happen when the plant is not receiving enough water. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that if the soil is allowed to dry out excessively, the plant will wilt, and the leaves and edges will turn yellow. UF/IFAS Extension calls underwatering the most common problem and says it causes the entire plant to wilt severely. If the soil is dry, the fix is a thorough watering now, and the plant usually recovers within hours — that fast recovery is the sign you read it right. If the soil is wet and the plant still droops, do not water again: the roots may be rotting, and the fix is to let the soil dry out, improve the drainage, and water only when the top inch is dry. A plant that droops every few days in dry soil needs a bigger pot or more frequent checks; a plant that droops in wet soil needs less water, not more. That soil check is the first step in every drooping diagnosis in how to care for peace lily.

### Brown leaf tips

Brown tips are the second most common complaint, and they have four possible causes — which is why they are so often misdiagnosed. NC State Extension says brown leaf tips are caused by excessive direct sunlight, underwatering, or low humidity. South Dakota State University Extension adds over-fertilizing to the list. Clemson Cooperative Extension says overfertilizing can cause the leaf tips and roots to burn and turn brown, and that the edges of the leaves can turn brown by overfertilization or by excessive drying. Check the four causes in order. First, the light: if the plant sits in direct sun, move it back from the window. Second, the water: if the soil is dry, water thoroughly and start the finger test. Third, the feeding: if you have been feeding every week, or at full strength, stop, flush the soil with plain water, and feed only at one-quarter strength every six to eight weeks in spring and summer. Fourth, the humidity: if the heat has been on for weeks and the air feels dry, set up a humidifier or a pebble tray. Do not cut the brown tips off the leaves — the brown part is dead, but trimming it leaves a blunt end that looks worse and does not help the leaf. Leave the tips alone, fix the cause, and judge the plant by its new growth, not by the browned tips. That patience is the troubleshooting habit in how to care for peace lily.

### Yellow leaves

Yellow leaves are the most ambiguous symptom, because they point in two opposite directions. South Dakota State University Extension says yellow leaves will occur if the plant is overwatered or underwatered, and that older leaves will yellow if the plant has not been repotted in a while. Clemson Cooperative Extension says that if the soil is allowed to dry out excessively, the leaves and edges will turn yellow, and that a magnesium deficiency can cause plants to become stunted, with leaf tissue between the veins turning yellow while the veins remain green. Check the soil first: if it is wet, the plant is over-watered — let it dry out and empty the saucer. If it is dry, the plant is under-watered — water thoroughly. If the soil is fine and only the oldest, lowest leaves are yellowing, the plant may simply need repotting, per South Dakota State University Extension, or the oldest leaves may be dying naturally as the clump renews itself. A few yellow lower leaves on an otherwise healthy plant are normal; a whole plant turning yellow at once is a watering problem, and the soil tells you which one. That soil check is the yellow-leaf rule in how to care for peace lily.

### No flowers

A plant that stays green and never flowers is almost always short of light or too young — and the fix is time and a brighter spot, not fertilizer. Clemson Cooperative Extension says lack of flowering usually occurs when plants are less than a year old or are growing in insufficient light. Move the plant to bright indirect light, give it a cool autumn with short days, feed it lightly in spring and summer, and wait a season. A plant that was forced into bloom at the shop with a gibberellic acid spray, per UF/IFAS Extension, may take a year to flower again on its own cycle — that is normal, and it is not a sign that anything is wrong. Do not move the plant into direct sun to force flowers, and do not feed it heavily: both damage the leaves and make flowering less likely, not more. The flowering section above covers this in full, and the short version is the same: bright light, a cool autumn, light feeding, and patience. That waiting is the hardest lesson in how to care for peace lily, and it is the one most owners need to hear.

### Pests

The peace lily is generally a healthy plant indoors, and pest trouble is uncommon — but there are four pests to watch for. Clemson Cooperative Extension says peace lilies have few insect pests, but fungus gnats, mealybugs, spider mites, and scales can be problematic, and to examine the leaves often for evidence of insect infestations. NC State Extension says mealybugs are probably the most common insect indoors, and that cleaning the leaves regularly with a damp cloth to remove dust will help the plant's appearance and remove some pests that tend to gather under the bottom of the leaves. South Dakota State University Extension lists the same pests: scales, mealybugs, and fungal gnats, which all feed on the peace lily. Clemson Cooperative Extension also says the wide glossy leaves accumulate dust, and to wipe the leaves regularly with a damp cloth to clean the foliage. That wipe is the pest rule in how to care for peace lily: check the undersides of the leaves when you water, wipe off any mealybugs or scale you find with a damp cloth, and let the soil dry slightly between waterings to discourage fungus gnats — our guide to [how to get rid of fungus gnats](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-fungus-gnats/) covers that pest in full. Root and stem diseases follow over-watering, per Clemson Cooperative Extension, so the watering rule is the disease rule too: moist, never soggy, and never sitting in water.

## Is peace lily poisonous to pets?

Yes — and that is the safety fact to know before you buy one in how to care for peace lily, because the plant is so common that it sits within reach of pets in millions of homes. The ASPCA lists the peace lily (*Spathiphyllum*) as toxic to dogs and cats, because it contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, and lists the signs as oral irritation, intense burning of the mouth, tongue, and lips, excessive drooling, vomiting, and difficulty swallowing. Clemson Cooperative Extension says calcium oxalate crystals are present in the plant cells and, if consumed, can cause toxicity in pets and children, most commonly burning of the mouth, throat, and tongue. South Dakota State University Extension calls it mildly toxic and advises keeping small children, cats, and dogs from chewing on the plant. NC State Extension rates the plant as a medium-severity poison for cats, dogs, horses, and children.

### Not a true lily — and that changes everything

But a peace lily is not a true lily, and that distinction is the difference between a frightening afternoon and a catastrophe. True lilies and daylilies can cause kidney failure in cats, and even a few petals or a sip of vase water can be fatal within days. A peace lily does not contain that poison: its crystals irritate the mouth, the signs usually settle on their own, and the ASPCA says many of these exposures can be managed at home, with no long-term effects expected. If a pet chews a peace lily, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 — and do not wait to see if the signs pass, because swelling at the back of the throat, though uncommon, needs a vet. The comparison with its neighbors on the same windowsill is worth making: the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) is non-toxic, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) is mildly irritating if eaten, the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) is toxic, and the [Norfolk Island pine](/blog/how-to-care-for-norfolk-island-pine/) is non-toxic. The peace lily sits in the middle of that row — not the most dangerous plant in the house, and not a safe one to leave within reach of a chewing cat.

### Keep it out of reach anyway

Toxic does not mean the plant has to leave the house — it means the plant has to live where pets cannot chew it. Put a peace lily on a high shelf or a plant stand, out of reach of a cat that jumps and a dog that chews, and keep a small plant off the floor where a toddler can reach it. A cat that treats the leaves as a toy will chew them, get a burning mouth, and learn — but the lesson should not have to be learned at all. If a pet eats a large amount of any plant, call your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435. For a household with cats, dogs, and small children, that placement is the one safety rule in how to care for peace lily that matters more than any other — and it is decided by where the pot sits, not by anything you buy.

## The short version

How to care for peace lily, in one paragraph: buy a glossy, green plant in the right size — 'Wallisii' for a shelf, a standard plant for a table, 'Sensation' only for the floor; set it in bright indirect light near an east-facing window, with no direct sun; water thoroughly when the top inch feels dry, drain it fully, and empty the saucer — roughly weekly in summer, every two weeks or longer in a cool winter room; keep it at 68 to 85°F by day, a few degrees cooler at night, never below 60°F, away from drafts and vents; feed at one-quarter strength every six to eight weeks in spring and summer, nothing in autumn and winter; repot every one to two years in February or March, one size up; divide the clump in spring for more plants; a divided plant will not flower in its first year; trim each faded spathe at the base; and keep the plant out of reach of cats, dogs, and small children, because it is toxic if chewed. Do that, and the 15-inch plant you buy this November will be a 2- to 3-foot floor plant in a few years, flowering every year — and a plant you can divide and share. ### And a second plant for the same windowsill

If you want a second houseplant for the same windowsill, the [poinsettia](/blog/how-to-care-for-poinsettia/) blooms in the same cool room, the [Christmas cactus](/blog/how-to-care-for-christmas-cactus/) flowers on the same short days with none of the watering worry, the [cyclamen](/blog/how-to-care-for-cyclamen/) keeps blooming for up to three months in the same bright, cool spot, and the [amaryllis](/blog/how-to-care-for-amaryllis/) flowers on a tall stem from a bulb — though it is toxic too, so keep it out of reach as well.

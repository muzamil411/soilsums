If you are looking up how to winterize a garden, you are probably standing in front of beds that look finished for the year and wondering how much of the fall work actually matters. It matters more than the spring work: University of Minnesota Extension says a few hours of fall cleanup and maintenance can protect plants, improve soil health, reduce pest and disease problems, and set the stage for next year's growing season. This guide to how to winterize a garden follows the order extension sources recommend — what to clear, what to feed, what to cover, and the one timing mistake that undoes the rest.

## When to winterize your garden

There is no national start date for how to winterize a garden. The calendar is set by your first hard freeze, and that moves by weeks between Minnesota and Georgia. The work below falls into two phases: soil work — clearing, amending, cover crops — happens while the ground is still workable, and insulation work, like winter mulch around tender plants, happens after the soil has cooled. Once the ground freezes solid, you can no longer amend it, plant cover crops, or lift tender bulbs, so everything in the first group has a real deadline.

Raised beds go first in the queue. UMN Extension notes their soil experiences greater temperature fluctuations than in-ground gardens, which means they freeze harder and earlier. If you grow in raised beds, start there — our guide to filling them correctly is worth a re-read before you top them up for winter (/blog/what-to-fill-raised-garden-beds-with/).

## Clear out diseased plants first

The first step in how to winterize a garden is subtraction, not addition. Before anything gets added, take things away. UMN Extension's fall guidance starts here: remove and dispose of diseased plant material — blight-infected tomatoes, vegetables hit by powdery mildew — so diseases don't survive the winter and return next season. Bag it or trash it; do not compost it. UMN's instruction is to compost healthy plant debris and dispose of the diseased material, and the distinction matters, because a typical home compost pile never gets hot enough to kill pathogens. (Our [compost troubleshooting guide](/blog/why-is-my-compost-not-breaking-down/) shows what a genuinely active pile looks like.)

For tomatoes specifically, UMN's Rice County coordinator Lorrie Rugg advises cutting the plants off at ground level and letting the root systems decompose naturally. Ripping roots out disturbs the soil food web for no benefit — the tops are the problem, not the roots.

## Harvest before the hard freeze

While you are clearing, harvest what is left. UMN Extension draws the line clearly: root crops such as carrots, beets, and parsnips tolerate light frosts, but warm-season crops like tomatoes, peppers, and beans are easily damaged by freezing temperatures. Pick the tender crops before the hard freeze arrives; the roots can stay in the cold ground longer and often sweeten there.

This is also the moment to save seed — but only from healthy plants. UMN's fall guidance warns that many diseases travel on seed, so saving seed from a diseased plant just plants next year's problem.

## Decide what to do with crop residue

Now the fork in the road that most how to winterize a garden guides gloss over. UMN Extension lays out three options for spent crop residue, each with honest trade-offs:

- **Work it into the soil.** Tilling returns nutrients and organic matter, and chopping the residue makes it available to soil microbes quickly — which also helps destroy plant diseases. The cost: tillage harms soil structure over the long term, and if you till without planting a cover crop behind it, the soil sits bare all winter and erodes.
- **Leave it on the surface, chopped.** The same nutrients return to the soil without tillage damage. Chop the residue with shears, pruners, or a mower so it breaks down faster. The downsides: it decomposes more slowly than tilled-in residue, and hand-chopping is real work.
- **Remove it and compost it.** This gives the cleanest planting surface for fall greens or cover crops, and removing residue keeps diseases on that residue from overwintering in the soil. The cost is losing the residue's nutrients and organic matter — unless the finished compost goes back on the bed.

Pick the option that fits your spring plans, but pick one deliberately. The fourth option — tilling everything under and walking away — is the one UMN warns against: bare soil left through winter is vulnerable to erosion.

## Feed the soil, then cover it

With beds cleared, feed them — of every step in how to winterize a garden, this one pays back the most visibly in spring. UMN Extension's fall-care guidance (published October 2026): after removing spent crops, add compost and other organic materials such as shredded leaves or well-rotted manure to replenish nutrients and improve soil health — then cover the beds with a 2- to 4-inch layer of mulch to reduce erosion, suppress weeds, and add organic matter.

One honest caveat, from within the same university: UMN's Rice County office advises holding compost until spring in regions with heavy snowmelt, because nutrients can wash away with the meltwater. In the snow belt, shredded leaves or straw make the better fall cover and the compost waits for spring; where winters are drier, fall composting follows UMN's main guidance. Either way, the non-negotiable is coverage — as Rugg puts it, "add leaves or straw to cover the ground" and don't leave it bare. Fallen leaves are free mulch and free compost feedstock; our leaf guide shows how to shred and use them (/blog/how-to-compost-leaves/).

## Plant a cover crop if your timing still works

If your ground is still open and the first hard freeze is weeks away, a cover crop is the highest-value step in how to winterize a garden. UMN Extension recommends a mix of oats and peas for backyard gardeners: planted in early fall, they grow through the season, die over winter, and break down naturally — leaving a seedbed in spring with no termination work. Cover crops prevent erosion, hold nutrients, and add organic matter.

The honest limitation: UMN's seeding windows are Minnesota-specific, and by early November the window has closed in the northern zones. A cover crop sown too late won't establish, and that is wasted seed and wasted effort. In that case, mulch is the cover — a 2- to 4-inch layer does the soil-protection job for the winter.

## Time your winter mulch after the soil cools

This is the step most gardeners get backwards, and the one that separates a real how to winterize a garden plan from a generic fall cleanup list. Purdue Extension's rule: mulch after several hard frosts, once the soil has cooled but before it freezes. Mulching too early — while the soil is still warm — can increase rodent activity and delay plant dormancy. The mulch goes on to lock the cold in, not to keep warmth in.

Apply 2 to 4 inches for most perennials and ornamentals, and up to 6 inches for tender perennials, bulbs, and garlic. (Fall is garlic planting season — our [garlic planting guide](/blog/when-to-plant-garlic/) covers the timing.) Keep mulch a few inches away from plant crowns and tree trunks — no volcano mulching — and if the soil is dry, irrigate deeply before mulching.

Good materials, per Purdue: shredded leaves, which break down fast and enrich the soil; clean straw, a lightweight insulation ideal for garlic, strawberries, and overwintered vegetables; or compost topped with wood chips to hold it in place. Avoid mulch made from diseased plants, which can harbor and spread pathogens, and fresh grass clippings, which mat down and hold excess moisture. Purdue also flags black walnut mulch: it contains juglone, a compound toxic to plants including peonies, hydrangeas, and viburnum.

## Leave the right things standing

Not every bed needs a buzz cut. UMN Extension advises leaving healthy stems standing until spring: hollow perennial stems offer shelter for native bees and other beneficial insects, while seed heads from coneflowers, black-eyed Susans, and ornamental grasses provide winter interest and food for birds. Rugg's version of the same advice: leave hollow-stem plants in the garden so bees and insects have a place to rest for the winter.

Cut back only what is mushy or diseased — hostas after frost, anything with mildew — and leave the rest for wildlife. You will cut it back in spring anyway, and the garden will be livelier for the patience.

## Test your soil while the labs are quiet

Fall is an ideal time to test garden soil, according to UMN Extension — and a soil test is the cheapest step in how to winterize a garden. There is a practical reason beyond the agronomy. Soil labs are less backlogged than in the spring rush, and the results give you months to apply amendments like lime, sulfur, phosphorus, or potassium before spring planting. Testing first means you buy only what the soil actually needs, which UMN notes saves money and helps protect water quality.

If the test comes back acidic, our lime calculator works out the application rate (/tools/lime-calculator/). If it comes back heavy clay, fall is a fine time to start the long fix (/blog/how-to-amend-clay-soil/). ## Dig the tender bulbs; plant the hardy ones

Two bulb jobs, in opposite directions — and the most time-sensitive part of how to winterize a garden. Dig tender bulbs — cannas, callas, gladioli, dahlias, amaryllis — before cold weather arrives. Brush or wash off the extra soil and let them cure for three to five days before putting them into storage, per UMN's Rice County guidance. In the other direction, fall is planting time for hardy bulbs: tulips, daffodils, and crocus go in the ground now, and you will thank yourself in spring.

## Clean and sanitize your tools

End the season at the workbench. Plant pathogens — bacteria, fungi, viruses — survive on tools and move from diseased plants to healthy ones on a dirty blade, which is why UMN's Rice County office says to clean and sanitize everything before storage. The recipe is simple: a 10% bleach solution, one part bleach to nine parts water. Once tools are clean and dry, coat them with linseed, tung, or mineral oil to prevent rust — and wash your garden gloves too, since they harbor diseases just like tools.

## Drain hoses and shut off outdoor spigots

Water is the quiet destroyer in any how to winterize a garden checklist. Illinois Extension's guidance is blunt: empty irrigation lines before freezing, because water expands as it freezes and the expansion cracks rigid plastic fittings. The author learned this the expensive way — after leaving water in his drip lines one year, fountains erupted all over the garden the following spring.

Remove and drain all hoses from the spigots around your home and store the empty hoses somewhere sheltered. A hose left attached traps water in the line, and as ice forms it can progress back into the spigot and into the house. For the spigots themselves: shut off the interior valve to each exterior spigot, then open the bleeder valve to empty the waterline. Frost-proof spigots reduce the risk but do not eliminate it — it depends on how cold it gets and how well insulated the wall cavities are.

## How to winterize a garden: three mistakes that undo the work

First, mulching too early. Warm-soil mulching delays dormancy and invites rodents — Purdue's timing exists for a reason. Second, composting diseased plants. UMN says compost the healthy debris and dispose of the diseased material; your home pile is not a sterilizer. Third, leaving soil bare all winter. Whether you till, chop-and-drop, or compost the residue, the ground needs a cover: a cover crop, mulch, or shredded leaves. Bare soil erodes, and spring-you will pay for it.

That is how to winterize a garden the extension-backed way: clear the diseased material, feed the soil, cover it before winter, and put the water system to bed. Do it now, while the ground is still workable, and spring starts a month ahead.

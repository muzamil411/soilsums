The first hard frost is the deadline for rosemary, and the question of how to overwinter rosemary is really two questions wearing one name. The first is whether your plant can stay outside at all, which depends on your zone, your variety, and — more than most gardeners expect — whether your soil drains in winter. The second is how to keep a potted plant alive indoors for four months, which depends on light and water far more than on temperature. Get the first question wrong and the plant freezes — and that is why how to overwinter rosemary starts with the zone, not the pot. Get the second wrong and it dies slowly in a warm living room, brown and crisp by January, killed by the exact care that would save a houseplant. This guide to how to overwinter rosemary follows the extension services that publish the method: the zone limits and where they disagree, the varieties that buy you a zone, the outdoor protection that actually works, the date to bring pots in, and the light, water, and air routine that keeps a plant alive indoors until spring.

## Can rosemary survive winter outdoors?

It depends on your zone, and the extension services do not all give the same number — which is the first honest thing to say about how to overwinter rosemary. Rosemary is a tender perennial evergreen shrub, native to the dry, rocky hillsides of the Mediterranean, and Penn State Extension lists it for USDA zones 8 to 10. Nebraska Extension says plants are winter hardy in zones 7 to 10. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension is the most cautious of the three: it says rosemary is winter hardy only to zone 8a, and that in Wisconsin it is generally only grown as a potted plant. Illinois Extension splits the difference, calling rosemary typically hardy to zone 7 in the United States.

That spread is not a mistake to be resolved by picking the friendliest number. It is four services writing from four climates, and the range itself is the information: rosemary is reliably perennial where winters stay mild, borderline where they do not, and a container plant everywhere else. Use the range, not a single number, and plan from the cold end of it. A plant rated for zone 8 that meets a zone 7 winter with wet soil and a north wind is a dead plant, and no mulch depth fixes a zone error of two full zones.

### The rule by zone

| Zone | What to do | Why, per the sources |
| ---- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| Zones 8–10 | Leave in the ground with good drainage | Penn State Extension lists zones 8 to 10; Nebraska Extension lists zones 7 to 10; UW-Madison lists zone 8a as the limit |
| Zone 7 | Borderline: hardy varieties only, sheltered site, mulch and wind protection | Illinois Extension calls rosemary typically hardy to zone 7; Penn State rates 'Arp', 'Athens Blue Spire', 'Madalene Hill', and 'Miss Jessop's Upright' to zone 7 or zone 6b with protection |
| Zone 6 | Pot it and bring it indoors, unless it is 'Arp' or 'Madalene Hill' in a protected spot | Nebraska Extension calls 'Arp' one of the most winter hardy types, to zone 6; Illinois Extension rates 'Arp' and 'Hills Hardy' to zone 6 |
| Zone 5 and colder | Grow as an annual, or in a pot brought indoors before frost | Nebraska Extension, writing from zones 4b, 5a, and 5b, treats rosemary as a tender perennial grown as an annual or a houseplant; Illinois Extension says central Illinois, mostly zone 5, cannot overwinter rosemary without help |

That table is the first filter for how to overwinter rosemary: it tells you whether the job is protection at all, or whether the plant belongs in a pot that comes inside. The zone is not the whole story, though — it is the starting point, and two other factors decide whether a zone 7 plant actually survives a zone 7 winter.

### Cold is not what usually kills it

The second filter is water, and every extension source that writes about rosemary says the same thing about it: wet soil kills more rosemary in winter than cold does. Nebraska Extension names it directly — the biggest threat to rosemary's winter survival is wet, poorly drained winter soil, leading to root rot. Penn State Extension says rosemary is sensitive to wet, poorly drained soils, and that overwatering causes root rot, which can be deadly outdoors and especially in pots overwintered indoors. UW-Madison says to keep the soil moist but that good drainage is a requirement. Illinois Extension says rosemary requires a well-drained site to thrive, and recommends a raised bed or container where the soil stays wet.

So a zone 8 plant in heavy clay that holds winter rain is in more danger than a zone 7 plant in sandy, gravelly soil on a slope. That is why the drainage advice matters as much as the zone number in how to overwinter rosemary: the plant evolved on Mediterranean hillsides where water runs off, and a winter of standing water around its roots rots them before the cold ever gets a chance. That is the drainage half of how to overwinter rosemary, and it matters as much as the zone. If your soil is heavy clay, the honest move is a raised bed or a container — not more mulch, and not a hardier variety.

### The temperature floor

The third filter is the actual low temperature, and here Illinois Extension gives the number that matters: single-digit temperatures are often fatal for rosemary. That is the temperature floor for how to overwinter rosemary outdoors, and it is the reason zone 7 is a gamble rather than a guarantee — a zone 7 winter can stay above single digits for years and then drop to 5 degrees for one night in January. UW-Madison's zone 8a limit and Penn State's zones 8 to 10 are the same warning expressed as zones rather than degrees: an unprotected rosemary facing single-digit cold is living on luck, and luck is not a method. Snow cover helps — Illinois Extension notes that snow can actually insulate and protect plants from extreme lows, and that the dangerous combination is no snow cover plus extreme low temperatures. A plant under a foot of snow is warmer than a plant standing bare in a dry 5-degree wind.

## The varieties that buy you a zone

Variety is the second lever in how to overwinter rosemary, after the zone itself — and it is the one lever you can still pull in November if you are buying next year's plant with winter in mind. Most rosemary sold at garden centers is simply labeled "rosemary," and that unnamed plant is the zone 8 to 10 plant. The named cultivars below are the ones the extension services single out as hardier, and the differences between them are real but small: one zone, bought with the right site and protection, not a free pass to ignore the zone table.

'Arp' is the hardiest rosemary the extension services name, and it is the first choice for anyone gardening at the edge — the first name to learn when you are learning how to overwinter rosemary at the edge of its range. Nebraska Extension calls 'Arp' one of the most winter hardy types, to zone 6, and notes it originated with Madalene Hill from Arp, Texas, growing to 3 feet tall with light blue flowers. UW-Madison says 'Arp' is hardy to zone 7a, potentially to zone 6a with protection, and records that it was discovered in Arp, Texas, in 1972 by professional herb grower Madeline Hill. Illinois Extension lists 'Arp' among the few varieties noted to be hardy to zone 6. Penn State Extension includes 'Arp' in its hardier group, rated to zone 7 or zone 6b with protection.

### 'Madalene Hill' and the other hardy names

'Madalene Hill' — listed by UW-Madison as 'Madeline Hill', and sold as 'Hill Hardy' — is the second name to know. Penn State Extension includes it in the hardier group to zone 7 or zone 6b with protection. UW-Madison says it has improved hardiness and can survive in zone 7a, or zone 6a in protected sites, growing 3 feet tall and 1½ to 2 feet wide. Illinois Extension pairs it with 'Arp' as hardy to zone 6. The two spellings, Madalene and Madeline, are the same cultivar in different extension publications — if the tag says 'Hill Hardy', it is this plant.

Penn State Extension names two more cultivars in the hardier group: 'Athens Blue Spire' and 'Miss Jessop's Upright', both rated to zone 7 or zone 6b with protection. UW-Madison's cultivar list adds the caution that none of them can survive a Wisconsin winter, which is the honest limit of the whole group: a hardy cultivar buys you one zone, sometimes two with a sheltered site and mulch, and no cultivar buys you a zone 5 winter in the ground. If you are in zone 6 and the plant matters to you, grow 'Arp' in a pot anyway — the cultivar is insurance, not a guarantee, and the pot is the guarantee. The lesson of the whole group is the same one that runs through how to overwinter rosemary in zone 6: buy the hardy variety for the temperature, and keep the pot for the plant you cannot lose.

### What the hardy varieties do not change

The hardy varieties change the temperature the plant can take, not the soil it needs or the water it hates. 'Arp' in wet clay dies the same way an unnamed rosemary dies in wet clay — Nebraska Extension's warning about wet, poorly drained winter soil applies to every cultivar, and Penn State Extension's pH 6.5 to 7.0 and light, well-drained, sandy or gravelly soil apply to the whole species. A hardy variety in the wrong soil is a hardy plant rotting at the roots, and the zone it survives on paper does not save it. Buy the variety for the temperature, and fix the drainage for the plant — that is the order that matters in how to overwinter rosemary, because the variety only helps if the roots are alive to feel the cold.

## How to overwinter rosemary outdoors

For gardeners in zone 8 and warmer, and for zone 7 gardeners with a hardy variety, the outdoor method is four moves — site, drainage, mulch, and wind protection — and each one has a number or a rule the sources state plainly. Together they are how to overwinter rosemary outdoors, and skipping one does not always kill the plant, but the winter losses almost always trace back to one of them.

### Pick the site first

Site is the move you make once and live with all winter, so it comes first. Nebraska Extension says to locate the plant close to the house on the south or west side, where it benefits from retained and reflected winter warmth from the foundation. Illinois Extension says the same thing in plainer terms: for overwintering outdoors, select a site protected from weather extremes, like a spot along a sunny, south-facing wall of your home. The wall does two jobs at once — it blocks the wind and it gives back heat at night — and a plant against a south-facing wall is living in a warmer microclimate than the same plant ten feet out in the open bed. Full sun is the other half of the site: Illinois Extension requires more than 6 hours of direct sunlight a day, and Penn State Extension lists full sun with light, well-drained soil. A shaded, north-facing corner is the worst site in how to overwinter rosemary outdoors, and no amount of mulch makes up for it.

### Fix the drainage before the mulch

Drainage is the move that decides whether the mulch helps or hurts, so it comes before the mulch. If the soil holds water, fix that first — a raised bed, a container, or a spot on a slope where water runs off — because mulch over wet soil keeps the soil wet longer, which is the opposite of what rosemary needs. Nebraska Extension's rule is the one to remember: make sure the chosen garden site has soil that continues to be well-drained in winter, not just in summer, when the ground is frozen and the rain has nowhere to go. Penn State Extension prefers pea gravel or sandy mulch over organic mulch for exactly this reason: organic mulch retains moisture, and rosemary does poorly in wet or poorly drained clay soil. That is the drainage rule inside how to overwinter rosemary outdoors: fix the water first, and the mulch second. If you are weighing the method against the other winter jobs in the garden, our [winterizing guide](/blog/how-to-winterize-a-garden/) covers the rest of the November work, including the beds a rosemary plant is coming out of — or staying in.

### Mulch the roots, 5 to 6 inches

With the site and drainage settled, mulch is the insulation. Nebraska Extension gives the number: in fall, mulch the roots with 5 to 6 inches of wood chips to provide additional protection. Illinois Extension says to add a layer of heavy mulch upon the first frost, and to add a temporary extra layer of insulating mulch when there is no snow cover and temperatures reach extreme lows. Penn State Extension's preference is pea gravel or sandy mulch rather than organic mulch, because organic mulch retains moisture — so if your winters are wet, gravel or sand over the root zone insulates without holding water against the crown, and if your winters are dry and cold, wood chips are the Nebraska figure to use. Either way, mulch the roots, not the stem: the point is to hold the soil temperature steady around the root zone, and the roots are more cold-hardy than the top growth, per Nebraska Extension — but they still need protection.

### Screen it from the wind

Wind is the last outdoor move, and it is the one most gardeners skip. Nebraska Extension says to protect the branches and foliage from desiccation by strong winter winds, using rose cones, burlap wraps, chicken wire cylinders filled with leaves or straw, or commercial products like the Pop-up Plant Protector or Shrub Jacket. The timing matters: protection should be installed after the first hard frost and not removed in spring until the danger of frost is past. Installing it early traps warmth and moisture around a plant that is still growing; removing it early exposes new growth to a late frost. Wind dries the needles out faster than the frozen roots can replace the water, which is why a plant can look freeze-killed when it actually died of winter drought — the same desiccation that kills unprotected broadleaf evergreens, working on a Mediterranean shrub that never evolved for a dry, freezing wind. That is why wind protection belongs in any plan for how to overwinter rosemary outdoors.

## When to bring rosemary indoors

For everyone in zone 6 and colder — and for zone 7 gardeners who do not want to gamble a plant they care about — the indoor route is the reliable one, and the date is the first decision in how to overwinter rosemary indoors. Penn State Extension says to take pots indoors 2 to 3 weeks before the first frost date in the fall, where the plant is not hardy. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension gives the same window: dig the plant 2 to 3 weeks before the expected first fall frost, repot it, and bring it inside. That two-to-three-week buffer is not caution for its own sake — it is the adjustment period. A plant moved from cool autumn nights straight into a warm, dry house drops leaves and stalls, while a plant moved while the days are still mild settles into its winter spot before the light drops and the furnace comes on.

### The two routes inside

UW-Madison offers two ways to get the plant indoors, and the choice depends on how it spent the summer. The first route is for plants already in pots: keep the plant in its pot and sink the pot to its rim in the garden for the summer, so nothing is disturbed when you lift it in fall — the roots never leave the pot, and the move indoors is just a lift and a carry. The second route is for plants grown in the ground: transplant them out for the summer, then dig 2 to 3 weeks before the expected first fall frost, repot, and bring them inside. The sunk-pot route is the easier of the two and the one to plan for next year, when you are setting up how to overwinter rosemary indoors for the following winter: a rosemary grown in a container from the start is a rosemary that moves indoors without a dig, without root damage, and without the transplant shock that kills as many plants as the frost would have.

Do not wait for the frost warning itself. The first frost date is an average, not an appointment, and a plant that has already taken a hard frost has already taken damage — browning tips and blackened needles do not heal indoors. Find your average first frost date with our [Planting Date Calculator](/tools/planting-date-calculator/), count back two to three weeks, and put that date on the calendar now, while the plant is still healthy. That date is the real deadline for how to overwinter rosemary indoors, and it arrives before the frost does.

## How to move a rosemary pot indoors, step by step

The move itself is five steps, and each one has a reason the extension services state plainly. Together they are how to overwinter rosemary indoors in practice — and the steps that look optional are the ones that decide whether the plant is alive in March.

### Pick the date

Aim for 2 to 3 weeks before your average first fall frost, per Penn State Extension and UW-Madison. Mark it on the calendar and treat it as a deadline, not a suggestion — the plant adjusts better to the move while the days are still mild, and a plant brought in after a frost has already been hurt.

### Choose the pot

The pot matters because rosemary hates wet feet, and the pot is the drainage — the pot is the drainage in how to overwinter rosemary indoors, and it deserves the same attention as the soil. Penn State Extension notes that clay pots permit rapid drying, which is exactly what a plant prone to root rot needs indoors — a clay pot dries from all sides, while a plastic pot only dries from the top. Whatever the material, the pot needs a drainage hole, and it should fit the roots without much spare room: a pot far bigger than the root ball holds soil that stays wet long after the roots have had enough. If the plant spent the summer in the ground, dig it with as much of the root system intact as you can, and choose a pot big enough to contain the roots but not very much bigger. Set the pot on a saucer, but never let water stand in the saucer — standing water is the indoor version of the wet winter soil that Nebraska Extension warns about outdoors.

### Check for pests before it comes in

Inspect the plant before it joins your other houseplants. Penn State Extension lists the indoor pests to look for: thrips, aphids, whiteflies, mealybugs, and spider mites may be a problem indoors. UW-Madison names aphids, spider mites, mealybugs, and scales as the insects that attack rosemary, with root rot and botrytis as the most common disease problems. Check the undersides of the leaves and the joints where stems meet — spider mites in particular are easy to carry in unnoticed and hard to evict from a dry winter windowsill. A plant with pests goes in alone, away from the other pots, until it is clean — not onto the windowsill next to the rest of the collection.

### Pick the spot: the brightest, coolest place you have

A south-facing window in full sun, per Penn State Extension, away from heat registers and radiators — that is the spot, and it is the hardest one to find in most houses. Illinois Extension says the goal for an overwintering spot is cool temperatures around 50 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit and very bright sunlight, and calls a sunny basement window the ideal location, with a partially heated back porch that receives a lot of light as the next best thing. The combination is the problem: the brightest windows in most houses are in the warmest rooms, and the coolest rooms are the darkest. A sunny basement window, a cool back porch, or a south-facing window in a room you keep cool are the three spots that actually work — a warm living room windowsill is bright enough and too warm, and a cool dark garage is cool enough and too dark. Choose the spot by both measures together, not one of them — cool and bright is the combination that makes how to overwinter rosemary indoors work.

### Cut back the watering on day one

Water only when the mix is dry to the touch — that is the rule from the day the plant comes in, not from the day it starts looking thirsty. The plant's water needs drop the moment it comes indoors, because it is growing slowly in short days and cool air, and the watering habit from summer is the fastest way to rot the roots. The next two sections are the detail on that rule, because watering and light are where indoor rosemary actually dies.

## Light: the first indoor problem

Penn State Extension is blunt about the difficulty: overwintering rosemary indoors may be difficult due to low-light conditions and too little or too much water. Light is the first half of that sentence, and it is the harder half to fix — you can always water less, but you cannot make a north window face south. Rosemary is a full-sun Mediterranean shrub, and a winter windowsill is a pale substitute for a summer garden. The light rules below are the numbers the extension services publish, and they are the minimums, not the ideals.

### Six hours is the floor

Penn State Extension says most herbs need 6 hours of direct sunlight, and lists rosemary among the herbs that do best in direct sun — bay, rosemary, and thyme. Illinois Extension requires more than 6 hours of direct sunlight a day for rosemary in the garden, and the indoor requirement is no lower. Iowa State University Extension adds that some herbs will tolerate as little as six hours of direct light a day, but most want eight or more hours each day — and rosemary, as a sun-loving Mediterranean herb, sits on the eight-or-more side of that line. A south-facing window is the only window that reliably delivers it in winter; an east or west window gives a few hours of direct sun at best, and a north window gives none. If the plant starts stretching, with long pale stems and sparse needles, it is telling you the light is not enough — lanky growth means too little light, per Iowa State Extension, and the fix is more light, not more fertilizer. That stretch test is the first test of whether how to overwinter rosemary indoors will work in your house.

### When the window is not enough, add a light

If no window gives six hours of direct sun, a grow light is the answer, and the extension services give the setup in numbers. Iowa State University Extension says to look for full-spectrum grow lights in high-output fixtures, place the lights within 6 to 12 inches of the leaves for higher light intensities, and plug the light into a timer set to be on for 12 to 16 hours a day for ideal growth. Penn State Extension gives the same shape of figure for fluorescent light: place herbs 6 to 12 inches from two 40-watt cool white fluorescent bulbs for 14 to 16 hours. The distance matters as much as the hours — a light three feet above the plant is decoration, not horticulture — and the timer matters as much as the distance, because rosemary needs a dark period too. Twelve to sixteen hours on, the rest off, every day, is the routine that keeps a plant growing slowly through the winter instead of stretching toward a dim window and dying back from the base — the light half of how to overwinter rosemary indoors.

## Watering indoor rosemary: the dry side

Water is where indoor rosemary dies, and the extension services agree on that more clearly than on anything else in how to overwinter rosemary indoors. The University of Wisconsin-Madison Extension says the biggest cause of plant decline and death is overwatering. Penn State Extension says overwatering causes root rot, which can be deadly outdoors and especially in pots overwintered indoors — and that browning of the tips is a sign of root rot, not of dryness. That last point matters, because the instinct when a rosemary's tips brown is to water it, and watering a rotting plant is what finishes it.

### The dry-side rule

The watering rule for how to overwinter rosemary indoors is to keep the plant on the dry side, and to water by feel rather than by the calendar. Nebraska Extension says to water as needed to keep plants evenly moist to slightly dry. Penn State Extension's indoor-herb guidance says rosemary should dry out slightly between waterings. Iowa State University Extension says to water when the potting mix is dry to the touch and before the plant wilts — and to empty the saucer after watering, so the pot never sits in water. Three services, one rule: let the top of the mix dry, water thoroughly, let it drain, and empty the saucer. A plant that is watered a little every day stays wet at the roots and rots; a plant that is watered deeply when the mix is dry stays alive.

### How often, in practice

There is no number of days, because the days depend on the pot, the room, and the plant — a clay pot in a cool bright room dries faster than a plastic pot in a cool dark one, and a big plant drinks faster than a small one. Check the mix with a finger, not with a calendar: push a finger an inch into the mix, and water only if it feels dry at that depth. In a cool room at 50 to 60 degrees, the mix dries slowly, so watering is infrequent; in a warmer room, it dries faster — but the test is always the mix, never the day of the week. If the plant wilts, water it — Iowa State Extension's rule is to water before the plant wilts, not after — but do not water a wilting plant and then water it again the next day out of guilt. One deep watering, drained and emptied, and then wait for the mix to dry again. That rhythm is the whole of watering in how to overwinter rosemary indoors: deep, drained, and dry before the next drink. And do not feed it: UW-Madison says to fertilize lightly, because excess fertilizer reduces flowering and fragrance, and Penn State Extension's low-dose feeding every two weeks is for herbs in active growth, not for a rosemary resting through the winter. A plant that is barely growing does not need feeding, and fertilizer in a pot that stays wet only adds salts to a root zone that is already struggling.

## Air, humidity, and temperature indoors

Light and water get the attention, but air and temperature decide whether the plant that survives them stays healthy. Rosemary indoors faces two air problems at once: still, damp air that breeds mildew, and warm, dry furnace air that dries the needles out. Both are fixable, and both are cheaper to prevent than to cure.

### Cool beats warm

Temperature is the first of the two, and the extension figures all point the same way: cool. Illinois Extension gives the target as around 50 to 60 degrees Fahrenheit with very bright sunlight. Nebraska Extension says normal indoor temperatures are fine during winter, but rosemary would be happier in cooler conditions, 50 to 55 degrees, if that is available. Penn State Extension's indoor-herb guidance gives 65 to 70 degrees during the day and 55 to 60 at night for herbs generally — warmer than the Illinois and Nebraska rosemary figures, because it covers the whole herb shelf rather than rosemary alone. The shared point is that a living room in the low 70s is warmer than rosemary wants in winter: warm air pushes the plant to grow while the light is not there to support it, and the growth it pushes is weak, pale, and prone to mildew. A cool room — a basement window, a back porch, a room you keep at 55 to 60 degrees — is the goal, and it is worth rearranging the house for. If the only bright window is in a warm room, the grow light in a cool room beats the warm windowsill, because light can be added and heat is hard to remove — that is the temperature trade-off inside how to overwinter rosemary indoors.

### Air movement prevents mildew

Penn State Extension says rosemary is prone to developing powdery mildew, and to ensure adequate airflow between plants to prevent it. UW-Madison says good air circulation is important to prevent foliar disease, with root rot and botrytis the most common problems. The fix is simple: do not crowd the rosemary against other plants, do not push it hard against the glass where condensation collects, and give it a little moving air — a small fan on low in the room, or simply a spot that is not a still corner. Dry heated air is the other problem: Illinois Extension notes that most homes are a little too warm and too dry for rosemary, and the needles dry out in furnace air even when the roots are damp. A pebble tray under the pot or a humidifier in the room helps, but the temperature matters more than the humidity — a cool plant in dry air survives, while a warm plant in dry air crisps from the tips inward. If the tips brown, check the roots and the watering first, per Penn State Extension: browning tips are a sign of root rot, not a sign to mist more. That check — roots first, misting second — is the last habit to learn in how to overwinter rosemary indoors.

## The numbers: one rosemary plant, three winters

The figures above give a concrete answer if you run them through one plant, and the comparison is the practical payoff of learning how to overwinter rosemary by the numbers rather than by habit. Take one healthy 'Arp' rosemary, grown outdoors through the summer, and give it three different winters:

### The three winters

- **Left in the ground in zone 8, against a south-facing wall, in sandy, well-drained soil, with 5 to 6 inches of wood chips over the roots and a burlap wind screen put up after the first hard frost:** the plant is inside its hardiness range, the wall gives back heat at night, the mulch holds the soil temperature steady, and the screen stops the wind from drying the needles. That is the Penn State, Nebraska, and Illinois rules all followed at once — and the plant comes through spring with green needles and new growth. It is the control case: how to overwinter rosemary outdoors, done by the book.
- **The same plant in a pot, brought indoors 2 to 3 weeks before the first frost, set in a south-facing window in a cool room at 50 to 60 degrees, watered only when the mix is dry to the touch, with a grow light on a timer for 12 to 16 hours a day when the window falls short:** the plant grows slowly, keeps its needles, and goes back outside after the frost danger has passed. That is the Penn State, UW-Madison, Illinois, Nebraska, and Iowa State indoor rules all followed at once — and the same plant that would have frozen in the ground in zone 5 is alive in March. It is the indoor control case for how to overwinter rosemary, and it is the only reliable method in zone 6 and colder.

### The winter that fails

- **The same plant left in the ground through a zone 5 winter, or brought into a warm living room at 70 degrees and watered on a fixed schedule whether the mix is dry or not:** in the ground, single-digit temperatures kill it, per Illinois Extension — and the wet, poorly drained winter soil rots the roots first, per Nebraska Extension. In the warm living room, the heat pushes weak growth the dim light cannot support, the daily watering rots the roots, and the tips brown — which Penn State Extension says is a sign of root rot, not a sign to water more. Either way the plant is dead by February, and the cause is not the winter: it is the method. That is the cost of skipping how to overwinter rosemary the extension-backed way.

### The indoor numbers, side by side

And the indoor numbers for how to overwinter rosemary, since the sources give them as ranges rather than single figures: light is at least 6 hours of direct sun, and 8 or more is better, per Iowa State Extension — or 12 to 16 hours under a full-spectrum grow light 6 to 12 inches from the leaves. Temperature is 50 to 60 degrees, per Illinois Extension, with Nebraska Extension preferring 50 to 55 and Penn State Extension's general herb figure at 65 to 70 by day and 55 to 60 by night. Water is when the mix is dry to the touch, per Iowa State Extension — evenly moist to slightly dry, per Nebraska Extension, and dry out slightly between waterings, per Penn State Extension. The ranges overlap on the same point: bright, cool, and dry-side. A plant kept at any point inside those ranges survives; a plant kept outside all three of them — dim, warm, and wet — does not, and no single fix saves it.

## Taking cuttings: free insurance against a hard winter

If the plant matters to you, do not bet the whole winter on one pot: taking cuttings is the backup plan inside how to overwinter rosemary, and it costs nothing. Penn State Extension says rosemary is best propagated from stem cuttings, and that named cultivars do not stay true to type from seed — a cutting of 'Arp' is 'Arp', while a seedling of 'Arp' is a gamble. The method is specific: take a 6-inch section of stem tip, strip the lower leaves, dip the base in rooting hormone, and place it in a sterile, well-aerated soilless mix with bottom heat of 70 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit. Once the cuttings root — 10 to 20 days, per Penn State — transplant them out in full sun, two feet apart.

UW-Madison gives the same method with slightly different numbers, which is worth showing rather than hiding: take 4- to 6-inch tip cuttings from a flower-free shoot in late spring to early summer, remove the lower leaves, and place the cuttings in a rooting medium such as perlite until roots have formed, treating them with rooting hormone first to improve rooting speed. The two versions agree on the shape — tip cutting, lower leaves off, rooting hormone, a sterile medium — and differ only on length and season. Either version works, and a cutting taken before the mother plant comes indoors for winter is a young plant to carry through if the big one fails. That is the cheapest insurance in how to overwinter rosemary: a 6-inch tip in a small pot on the same windowsill, costing nothing, that becomes next year's plant if the winter takes the old one.

### Why not seed

Seed is the slow road, and the extension services say so plainly. Penn State Extension says low viability and slow germination rates make rosemary difficult to propagate from seed. UW-Madison is more specific: germination is very irregular and slow, even at an ideal 60 degrees Fahrenheit for 14 to 21 days, and it takes up to three years to produce plants large enough to provide a good harvest — and plants grown from seed will not have the same characteristics as vegetative cultivars. If you want more rosemary, take cuttings; if you want a surprise, sow seed. Our [seed-starting guide](/blog/how-to-start-seeds-indoors/) covers the indoor setup, but for rosemary the cutting is the method, and the seed is the experiment.

## Harvesting rosemary in winter

A rosemary plant that survives winter is also a rosemary plant you can cook with all winter — lightly. UW-Madison says to pick small amounts as needed, but do not remove more than 20 percent of the growth at a time, and to avoid cutting into woody parts of stems unless you are shaping the plant, because cutting into old wood hinders development. That 20 percent is the harvest limit for how to overwinter rosemary without weakening the plant you are trying to keep alive: a sprig here and there for cooking, not a haircut. The plant is growing slowly in short days and cool air, and every stem you take is growth it cannot quickly replace.

### Drying what you pick

For drying, the timing is before the plant flowers. Penn State Extension says to harvest leaves for drying prior to blossoming, and to hang the bundles upside down in an open area to dry. Nebraska Extension gives the same method with the storage detail: hang the plants or bunches upside down in a warm, well-ventilated location to dry, then strip the leaves from the stems and store them in tightly covered jars in a dark, cool location — and if moisture condenses inside the jars, more drying is needed. For freezing, Penn State Extension says to finely slice the leaves and put them in foil, a zip-top freezer bag, or an ice cube tray with water. Our [herb drying guide](/blog/how-to-dry-herbs/) covers the full drying method for the herb shelf, including the rosemary you pick before the plant comes in for winter — the harvest you take in fall is the rosemary you cook with in January, while the plant itself rests on the windowsill, the quiet reward for getting how to overwinter rosemary right.

## Spring: moving rosemary back outside

How to overwinter rosemary ends in spring, and the return has its own rules. Penn State Extension says to transfer pots outside in the spring after the danger of frost has passed. Nebraska Extension says the same thing for the outdoor protection: remove the wind screens and wraps only when the danger of frost is past. Do not rush it — a late frost after a warm spell does more damage than the whole winter, because the plant has started growing again and the new growth is tender. Wait for the frost danger to pass, not for the first warm weekend.

### The spring tidy-up

Once the plant is back outside, the spring jobs are light feeding and light pruning. UW-Madison says to fertilize lightly, because excess fertilizer reduces flowering and fragrance — and the same warning applies in spring as in winter: rosemary is a lean-soil Mediterranean shrub, and a heavy feed makes soft growth that the next frost or the next dry spell punishes. Penn State Extension says to prune after flowering, and UW-Madison says to avoid cutting into woody parts of stems unless you are shaping the plant. Cut back the dead tips to green wood, shape lightly, and leave the woody frame alone — rosemary does not reliably regrow from bare old wood, and a hard spring haircut is the fastest way to turn a plant that survived the winter into a plant that never recovers. Illinois Extension's reminder is the encouraging one: rosemary planted in spring grows into a sizeable plant over one growing season, so a plant that comes through winter small still has a full summer to become the shrub it was. That is the payoff of how to overwinter rosemary done well: the plant you carried through winter becomes the shrub of next summer.

## Mistakes that kill rosemary in winter

The short list of what goes wrong when people learn how to overwinter rosemary — all of it avoidable:

### Outdoor mistakes

- **Trusting the zone on the tag.** Most rosemary sold is the zone 8 to 10 plant, per Penn State Extension — check the variety, and assume zone 8 unless the tag names 'Arp' or 'Madalene Hill'.
- **Planting it in wet clay.** Wet, poorly drained winter soil is the biggest threat to winter survival, per Nebraska Extension — fix the drainage before the mulch.
- **Mulching with wet organic matter over soggy soil.** Pea gravel or sandy mulch is preferred over organic mulch, per Penn State Extension, because organic mulch retains moisture.
- **Leaving it exposed to winter wind.** Protect the branches from desiccation by strong winter winds, per Nebraska Extension — a wind screen is part of the method, not a decoration.
- **Counting on a mild winter in zone 7.** Single-digit temperatures are often fatal, per Illinois Extension — a hardy variety and a sheltered site are the plan, not optimism.

### Indoor mistakes

- **Bringing it in on the frost warning.** Pots go in 2 to 3 weeks before the first frost date, per Penn State Extension and UW-Madison — a plant that has already frozen has already been hurt.
- **Putting it in a warm living room.** The goal is cool temperatures around 50 to 60 degrees with very bright sunlight, per Illinois Extension — a warm room pushes weak growth the light cannot support.
- **Watering on a schedule.** Water when the mix is dry to the touch, per Iowa State Extension — and the biggest cause of plant decline and death indoors is overwatering, per UW-Madison.
- **Watering more when the tips brown.** Browning of the tips is a sign of root rot, per Penn State Extension — more water is what finishes a rotting plant.
- **Giving it a dim corner.** Most herbs need 6 hours of direct sunlight, per Penn State Extension, and most want eight or more, per Iowa State Extension — a north window is not a winter home for rosemary.
- **Feeding a resting plant.** Fertilize lightly, per UW-Madison, because excess fertilizer reduces flowering and fragrance — a plant barely growing in winter does not need feeding.
- **Skipping the pest check.** Thrips, aphids, whiteflies, mealybugs, and spider mites may be a problem indoors, per Penn State Extension — inspect before the plant joins the other pots.

Get the zone right, the drainage right, the pot in 2 to 3 weeks before frost, the room cool and bright at 50 to 60 degrees, and the watering dry-side — and that is how to overwinter rosemary the extension-backed way: a plant that comes in green in November goes back out green in spring, and the same plant cooks for you for years.

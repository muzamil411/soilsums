Lowering soil pH is harder, slower and less often necessary than raising it. Most gardeners who
think they need to do not, and most of the popular home remedies do nothing. Here is what
actually works, and when it is worth bothering.

## First, check whether you need to

Most vegetables are comfortable between pH 6.0 and 6.8, and many tolerate up to 7.5 without
complaint. A soil at pH 7.2 is not a problem for a vegetable bed, and treating it as one wastes
effort.

The cases where lowering pH genuinely matters:

- **Blueberries**, which want pH 4.5 to 5.5 and will be chlorotic and stunted above about 6.0.
- **Rhododendrons, azaleas and camellias**, similar.
- **Potatoes**, mildly — they prefer 5.0 to 6.5, largely because scab worsens as pH rises.
- **Genuinely alkaline soil**, above about pH 7.5, where iron and manganese become unavailable
  and leaves yellow between the veins.

If your soil is above 7.5 because it sits on chalk or limestone, be realistic: you are fighting
the parent material and it will win. Growing acid-lovers in containers or raised beds filled with
an appropriate mix is far more effective than trying to acidify the ground.

## Elemental sulfur, the one that works

Elemental sulfur is the standard amendment. Soil bacteria oxidise it into sulfuric acid, which
lowers pH.

Two consequences follow from that being a biological process:

**It is slow.** Several months to a year for the full effect. The bacteria need warm, moist,
active soil, so sulfur applied to cold ground in late autumn sits there until spring.

**It needs the soil to be alive.** In a sterile or very dry soil the conversion is slow to the
point of ineffective.

Rough quantities to lower pH by one unit, per 1,000 square feet, worked into the top six inches:

| Soil texture | Elemental sulfur |
| ------------ | ---------------- |
| Sandy        | about 10–15 lb   |
| Loam         | about 20–25 lb   |
| Clay         | about 30–40 lb   |

The same buffering logic applies as for lime, in reverse: clay resists the change and needs more.
These are starting figures and a soil test that gives a direct recommendation is worth more.

Apply no more than about a unit's worth in a year, water it in, and retest the following season
before adding more. Overshooting downwards is just as awkward as overshooting upwards, and the
correction is lime and another year.

## Aluminium sulfate, and why to be careful

Aluminium sulfate acidifies much faster, which is why it appears in blueberry advice. It works by
releasing aluminium ions, and aluminium is toxic to most plants at the concentrations involved.

It is defensible for a dedicated blueberry bed where you want a fast change. It is a poor choice
for a vegetable garden, where you are adding a plant toxin to soil you intend to grow food in for
years. Use sulfur unless you have a specific reason not to.

## Ammonium-based fertilizers

Ammonium sulfate and urea both acidify soil gradually as a side effect of nitrification. This is
not a fast or controllable way to change pH, but it is worth knowing about for two reasons: it
explains why long-fertilized beds drift acid over time, and it means that if you are feeding an
acid-loving plant anyway, an ammonium-based fertilizer works with you rather than against you.

## Organic matter

Adding plenty of compost and, for acid-lovers, materials such as pine bark or composted conifer
sawdust, has a modest acidifying effect and a large effect on everything else. It also increases
buffering capacity, which cuts both ways: the soil becomes more resistant to pH change in both
directions.

For blueberries in particular, a mulch of pine bark or sawdust, renewed annually, does more good
over time than a single sulfur application.

## What does not work

**Coffee grounds.** Mildly acidic when fresh, close to neutral once brewed, and present in
quantities far too small to shift soil pH. Excellent in a compost pile; useless as an acidifier.

**Pine needles.** The reputation is much larger than the effect. Fresh needles are acidic, but the
finished compost ends up near neutral and the change to soil pH under a needle mulch is small.

**Vinegar.** Lowers pH for hours, then soil microbes consume the acetic acid and it reverses.
Meanwhile it damages roots and soil life. Not a soil amendment.

**Citrus peel, tea leaves, sulphur powder from the garden centre labelled as a fungicide.** The
first two are compost ingredients. The third may not be the right material at the right particle
size — read the label and buy something sold as a soil acidifier.

## A better plan for acid-lovers

If you want blueberries and your soil is pH 7.0, the honest answer is usually a container or a
dedicated raised bed rather than an attempt to acidify the garden. A half barrel of ericaceous mix
will grow a better blueberry than three years of sulfur applications on limestone soil, and it
will not drift back.

## The short version

Confirm you actually need to lower pH — most vegetables do not care above 6.0. Use elemental
sulfur, roughly 10 to 40 pounds per 1,000 square feet per pH unit depending on texture, applied
in warm soil and worked in. Expect a season for it to act, retest before adding more, and ignore
coffee grounds and vinegar. For serious acid-lovers on alkaline ground, grow in containers
instead.
